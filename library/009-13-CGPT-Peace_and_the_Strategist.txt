Art of Peace and the Strategist


INTRODUCTION

Peace gives strength a purpose and judgment a measure. It asks what has been preserved, whose freedom remains, and what can continue when an undertaking is complete.

These two works concern the choices through which peace becomes a way of living. Art of Peace unfolds the grounds, capacities, and relations of that life. Art of the Strategist gathers these concerns around the sustained judgment of a person acting within changing conditions.

Read each principle with the others. Let purpose guide the movement, and let consequences correct the account.

The maps show the territory. The appendices distinguish its forms and relations. The glossary keeps their meanings within reach.

The work of judgment remains with the reader.


MAP OF THE WHOLE

These two works examine the choices through which peace becomes practicable and can continue. Purpose, perception, capacity, relationship, and consequence remain together in the account. The first work unfolds their distinctions across a way of living; the second gathers them within the sustained judgment of the Strategist.

PART I — ART OF PEACE

What should strength serve, which choices should conduct preserve, and what makes peace capable of continuing?

Art of Peace develops the motivating ground, capacities, relations, and limits of strategic conduct toward peace. Its eight books move from belonging and choice through perception, configuration, cultivation, and shared action to the transformation of conflict and the care of continuing life.

Book I, The Ground of Peace — Chapters 1–7: belonging, dignity, purpose, harmony, and the measure of success.

Book II, The Actor and the Act — Chapters 8–19: will, judgment, freedom, character, and alignment.

Book III, Seeing and Being Seen — Chapters 20–31: perspective, information, belief, surprise, visibility, and recognition.

Book IV, The Field in Motion — Chapters 32–55: position, geometry, power, configuration, momentum, and time.

Book V, Cultivation and Readiness — Chapters 56–74: capacities, preparation, support, renewal, and sufficient readiness.

Book VI, The Composition of Action — Chapters 75–105: principles, modes, movements, grammar, cooperation, and shared authorship.

Book VII, The Transformation of Conflict — Chapters 106–119: protection, Denial, Redirection, Restraint, Transmutation, Deterrence, and shared victory.

Book VIII, The Life of Peace — Chapters 120–138: responsibility, repair, love, care, enjoyment, transmission, completion, and release.

The twenty-seven principles close this work in their original order and wording. Each remains connected to the purposes, conditions, and limits developed through the others.

PART II — ART OF THE STRATEGIST

How does judgment remain responsive to purpose, limited means, other participants, and changing consequences throughout an undertaking?

The thirteen chapters return to unequal ground with the fuller account of what strength should serve. The Strategist preserves a center, observes, prepares, coordinates, learns, examines hidden causes, shares authorship, and transforms the undertaking. The questions concern the relations among these acts and the life left possible when the work can be laid down.

THE RETURN THROUGH THE WHOLE

Art of Peace reaches ordinary care, transmission, completion, and release. Art of the Strategist begins again within an encounter, gathering those concerns around the choices a person must make. Both works return to the care of what remains and the freedom to let a completed undertaking end.

THE REFERENCE MATERIAL

The appendices distinguish capacities, modes, movements, geometry, continuation, and grammar. Domains, scales, cultivation, opposites, cycles, and obstacles extend the field of examination. The glossary connects their terms and the principles that guide their use.

Grounding gives the inquiry its motivating perspective. Choice gives it a course. Consequences return the account to the life it affects.

PART I — ART OF PEACE

Choice connects its eight books: the Actor learns to perceive, judge, cultivate capacity, compose action, meet conflict, and care for what follows. The movement concludes with repair, love, transmission, completion, and the freedom to release a role whose work is done. The twenty-seven original principles follow the final chapter.

PART II — ART OF THE STRATEGIST


Art of the Strategist gathers the purposes and relations developed in Peace into the judgment of a person acting with limited resources in a changing field. Its thirteen chapters move through unequal conditions, attention, preparation, timing, shared action, learning, and transformation. Each inquiry considers how choices remain coherent across an undertaking. The final reckoning concerns what the work leaves possible: dependable protection, restored capacity, independent judgment, and a life free to continue beyond the contest.


I. The Unequal Field
Reckons unequal capacities, necessities, and support, showing how preparation and changed relationships can alter effective advantage while preserving limited strength.


II. The Center
Preserves a revisable center of judgment through competing demands, feeling, criticism, bodily needs, and habits, joining integrity with the capacity to return.


III. Seeing and Being Seen
Examines how position, interpretation, reporting, visibility, and concealment shape the field, coordinating disclosure and observation with cooperation, preparation, and changing responses.


IV. The Unseen Preparation
Traces an intended result through its prerequisites, joins preparation across dependencies, tests assumptions, preserves alternatives, and recognizes sufficient readiness for action.


V. The Uses of Time
Examines what intervals produce, coordinates different durations, tests urgency and delayed effects, and directs present expenditure toward what remains possible.


VI. One Action, Many Consequences
Follows one act through compatible benefits, competing consequences, responses, and continuing obligations, preserving clear execution while reconsidering the field it changes.


VII. Convergence and Discord
Brings distinct capacities and participants into workable accord through fitted connections, distributed judgment, useful tension, sustainable burdens, and leadership responsive to changing needs.


VIII. The Changing Ground
Investigates how access, dependence, provision, and participation shape advantage, preparing changes of ground and the transitions through which another course becomes practicable.


IX. Learning in Motion
Develops judgment through proportionate trials, comparisons, examined outcomes, and varied practice, carrying lessons into changed conditions while letting correction alter reliable conduct.


X. The Hidden Mechanism
Traces recurring results to distributed conduct, incentives, dependencies, and feedback, testing explanations and changing sustaining relations while preserving necessary functions.


XI. Authorship
Examines who defines purposes, roles, choices, and completion, joining meaningful authorship to responsibility, shared contribution, truthful records, and freedom beyond imposed roles.


XII. Transformation
Develops capacities and relationships through acquisition, release, practice, and revision, preserving support during change and allowing new possibilities to alter former ambitions.


XIII. What Remains
Reckons remaining strength, obligations, relationships, and maintenance, providing for restoration and independent transmission while releasing completed demands into a life beyond the contest.

Part I


Art of Peace

Peace is the end of War:
  Peace is the cessation of War.
  Peace is the goal of War.
The Art of War and the Art of Peace are:
  Two sides of one coin,
  Two paths to one end.

Let the end govern the means.
Let the means preserve what peace is meant to protect.

Peace preserves life,
Secures the freedom to act,
And gives those who differ a future they can share.

Its measures reach beyond the silence of weapons.
A frightened obedience may conceal an unfinished war.
A victory may leave the reasons for another.

Stop the attack.
Close the paths by which it can return.
Seek the relinquishment of the will-to-conflict.

Preserve the person through these changes.

The Strategist weighs every act by what it makes possible.
The Wise ask what deserves to become possible.

Let strength serve protection;
Let protection sustain freedom;
Let freedom find its fulfillment in a life of love, understanding, and shared good.

Prepare the conditions.
Choose the fitting act.
Carry peace into what follows.



BOOK I
THE GROUND OF PEACE



1. The Shared Whole

The Actor belongs to the life the act will change.

What sustains one person passes through others. What is injured here may diminish freedom elsewhere. An apparent victory can return through the world upon which the victor depends.

Grounding begins in the shared whole.

Self and other belong to one life. They encounter it from different places. Experience, power, and responsibility differ; the connection remains.

Hear those differences. Another person may see the cost hidden by your advantage. Allow their account to correct your own.

Include yourself in this care. Your safety, judgment, freedom, and strength are among the goods the act must preserve. To consume them without reckoning is to weaken part of the whole.

Include those absent. Some supply what the encounter spends. Some will inherit what it destroys. Some must inhabit the peace it leaves.

Grounding gives advantage its direction.
It gives restraint something to preserve.
It gives redirection a purpose beyond return of force.

Agreement may come later. Belonging precedes it. An adversary's denial of the connection does not dissolve it; nor does your recognition give you complete knowledge of it.

Stop the harmful undertaking where it must be stopped.
Keep the adversary's future within the purpose of the act.



2. Seeing, Accepting, and Loving

See what is present.
Accept what has occurred.
Love those whose lives the answer will touch.

Seeing includes your own hand in the difficulty. An account that omits it may conceal the nearest point of change.

Acceptance admits the ground upon which action must begin. What happened has consequences. What continues has support. What you have done may require an answer.

Receive the fact; discover the duty.

Love gives another's good a place in the decision. Affection may be absent. Reciprocity may never arrive. Care can still govern the means.

Let these three correct one another.

Care can make an unwelcome truth bearable.
Truth can reveal harm within a familiar kindness.
Acceptance can release the strength spent denying what now needs repair.

Where injury continues, clear acceptance brings it fully into view. Love then requires protection, refusal, or change.

Those threatened, those threatening, and those who protect remain within care. Their needs differ. Their responsibilities differ. Give each its weight.

The Companion asks what an offering has failed to understand. The Wise change its form when clearer sight requires it.

Choose truthful concern. Give it useful means. Answer for what your conduct brings.

The other person's response remains theirs.



3. The Dignity of Each

Oppose the deed with care for the doer.

A person remains more than an error, a weakness, a crime, or a defeat. These may change what can safely be permitted. They do not exhaust the life from which another act may arise.

Distinguish the person,
The undertaking,
And the means that give it force.

Close the path of injury. Preserve a path toward different conduct.

Bring your own vulnerability into the judgment. What would such exposure do to your freedom, your standing, your ability to meet another afterward? Let the Golden Rule reach the means as well as the aim.

Never make public humiliation an intended means or end.
No advantage releases this boundary.

Let conduct be answerable without making the person a spectacle.

Respect requires accurate sight. Imagined harmlessness can abandon the threatened. An image of permanent malice can conceal a real change. Judge the present closely; leave the future open.

Include your own dignity and the safety of those beyond the immediate encounter. Danger transferred to a third person has not disappeared.

The greater the power to impose, the greater the duty to understand what another can refuse, survive, or correct. A livable boundary protects agency. A burden that cannot be refused may wear the appearance of agreement.

Keep dignity present where restraint is hardest.



4. The Aim and the Boundary

Before asking how to succeed, determine what success may cost.

Desire supplies an impulse.
A role supplies an expectation.
A promise supplies a commitment.
None should govern the whole act unexamined.

Ask what the aim serves, whose good it includes, and what its pursuit requires. The strength to achieve it increases the consequence of this judgment.

Values direct the aim and govern the means together.

A boundary changes the strategic problem. Refuse an injurious means, and another sequence, another alliance of effects, another use of the field must be found.

The constraint becomes part of the art.

Distinguish four kinds of limit:
What the available means cannot perform;
What commitment forbids;
What preference resists;
What an unexamined assumption declares impossible.

Cultivate capacity where it can grow. Respect the limits that remain. Question the assumption. Weigh the preference. Keep the governing commitment within the reckoning of success.

Economy cannot justify spending what is not yours to spend.

When consequences expose a failed method, change the method. When they expose an unworthy aim, let correction reach the aim. A commitment may deepen through revision of its former meaning.

A boundary also imposes work. To refuse one means may require preparing another. Protection, repair, and an owed answer remain duties even when the easiest course is closed.



5. Harmony Among Differences

Harmony gives difference a place to contribute.

One voice answers; another sustains; another challenges; another completes. Some need only room to continue beside one another.

Find what can honestly be shared: a concern, a task, a dependence, a future.

Let each participant correct the account of it. A purpose becomes common through their participation in its formation. The strongest voice alone cannot make it so.

Difference may permit cooperation, competition, complementation, or coexistence. It may also disclose an incompatibility that must be faced.

First examine the arrangement.

Change the object, and the demands may cease colliding.
Change the timing, and both may receive their hour.
Move responsibility toward the means of carrying it, and opposition may become contribution.

A shared aim can emerge that none had conceived alone.

Where incompatibility remains, choose with its cost in view. The person who must yield remains part of the whole.

Smooth agreement proves little about the worth of a purpose. Many can act together against the good of those excluded. Examine what their unity accomplishes.

Preserve the voice that can disclose a defect. A boundary, an objection, or a necessary distance may keep the relation truthful.

Harmony needs room for the note that asks it to change.



6. The Measure of Success

Measure achievement through the life it leaves possible.

Reckon aim, means, cost, and consequence together. An advantage counted alone can hide the burden that purchased it.

The Strategist makes a fitting act serve several worthy ends. Find where one benefit sustains another. Preserve what would otherwise need repair. Let protection open room for understanding, and understanding lessen the need for repeated protection.

Count effort and time;
Count damage and repair;
Count trust, dependence, and future freedom;
Count the work left to absent hands.

A cost carried beyond sight remains a cost.

What survives unharmed belongs among the fruits of the act. A patient preservation may leave strength for the hour that needs it. An immediate gain may close tomorrow's best course.

Shared success need not satisfy every first demand. Change the terms where a better arrangement can serve the worthy purposes. No person's defeat is required merely to certify another's victory.

Judge the decision and its outcome distinctly. A sound act can meet an unforeseen obstruction. A careless act can inherit unearned help. Discover what actually sustained the result.

Recognize enough.

When further pursuit would consume the peace, freedom, or relationship that gave the aim its worth, end the pursuit.

The Master Strategist can leave a sufficient achievement whole.



7. A Life Worth Living

Give peace a life to inhabit.

The end of attack opens a space. Love, friendship, beauty, enjoyment, care, and understanding give that space its worth.

Let attention receive what is beautiful.
Let kindness arrive without being put to use.
Let delight have its hour.

The Strategist cultivates strength so that fewer days must turn upon its use. The Wise give those days to what can flourish.

Skill can acquire an appetite for demonstration. A power developed for necessity may begin seeking necessities to prove itself.

Keep the capacity available. Release its claim upon the day.

Ordinary care continues the work of peace: attention offered, work carried, company welcomed, freedom respected. The right to rest, receive, create, and enjoy becomes real through such participation.

During danger, remember this wider life. The urgent encounter must receive its answer; it need not become the measure of everything.

Let the life you seek to preserve govern the means of preserving it.



BOOK II
THE ACTOR AND THE ACT



8. Will and Decision

Will is the faculty of choice.

The Actor decides through attention, feeling, memory, imagination, understanding, and judgment. Their cooperation gives the decision its reach.

Attention brings forward.
Feeling gives significance.
Memory carries experience.
Imagination opens the absent.
Understanding finds relations.
Judgment brings them to the act.

Distinguish what is possible from what is seen as possible; what is selected from what can be carried out; what is done from all that follows it.

A passage may exist unseen. An imagined passage may lack support. Preparation brings perception and capacity nearer to one another.

Decision is itself an act. It commits strength, alters expectation, and prepares the ground of later decisions. A strategy takes shape through these relations.

Deliberate where deliberation is needed. Let cultivated responsiveness act where understanding has become fluent. Every movement need not await an inward command.

Others also decide. Their knowledge, cooperation, refusal, and initiative enter what your intention can accomplish. Shared means extend reach and make that reach dependent upon continuing support.

Answer for your participation and the consequences reasonably within sight. Learn from those disclosed later. Responsibility can remain where control of the whole result is absent.

The faculty that directs is itself influenced.
Examine what directs it.



9. Attention and Awareness

Concentrate upon the task. Keep its surroundings available.

Attention brings a part forward; awareness leaves room for the field. Without focus, strength scatters. Without an opening, focus can carry a decision beyond the conditions that justified it.

Attend according to consequence.

Repeated demands can govern the direction of attention before judgment begins. Fear makes a sign vivid. Expectation prepares an answer. Familiar urgency can hide unfamiliar change.

The Observer examines the ordering of attention as part of the field.

A reaction noticed becomes available to judgment. An assumption seen can be questioned before it becomes a command. Even a brief interval may admit another act.

Preserve the means of noticing. Fatigue, pain, hunger, and haste narrow what can be held in view. Rest and dependable help can reopen it.

Give correction a route into the work.

Where interruption is forbidden, a warning may arrive too late. Where every demand is urgent, none receives sufficient attention. Make time for a question and give it access to someone able to change the course.

When focus shifts, reckon what is left unattended and who must carry the absence.

Let the work hold attention without setting the limits of what can be seen.



10. The Counsel of Feeling

Receive the feeling. Examine the command it appears to give.

The sensation is present. Its cause and meaning may still be unknown.

Fear can report danger.
Anger can report injury.
Pleasure can report a good.
Ease can report freedom, familiarity, or a difficulty still unseen.

Bring each report before the whole situation.

Desire points toward an end; its force does not establish the end's worth. Consider what pursuit would require and whose life it would change.

Aspiration can sustain a long work. Compulsion makes repetition its own urgency. Aversion may preserve a necessary boundary or guard a familiar discomfort from examination.

Learn which movement is asking to lead.

The Learner admits the feeling honestly and keeps its explanation open. Another's account may disclose an intention different from the one feared, or an injury hidden within the result enjoyed.

Steadiness keeps this counsel available. Numbness can silence the warning; agitation can turn it into an order.

Give feeling room to speak and judgment room to decide.

Courage may carry fear. Care may uncover pain. Neither pain nor fear decides whether the act is fitting. Examine its purpose, means, and consequence.

A person deeply moved can still move precisely.



11. Memory and Imagination

Carry the lesson. Keep the hand open.

Memory preserves experience, affection, obligation, and the consequences of earlier conduct. Through it, a promise can outlive the hour in which it was made.

Memory also carries interpretation. Later evidence can change the meaning of an event without changing what occurred. Let understanding reach backward.

Imagination reaches beyond what is present. It opens a course before the means to take it exist.

Give the course its ground:
The support it needs,
The participation it requires,
The consequences it prepares.

Preparation can make an imagined possibility usable. Anticipation can also make a feared event feel accomplished before it has begun.

Keep the future answerable to what arrives.

An old injury can supply the shape of every expected threat. An anticipated triumph can alter which memories receive attention. Watch these powers shaping one another.

Meet the person now present. A remembered adversary may have changed. A remembered self may deny capacities already gained.

Preserve the obligation while revising the story.
Preserve the lesson while discovering its limits.
Preserve affection while allowing another life to move.

The Companion can remember faithfully and begin again.



12. The Work of Judgment

Know what you seek.
Know the field it enters.
Know the means, the limits, and the lives they touch.

Purpose cannot supply missing capacity. Capacity cannot confer worth upon a purpose. Judgment brings them together within the actual situation.

Know yourself, the other participants, and the way an act becomes effective. Each knowledge corrects the reach of the others.

Compare the courses by what they preserve, change, risk, consume, and leave possible.

Decision changes its own field.

A commitment closes some branches and strengthens others. A refusal can remove a support upon which later plans depended. A new relation can make possible what no earlier calculation contained.

Let the principles work together: one directs, another enables, another limits, another times, another corrects.

Hear hesitation before overcoming it. It may disclose missing knowledge, a conflict of duties, or a refusal that has not found its words. It may also spend the hour without changing the grounds of action.

Measure inquiry by urgency, consequence, reversibility, and room for correction. An uncertain act may be necessary. An irreversible act may require more knowledge than the next available moment can give.

Carry the responsibility of a decision while allowing its grounds to be revised. Action discloses what reflection cannot reach alone.

Be firm enough to act and free enough to learn.



13. The Self and Its Image

An image can become a second undertaking within every undertaking.

The Actor must then perform the work and preserve an appearance. When these conflict, the appearance may consume the work.

Keep the useful functions of self: memory, continuity, judgment, responsibility, and a place from which to act. Ego can gather and protect them. Its claim must remain answerable to the life they serve.

Examine what the image demands.

It may forbid an admission.
It may require a demonstration.
It may turn a correction into a threat.
It may keep a role alive after its work is finished.

Praise can strengthen the demand. Criticism can expose it. Neither decides the worth of the person or the truth of the conduct.

Acknowledge the error where it is yours. One admission can recover the freedom that many explanations would consume.

Release the defended image; retain the memory and responsibility needed to answer for the act.

The Wise need no permanent appearance of wisdom. The Peacemaker need not seem untouched by disturbance. Growing competence can receive correction without staging its own superiority.

Let the encounter call forth the capacity it needs.
Carry the understanding gained through a role.
Let the role become smaller when its work is done.



14. From Inner Contest to Partnership

Know the tactics by which you defeat your own purpose.

Avoidance changes the subject.
Justification conceals the cost.
Distraction spends the interval.
An endless accusation prevents the next beginning.

Study their return. Learn what each is protecting.

One concern urges movement; another fears what movement may destroy. Desire, memory, fear, and commitment may each preserve something necessary. Inner challenge becomes useful when a claim can answer, learn, and change.

When every proposal is attacked and no contribution can succeed, contest hardens into division. Strength is spent before the outer encounter begins.

Change the inward arrangement.

Give different concerns their proper objects, duties, and times. What demanded command may need a dependable place within the whole. Let protection inform courage, desire receive proportion, and memory advise without closing the future.

The self-fight becomes a self-dance.

One movement makes room for another. Challenge continues as cooperation. The energy of resistance becomes available for discernment and action.

From the dance, cultivate partnership.

No single impulse, fear, or account of the self requires permanent command. Each gains use through correction by the others.

Equanimity grows where the whole person can meet difficulty without first defeating a part of themselves.



15. Equanimity Amid Change

Keep judgment available amid disturbance.

Feeling may rise. The field may change. Others may demand an answer before its grounds are clear. Preserve the capacity to perceive, decide, protect, and follow through.

Inward cooperation gives steadiness one support. The surrounding life gives it others: rest, bodily strength, dependable relationships, and practical provision.

Attend to both. A settled purpose still needs a body able to carry it. Receive help while help can still be used.

A calm surface may conceal a judgment unable to change. A troubled face may accompany exact perception. Examine what the person can actually do.

A stable commitment can admit error and alter its method. Equanimity travels with that capacity.

Release the need to appear composed when a warning, a refusal, or an admission is required.

Where a pause restores perception, pause.
Where delay closes protection, move.
Where strength is insufficient, seek support.
Where continuation would consume the means of judgment, set a boundary or stop.

Hear feeling without giving it immediate command. Let steadiness enter the act.



16. Freedom and the Actor

Give freedom the means to act.

A permitted course requires capacity. A permitted refusal requires a way to bear its consequences. Judgment needs information, time, and an opening through which a demand can be questioned.

Build these supports where they are absent.

The Actor also carries a history. Habit, memory, fear, dependence, and earlier commitments shape what appears desirable and what scarcely appears possible.

Examine the limit around the person and the limit within the way of seeing.

Selection compares courses. Deliberation works with incomplete knowledge. Inner conflict contends among demands. Clear perception may sometimes disclose a fitting act before that contention begins.

Do not confuse the feeling of certainty with clarity. Ask what the judgment has considered and what tradeoffs remain.

The Guide strengthens another's capacity to judge. Information, support, and a practicable path give assistance substance. The resulting decision belongs to the person receiving them.

Help becomes command when every departure from the giver's preference threatens its withdrawal.

Commitment can close options while making a worthwhile freedom dependable. The number of available courses is therefore no sufficient measure.

Restrict the means of coercion. Close the effective paths of attack. One person's harmful undertaking may need to lose its reach for another person's agency to recover.

Preserve the person within this restraint. Let people participate without becoming instruments of another's will.



17. Conduct and Character

Each act prepares something in the Actor.

What is repeated becomes easier to find, easier to imagine, easier to begin. A path forms through use.

Compare the character intended with the conduct made easier. Intention, action, consequence, and the conditions of repetition form it together.

Dependable care benefits from a prepared response. Difficulty need not consume the time required to invent it anew.

Keep that response awake to its occasion.

Courage can become attached to seeming fearless.
Patience can continue after waiting has ceased to help.
Firmness can defend a judgment already corrected by events.

Let the purpose examine the trait.

An act can express the person you understand yourself to be. It can also reveal what that understanding had excluded. Permit conduct to teach identity.

Failure leaves consequences to address and knowledge to receive. One encounter need not pronounce the person finished. The capacity to admit and correct error can itself become a dependable strength.

Others experience what intention overlooks. Let their account of your reliability, burdens, and omissions reach the next act.

Integrity grows through such answerability. Care and commitment can remain steady while their expression changes.

Cultivate what peace can depend upon. Revise what habit continues after its purpose has gone.



18. Alignment of Purpose and Conduct

Give the purpose a path through conduct.

The aim must reach the method;
The promise must meet capacity;
The result must answer to those who live with it.

Know the work of each act. Protection closes a route of harm. Refusal withholds participation. Redirection alters a course. Cooperation joins capacities. Withdrawal changes access and exposure.

Compose them according to what peace requires here.

Successive decisions can carry one purpose through changing positions, means, and rhythms. Let others depend upon the commitment without requiring every movement to remain fixed.

Where conduct repeatedly defeats the declared value, find the break. Capacity may be missing; the method may be mistaken; the arrangement may restore the difficulty; the declared purpose may conceal another.

Let the finding change what follows.

Fidelity can require a new form. Keep what gave the old form worth; release what now obstructs it.

Correction may need to travel farther. An aim can prove too narrow for the lives it affects. Permit newly understood consequences to change the purpose itself.

In shared work, let participants help form that purpose. An aim remains shared through their continuing ability to alter it.

A coherent course can change direction and remain answerable for the ground already crossed.



19. The Judgment of the Wise

The Wise consider what deserves to be done.

Skill finds an effective course. Experience enlarges the means. Wisdom weighs the destination, the means of reaching it, and the life that will remain.

Join purpose to proportion,
Proportion to timing,
Timing to circumstance,
And all to consequence.

A general principle receives its work from the particular field. Other principles supply its support, limit its reach, and correct its use. Let them meet in the act.

Experience becomes understanding when the conditions of its result become clear. Success may have borrowed unseen help. Failure may reveal missing capacity. Another person's account may uncover a cost concealed by both.

Receive correction before experience hardens into authority.

Know where competence ends. Seek the knowledge held elsewhere. The best beginning may belong to another.

Discern when further effort will preserve a good, deepen it, or consume it. Completion has its hour.

A sound undertaking can end.
A capable person can yield.
An insight can continue after its first interpreter falls silent.

Wisdom appears also in care sustained, beauty received, responsibility carried without possession, and another person's freedom welcomed.

Its measure extends through the quality of living.



BOOK III
SEEING AND BEING SEEN



20. The Observer Within the Field

The Observer stands within the field.

Position gives access. Interest directs attention. A question brings something forward and leaves something in shadow.

Learn where your view begins.

Your action changes the evidence that comes next. Your reaction enters the situation you are trying to understand. Questioning, watching, withholding, and describing also have effects.

Include them in the reckoning.

Hostility may disclose a contribution you have made to its continuation. Agreement may disclose the absence of a livable refusal. Examine the appearance with the conditions producing it.

Another participant can reveal what private reflection cannot reach. They may feel an influence invisible from the position exerting it.

Let their account enter without requiring all accounts to carry equal weight. One knows intention; another knows the means; another bears the consequence. Ask what each can disclose and what supports the claim.

Participation does not erase the distinction between event and interpretation. Evidence still matters. Your own claim remains among those to be examined.

Keep observation open to its effects.

The place from which you see need not become a position you must defend.



21. The Event and Its Interpretation

Distinguish what happened,
What it may mean,
What may have caused it,
And what may follow.

Each claim reaches a different distance. Let its grounds travel with it.

Sincerity shows that a person believes the account. Agreement shows that others share it. Accuracy requires the account to meet what it describes.

A comforting explanation may still misdirect the act. Shared certainty may close the gate through which correction would enter.

Protect the ground of shared judgment:
Evidence that can be reached;
Questions that can be asked;
Accounts that can be distinguished;
Correction that can change a decision.

A finding unable to reach conduct leaves the old error in command.

Examine shared words. The same name may conceal different promises, duties, means, and expected actions. Bring these into view before apparent agreement commits the participants farther.

Inference is necessary. No single observation contains every relation that matters. Make the passage from evidence to claim clear enough for another to examine.

Then disagreement can locate the point at which the accounts diverge.

When the understanding changes, carry the change to those still acting upon the former account.



22. The Reach of Perspective

Look widely enough to find the relation.
Look closely enough to preserve the difference.

More detail alone does not make a clearer view. Relevance gives knowledge direction.

The Strategist examines the position and the question from which the field is seen: what they reveal, what they emphasize, what they exclude, and what remains uncertain.

Attend to recurrence. Attend to the expected event that fails to arrive. An exception can reveal the support upon which the familiar pattern depended.

Let evidence correct the pattern before the pattern dismisses the evidence.

Another standpoint can enlarge the field. Preserve the differences in knowledge, power, and responsibility that give its contribution meaning.

A view becomes difficult to change when the person must defend it as part of themselves. Release that claim. Clarity need not remain in the shape through which it first arrived.

A new perspective may disclose a new path. The path still needs means, support, and a worthy purpose.

Widening must return to the act within reach. Keep the account clear enough to guide that act and flexible enough to receive its consequences.

Cultivate this movement throughout life. Return to the familiar question; discover what the field, the Observer, or both have changed.



23. Listening and Correction

Let the other person enter the account you are forming of them.

Their words may be remembered exactly while their meaning is lost. Expectation can arrange a new voice into an old shape.

Let the speaker correct that shape.

The Companion listens beyond the feeling of mutual understanding. Ask what has actually been understood, and allow the difference time to appear.

A person can clarify their intention. The facts they describe remain answerable to evidence.

Understanding can accompany disagreement.
Agreement can accompany a refusal to participate.
Cooperation in one purpose can preserve a wider difference.

Know which relation is present before acting upon it.

Attend to what is costly to say. Power, dependence, haste, and your own certainty can silence an answer before it is offered.

Preparation to defend occupies the speaker's room.
Eagerness to classify can imprison the meaning.
Readiness to advise may outrun the discovery of the need.

Receive the contribution before deciding its use.

Then let it change something: the response, the aim, the burden assigned, or the responsibility accepted. Listening reaches its consequence in what follows.

A truthful boundary may preserve the relation from false agreement. Necessary disagreement may keep shared work from an avoidable harm.

Keep care present while the difference becomes clear.



24. The Paths of Information

Follow knowledge to the place where it can change action.

A fact may be known where no decision can be altered. A decision may be made where the needed fact never arrives.

The path between them belongs to the strategy.

Examine access, delay, omission, repetition, and credibility. Each alters what a message can accomplish.

Knowledge arriving before commitment can prevent the need for repair. Knowledge arriving afterward may still guide it. Find the hour in which the information can do its work.

Let correction travel as far as the account it corrects.

Where an uncertainty matters, seek an answer fitted to the decision it could change. A small inquiry can reveal a condition, an opening, or another's readiness to act.

Reckon its burden, exposure, and ambiguity. The question itself may change the field or reveal more than its answer teaches.

Distinguish independent knowledge from repeated report. Many channels can carry one mistaken beginning. Find the separate path.

Listen where authority is absent. Those carrying the work may hold what those directing it need to know.

Give what arrives time to be understood and power to be used. An open channel alone cannot move a hand denied the means to act.



25. Belief and the Possible

Belief prepares the field before the first movement.

What seems possible invites preparation. What seems futile is neglected. What seems inevitable may receive obedience before its power has been tested.

Know your own purpose and capacity. Keep another person's description from becoming the command by which you act.

The Strategist studies what an adversary's belief permits and prevents.

A mistaken account may limit their preparations or direct them toward a response you need not supply. Visible conduct can remain consistent with that account while real capacity develops beyond their sight.

Keep protective preparation undisclosed where disclosure would strengthen the attack.

Withholding correction, confirming an untruth, and inventing a false claim are different acts. Let an existing mistaken inference remain where its continuation protects; judge what its continuation also prepares.

Confusion may restrain one attack and encourage another. Its advantage lasts only while the actual relation supports it.

Examine your own beliefs about the adversary. Motives, means, and intentions can change. A formerly useful account must still meet present conduct.

Make a better possibility credible through dependable action. An act can give experience what argument has failed to establish.

When cooperation opens, reconsider disclosure. An old misunderstanding may obstruct the peace it once protected. Share what mutual judgment now requires.



26. The Limits of Knowing

Give the unknown its proper weight.

Ask what is missing, why it matters, and which decision its discovery could change.

Map the possible courses. Trace the conditions supporting each, the evidence for confidence, and the consequence of being mistaken.

Let the estimate bear only the precision its knowledge permits. An exact number cannot supply absent grounds. A clear limit is useful knowledge.

Several explanations may lead toward the same prudent act. A small difference may deserve close inquiry when it separates a reversible movement from an irreversible loss.

Weigh the next question against its cost.

Information can improve judgment while consuming the hour in which action matters. Inquiry can expose preparation or distract from a condition already understood.

Sufficient clarity depends on urgency, consequence, reversibility, and the means of later correction.

Where uncertainty must remain, prepare a course able to receive new knowledge. Let the provisional decision direct real conduct without closing every route of revision.

An unfavorable outcome alone does not condemn the judgment. Examine what could have been known, what was considered, and what action disclosed.

Answer for the decision. Keep strength available for the part of the field revealed only after movement begins.



27. Expectation and Surprise

Repeated conduct teaches expectation.

Others prepare for its return. The Actor may also begin expecting only the familiar response from themselves.

Anti-Pattern changes that continuation.

Recognize the pattern, or make it legible.
Break the expected step where the break serves.
Use the interval before the former understanding is restored.

The Strategist can interrupt the rhythm an attack requires. The Learner can stop a habit before its familiar completion. The Companion can let dependable care arrive in a welcome, unexpected form.

Choose the surprise by the purpose, the Observer, the circumstance, and what can be done within the opening.

Some expectations deserve fulfillment.

Keep the promise dependable. Let its expression vary. Play can renew attention and shared enjoyment without spending another person's security.

A broken sequence may still repeat a larger pattern. A single interruption may leave the supports of recurrence intact. Look to what will resume afterward.

Surprise can also separate a shared act from its support. Preserve the references by which those moving together can find one another.

Grounding and restraint govern the opening. Use it to loosen harmful repetition, preserve freedom, or admit a fresh form of life.

Change the pattern while integrity remains recognizable.



28. The Visible and the Withheld

Let action be visible where visibility serves. Keep preparation concealed where understanding would arm the attack.

Intention, capacity, and possible response need not appear together. Their different hours of disclosure can alter what an adversary is able to prepare.

Wield uncertainty through real change.

Act so that their account must change.
Before the account becomes useful, act again.
Change the relation they are trying to master.
Before they regain their bearings, act again.

Let each movement accomplish something while the earlier question remains open.

Develop beyond the understanding that once described you. Let their attention follow the visible movement while position, capacity, or direction has already changed.

The advantage requires an interval and the means to use it. Empty motion spends both.

Silence can shield preparation, withhold fuel from provocation, or make room for reflection. It can also leave an obligation unanswered. Examine what continues while nothing is said.

A private plan differs from a false account. Concealment does not remove responsibility for the consequences of the act.

Disclose according to purpose, duty, vulnerability, and the understanding cooperation needs.

There is also an hour to wait. A fresh intervention can interrupt the effect already unfolding or crowd another person's growing understanding. Let the earlier act finish its work where completion serves.

When the relation changes, change the boundary of disclosure.
What obstructed attack may now obstruct peace.
What needed shelter may be ready to be shared.



29. The Gaze of Others

An imagined audience can direct the act before anyone has spoken.

Reputation asks for its next performance. Approval can become a duty. Examine what that performance would take from the work.

Visible conduct gives others evidence of intention, capacity, reliability, and limits. Their expectations shape what they make of it.

One act can enter several accounts.

Choose disclosure carefully. Reckon with interpretations you cannot command. The act continues through judgments made from places other than your own.

The Wise return attention to the undertaking and those it affects.

Where people depend upon a promise, give them grounds to trust it. Where an adversary depends upon predicting the response, let a change of form protect it.

Integrity can remain steady through both.

A visible admission of error can alter your own image and another's expectation. Let the correction carry its meaning without demanding admiration for having made it.

Praise may precede knowledge of the result. Criticism may miss a necessary condition. Consider their grounds and hear those bearing the actual consequences.

Quiet integrity retains its worth without an audience. The unseen act still changes the person who performs it and the lives through which it passes.



30. Conduct as a Mirror

Let conduct make the encounter clear.

The other person meets both their own action and the person toward whom it was directed.

An expected retaliation may have promised justification. Restraint leaves that continuation absent. A provocation may have required a role. Denial leaves it unoccupied.

The first act stands exposed to its author in a different light.

Give the question room to arise within them.

Stand willing to be seen. Your motive, error, impatience, care, and desire to prevail enter the same reflection. The Practitioner cannot offer a truthful mirror while demanding exemption from it.

Own what is yours to acknowledge. An honest admission can open the relation that mutual defense has closed. It makes another response possible; it does not command one.

The mirror also belongs within companionship. Assistance can disclose a neglected need. Patient correction can reveal a habit. Dependable care can make an overlooked good visible.

Grounding gives reflection its purpose: clearer participation, release from harmful misunderstanding, and a different future for the relation.

Keep the person within care while their self-image changes.

The elegance of an act proves nothing about whether insight occurred. Attend to what follows.

Offer truthful conduct. Leave what another comes to see through it within their freedom.



31. Recognition and Dignity

Protection need not wait for recognition.

Close the path of harm. Leave the inward work uncommanded.

A public encounter can preserve private knowledge. Each person may recognize their part without having their particular fault displayed before others.

Keep that room open.

Never choose public humiliation for persuasion, correction, deterrence, or victory. An audience must not become an instrument for the exposure you would refuse for yourself.

Grounding and the Golden Rule govern this clarity. The greater your power over the encounter, the greater the care required in making truth available.

Demand no confession, gratitude, credit, or agreement as payment for restraint.

Recognition is one event.
Acknowledgment is another.
Agreement is another.
Changed conduct requires its own evidence.
Restored trust requires its own grounds.

A person may see more than they say, admit more than they understand, or agree while their conduct remains unchanged.

Judge each by what can actually disclose it.

Honest insight may unsettle the person. Let the distress call forth care. Never cultivate or exploit it to obtain the response you seek.

The Peacemaker can allow recognition to be delayed, partial, or unannounced while remaining exact about the conduct that continues.

What requires restraint may be restrained.
What permits repair may be repaired.
Let conduct remain answerable and inward recognition remain free.



BOOK IV
THE FIELD IN MOTION



32. The Complete Situation

An act draws upon more than the hand that performs it.

Examine seven domains:
Body, which carries the act;
Attention, which finds its object;
Relationship, which joins its participants;
Work, which brings intention into conduct;
Resources, which supply its means;
Purpose, which directs it;
Renewal, which keeps these powers alive.

Strength in one can hide neglect in another. Follow what each receives and what each spends. A peace that exhausts its keepers has concealed part of its cost.

Examine five scales:
The personal;
The relational;
The communal;
The systemic;
Universal concern for the whole affected life.

At every scale, bodies bear the work, attention is occupied, resources are consumed, relationships are changed. Those absent from the decision may supply its strength or inherit its damage.

Enlarge the field of concern to include them.

When the scale changes, reckon afresh. Who participates? Who has authority? What carries a decision into effect? Who can correct it? Similar shapes can conceal different workings. What steadies one person may supply none of the means needed to make an institution dependable.

The Observer also stands in the field: supported, exposed, influential, and unable to see from every place.

Widen the view; find the act within reach. Receive what its consequences reveal.

The complete situation exceeds the account. The account must remain able to grow.



33. The Unequal Field

Permission has little strength where refusal cannot be endured.

One person can leave. Another would lose the means of living. Their agreement may use the same words; their freedom differs.

Weigh necessity beside consent,
Exposure beside strength,
Support beside resolve.

A title may authorize an act without supplying the means to perform it. A person without title may hold the provision upon which everyone waits. Discover what each can actually change.

Duration is part of the field. Waiting can replenish one side while consuming the other. Before calling for patience, reckon whose food, attention, safety, and reserve must sustain the interval.

Where one refusal could remove all protection, prepare another support. Where all provision passes through one permission, seek another source. Make the boundary livable for as long as it may need to hold.

Equal courage does not make an equal field.

Begin with the capacity present. Build the alternatives, reserves, and dependable help through which a better position can become real.

Those able to impose terms are answerable for what compliance costs. Use advantage to make protection dependable and participation worthwhile.

Judge the arrangement also from the place of those least able to leave it.

Strength serves peace when others can live securely without its continual display.



34. Position and Possibility

An advantage without a path into action cannot yet serve the Strategist.

Position joins reach, exposure, access, support, and dependence at this moment. Its strength lies in what can be done from it, at what cost, and for how long.

Trace the passage from means to effect.

Can the means arrive?
Can they be used?
Will the needed support continue?
What still rests upon another person's choice?

A clear plan supplies none of its absent conditions.

Open access, gather means, share support, establish dependable agreements: the field of practicable possibilities widens. Yet every new position makes demands. An opening may require a promise; the promise may close another route.

Reckon the later freedom alongside the present gain. Give confidence to a course according to the conditions it can sustain; leave uncertain what still depends upon unfinished work or unsettled consent.

From this position, what manner of meeting serves peace?
Protection, refusal, cooperation, redirection, withdrawal--each asks for different means. Several may belong within one approach.

Widen the paths of protection and shared work. Close the passages by which attack reaches its object. Leave the person a practicable course beyond attacking.

Then act, and take the position anew.

A promise has changed dependence. A refusal has changed access. The ground beneath the next decision has moved.



35. Configuration and Potential

A strength can be wasted by its place; a modest force can become decisive through its relation.

Order, distance, dependence, alignment, timing: change these, and the same elements acquire different powers.

Shih is the evolving potential of this configuration--what its relations favor, what they prepare, and where they tend toward instability or harm.

Unused capacity promises no effect. Its passage may be closed. Several strengths may contend for one narrow passage and consume one another there. A small contribution may complete an arrangement ready to receive it.

The Strategist sees both the means and their meeting.

Where one reachable change opens several useful possibilities, leverage is present. Trace the supports that carry the effect. If they fail beneath its weight, multiplication becomes collapse.

Advantage can gather danger around itself. What might fail together? What would become exposed? Who must sustain the gain? A powerful arrangement may exact more than its purpose deserves.

Refuse it when its danger outweighs its service to peace.

Preparation joins what is scattered, relieves what is overloaded, and brings strength into a better relation with resistance. The encounter begins changing before the first visible move.

The move changes it again.

A passage opens; another is watched.
A dependence loosens; another tightens.
A possibility becomes a commitment.

Choose with regard to the configuration that follows. The advantage of entering is only part of the advantage of being there.



36. Point and Center

A point marks a position.
A center gives positions their reference.

Command may stand in one place, provision enter from another, and purpose be determined elsewhere. Do not give one center the authority of all three.

From each position something is visible and something concealed. The Observer gains another view by receiving what can be seen from there.

Change the center of concern, and the meaning of an approach can change. Where vindication rules, correction threatens. Where the work stands foremost, correction can assist.

The center is held by its edges. Follow its strength outward to those whose dependable labor has made their needs easy to overlook.

Bodily footing, clear purpose, and trustworthy relationships support different parts of action. Their powers can assist one another; one cannot simply replace another.

Keep a reference steady enough for movement. Move the reference when it hides the life it was meant to serve.

The Wise can change the center without relinquishing care.



37. Space and Field

An empty place is not yet an open path.

Usable space requires room, time, attention, access, means, or permission. The field gives each its force through relationships, expectations, resistance, support, and obligations already present.

What looks vacant from outside may be occupied by work unseen.

To make an imagined course practicable, bring its conditions within reach and provide for their continuance. An opening useful for the first step may be unable to sustain the passage.

Enlarge the room for protection and cooperation. Restrict access to harm. Preserve a way by which the attacker can stop and still proceed with life.

Where one person's freedom grows by spending another's time, reckon both movements. Space taken here can become pressure elsewhere.

A pause can loosen expectation, restore attention, and make a different response visible. Necessary work continues through the pause. Arrange who will carry it and how they will be sustained.

The Actor cannot step outside the field by waiting, nor leave it unchanged by acting.

Disclosure changes expectation.
A promise changes dependence.
Refusal changes access.

Look again before the next step.



38. Line and Direction

Judge the destination before increasing the pace.

A line joins the present position to an aim. The actual course must pass through changing ground, the lives of others, and demands no line upon a plan can bear for it.

The shortest course may require the longest repair. A sheltered course may arrive after the need has passed. Weigh distance with duration, exposure with provision, arrival with what must continue afterward.

If an obstacle defeats the path, seek another path.
If the destination defeats the purpose, change the destination.

Fidelity can take an unfamiliar road.

Where intentions cross, establish passage: move together where the ground permits; determine an order where it does not; turn where continued approach would bring collision. Others need not first become obstacles before their purposes enter judgment.

Follow the chosen line beyond its earliest success. What life will be possible there? Which relationships will remain open? What will arrival make costly to begin again?

The Wise seek a destination that can be inhabited.



39. Circle and Boundary

A boundary takes its measure from what it protects.

Know the attention, commitment, resting place, or common work for which the circle is kept. Admit, delay, limit, or refuse access as its continuance requires.

Clear terms make participation dependable. Those approaching should be able to understand what is asked, question the terms, or depart. Details that would equip an attack need not accompany the invitation.

A well-kept boundary can make openness possible.

Its reckoning extends beyond its edge. Those outside may supply its strength or receive what it discards. Membership does not determine whose consequences count.

Nor does a line maintain itself. Someone watches, repairs, refuses, explains, and bears the strain of being tested. Give this work resources, relief, and a route for correcting its own conduct.

The Caretaker watches the gate and the keeping of the gate.

Open when welcome can be sustained.
Close when its conditions are being consumed.
Widen when participation can grow.
Narrow when renewal requires less exposure.
Move the boundary when its former place no longer serves.

An enclosure can preserve its shape while obstructing the freedom for which it was made. In that hour, guarding the line more fiercely may deepen the failure.



40. Spiral and Return

Return carrying experience; arrive willing to find a different world.

Repetition establishes a path.
Reflection examines where it led.
Revision changes the next passage.

These are three works. Repeating the first does not perform the other two.

The Learner returns to the conditions of success, including support now absent. What has changed? In which direction? For whom? Understanding may deepen while trust weakens; influence may widen while correction loses its entrance.

Where injury follows every recovery, find what recovery has restored. Strength may be returning only to feed the demand that exhausted it.

Change the relation through which the return begins.

Return also to the principle. Action can disclose a meaning the words concealed; consequence can reveal a limit the first understanding missed. The seed guides its unfolding, and the unfolding teaches the seed.

Carry the lesson without assigning every new encounter the parts of an old grievance.

Some returns are for completion. Make the repair. Fulfill the promise. Settle the unfinished responsibility.

Then let the return end.



41. Vertex and Threshold

One decision can gather many histories and divide many futures.

A vertex marks where directions meet. A threshold marks where the conditions change. Habits, injuries, commitments, and prepared capacities may converge at either.

Watch for the change of terms. What held a boundary yesterday may obstruct a passage today. An old obligation carried into a new agreement may exceed what that agreement can bear.

Before crossing, reckon the position beyond it.

What must accompany the movement?
What support will remain?
Which route cannot afterward be recovered?

Give an irreversible passage its full weight. Where a limited, reversible step can disclose whether the opening is usable, do not load it with the whole undertaking.

Another person's readiness belongs to them. Information, support, time, and a usable invitation can prepare a passage; refusal must remain possible. Changed conduct and inward recognition may arrive at different times. Receive each for what it actually permits.

Some openings close while deliberation continues. Capacity prepared earlier can meet their brief hour.

Cross with the needed means. Carry observation into action, discernment into cooperation, and regard into refusal. The new phase receives these powers in a new arrangement.



42. Triangle and Relationship

When two positions cannot move, examine what connects them to a third.

A shared need, a sustaining condition, or another participant may open a passage. The connection must bear actual work; its appearance settles no disagreement.

Two people may each trust the Guide while trust between them remains undeveloped. This can make a meeting possible. Use the meeting to strengthen their own powers of understanding and decision. If every choice must return through the Guide, mediation has gathered a new dependence.

An audience is also a third presence. Before it, correction may become a contest over standing. Give inward recognition room beyond the demand to make an admission for others to witness.

Look outside the agreement. Cooperation between two can spend the freedom of a third. A bargain incomplete in its reckoning may look complete to those it benefits.

When one connection changes, reconsider the others. Renewed trust may lessen the need for mediation; weakened support may alter what either party can promise.

Your arrival enters this geometry. Make the offered role clear enough to be questioned, and leave those concerned a real part in choosing what follows.



43. Square and Order

Order makes distinct contributions dependable.

Join purpose, responsibility, provision, and correction. Divide the work according to its needs. A square encloses a field; it does not appoint four bearers for every task.

Give a duty its means:
Knowledge of what is required;
Time in which to do it;
Access to what it needs;
Discretion where the description ends.

A name beside a task supplies none of these.

When commitments can be relied upon, attention is released for judgment and improvisation. Dependability gives freedom a footing within shared work.

Keep a road open from consequence to correction. The person who discovers a failure must be able to reach someone who can alter the rule, provision, or decision that sustains it.

An exception may reveal a defective rule. It may also reveal ground beyond that rule's proper reach. In the first case, revise the rule; in the second, reconsider its claim upon the occasion.

The Caretaker looks for duties without names: receiving delay, noticing omissions, restoring what visible effort has consumed. These workers may be holding the square upright while appearing nowhere in its order.

Give their labor a share of the provision before exhaustion announces its place.



44. Proportion and Balance

Enough in the wrong place, or at the wrong hour, can leave the whole unsupported.

Proportion joins amount, position, sequence, and rate. Find how the contributions fit one another and the work they must sustain.

Inward cooperation keeps judgment available. Outward support gives it means. A settled mind may still lack provision; abundant provision may still be spent by a divided will. Reckon both.

Equal needs may call for equal shares. Unequal needs, capacities, or responsibilities may require another distribution. Those who bear the result must be able to question it. Otherwise a privilege can call its own preservation balance.

In the golden ratio, the whole is to the larger part as the larger part is to the smaller.

The equality is exact. It appoints no just share of care, work, resources, authority, responsibility, or rest. Mathematics can sharpen the measure; it cannot decide whose burden ought to increase.

Judge the living proportion by purposes, needs, capacities, and consequences.

Economy includes the work hidden beneath the result: renewal, repair, replenishment, and the support that keeps each possible. Gain that consumes these borrows from a later hour.

Do the most with the least that can sustain the good.

When purpose or capacity changes, reconsider the share, the pace, and the order. Keep reserve for what cannot be apportioned beforehand.

The margin is part of the measure.



45. Resources and Dependencies

Follow provision beyond the place where it appears.

Effort, attention, time, materials, knowledge, and access each require a path to use. Reckon their renewal with their availability. Possession may still wait upon permission, arrival, or skill.

Supply depends upon maintenance. Maintenance draws upon knowledge and time. Those giving either may depend upon someone else carrying the work they have set aside.

The Caretaker follows this chain to the people it conceals.

A dependency is critical when its loss prevents important work from readily continuing. Its importance may have little likeness to its public standing. A quiet contribution can hold up a crowded undertaking.

Where interruption would be costly, prepare another source. Spread necessary knowledge. Keep reserve usable and provide for its renewal. Alternatives also require maintenance; their names alone furnish no protection.

Information has working dependencies of its own. A correction kept by the person who discovered it leaves others acting upon the former account. Carry it to those still governed by the error, in a form they can use.

Provision, expenditure, replenishment, redistribution: each moves cost and freedom among people. Reckon who supplies, who directs, who renews, and what else their labor could sustain.

When support changes, revisit the promises built upon it. An unchanged promise may now require changed means.



46. The Roots of Power

Power is disclosed by what can be made to happen.

Who authorizes?
Who withholds?
Who allocates the means?
Who fixes the terms and imposes the cost?
Who can change the decision?

The answers may name different people. Formal responsibility may stand far from the power to fulfill it; an obscure permission may govern the whole undertaking.

Trace the effect backward: through the decision, through its permissions, through resources and the dependencies that keep them available. Follow the actual passage of power beneath its display.

A causal node joins several processes. Change it, and more than the visible dispute may change. Before acting there, discover what else it carries.

Root access requires a condition that can actually be reached and altered. Knowing where harm begins does not supply that access.

Find what prepares the recurrence, rewards it, or restores it after interruption. The root may be fed by choices still being made. Change the relation that renews its strength.

Preserve necessary functions carried through the same arrangement. Give them another means of continuing as the harmful dependence is removed. Otherwise unmet need may reopen the path by which the former power returns.

A critical dependency may offer an advantage; it also discloses an obligation. The lives it sustains enter the reckoning with the harm it enables.

The Master Strategist seeks the reachable change through which protection becomes dependable, choice becomes practicable, and peace can continue after the first intervention has passed.



47. Hidden Advantage

A disadvantage belongs to a configuration.

Change the purpose, the timing, or the relation, and the same difference may acquire another use.

First see what the former question concealed. Then find what would bring that capacity into effect. A changed perspective can discover an advantage; a changed arrangement may still be needed to use it.

The Strategist traces the conversion:
What was the original operation?
Which relation changes?
What different consequence follows?
What permits it to follow?

Inversion works through this altered relation. What confined a movement may now help direct it; what demanded expenditure may now supply an effect. The favorable description must answer to something that can actually be done.

An advantage may last only while a narrow set of conditions holds. Use it for its hour; do not make it your name.

The cost can remain. A limitation can become useful while still requiring care, and learning from hardship gives no reason to praise every hardship endured.

Keep the larger purpose in command. Sustain what makes the advantage useful. When preserving it begins consuming the peace it was meant to serve, release it.



48. Momentum and Trajectory

Read the movement through time.

Momentum is the strength and manner of continuation.
Trajectory is the course that continuation takes.

Both change as participation, support, circumstances, and decisions change. A course can turn while gaining strength. It can hold its direction while losing the means to continue. A visible pause can leave the preparation for renewed movement untouched.

Habit, repeated choice, renewed provision, and a rule producing each succeeding state can all carry a process forward.

Discover which is at work.

The persistence of a habit and the inertia of a body obey different conditions. Extending the thought of continuation beyond physical motion establishes no universal law of conservation.

The Strategist asks what is likely to follow if the present generators and supports continue. Then the intended intervention can be weighed against the process it will actually touch.

Acceleration changes a rate.
Delay changes a meeting.
Redirection changes a course.
Interruption breaks a passage.

Stopping the visible movement may leave its source ready to begin again. Turning the course may bring it among new dependencies. Increasing its speed may consume the provision that carried it.

Follow the trajectory beyond the turn. Find what the new course requires, whom it reaches, and what must now be sustained.



49. The Conditions of Continuation

A stable condition can be the fruit of continuing work.
A changing form can be the fruit of an unchanged rule.

Discover what produces the next state.

A rule repeats. Resources are renewed. Rewards invite another act. An effect restores the conditions of its beginning. Through such relations, continuation can generate changes of speed, direction, and appearance without a new intervention.

Altering the present state can leave these generators free to produce another. A stopped effect can return when its support resumes.

The Master Strategist reaches beyond the interruption to a sustaining condition that can actually be changed.

Before removing it, trace the other work it carries. Provide another passage for necessary functions. An unmet need can restore the harmful dependence that seemed to have ended.

What appears to sustain itself may rest upon unnoticed attention, provision, or consent. Bring that work into view. Preserve and adjust the supports of beneficial continuations as circumstances change.

Then allow the initiating effort to recede.

What does the arrangement now produce? What does it require to go on producing it?



50. Patterns and Propagation

Watch what carries a pattern onward.

Demonstration gives it a visible form. Imitation and repeated use carry it into further acts. Shared conditions, rewards, and necessities can invite its return.

Reception changes the passage. Familiarity may ease acceptance or dull examination. Misuse may provoke resistance. Success may invite repetition where the support that made it successful is absent.

The outward shape can travel farther than its understanding.

The Observer follows the operative relation: where it serves, where it fails, where its meaning changes, and what new effects it acquires. Those receiving its consequences may know more of this change than those preserving its form.

Wider uptake can strengthen a useful practice. It can also multiply a defect before correction gains a hearing. Reach measures passage; neither reach nor familiarity establishes truth or worth.

Carry correction through the channels still bearing the former pattern. Give it enough clarity to change what people repeat.

To interrupt an unwanted recurrence, examine what welcomes it back. One refusal can open an interval. Changed conditions of reception can keep the old continuation from reclaiming that opening.



51. Feedback and Recurrence

An effect may return to alter what produced it. Trace the path of its return: this is feedback.

A returning effect can strengthen a movement, restrain it, hold a balance, or turn its direction. The act acquires another reach through what receives and carries it back.

Delay hides part of the circuit. An effect may still be forming while its apparent absence provokes another intervention. Repeated action can then accumulate beyond the intended amount.

Count what has begun without yet becoming visible.

A threshold changes the force of accumulation. Earlier increments may seem slight; together they can bring the arrangement beyond the point at which its former response remains possible.

Participation changes the circuit. Trust can open a passage; exclusion or withdrawal can close it. Each alters how later acts will be received and what choices remain.

A small beginning may reach widely through a particular connected chain. Find the links, their supports, their limits, and the places where the effect is diminished, redirected, or stopped. Smallness promises no magnitude of consequence.

If the return contradicts the account, revise the account before making it another reason to press harder.



52. Time and Ripening

Whose time is being spent?

While one waits, another works. Someone remains exposed; a reserve diminishes; an obligation grows. The patience of one may be supplied by the exhaustion of another.

An interval changes the field. Capacity matures or decays. Needs sharpen or pass. Expectations settle; relationships gain another history.

Distinguish ripening from disappearance.

Some openings require time for their parts to become ready. Others close during the wait. Keep the needed support alive until readiness and occasion can meet; a capacity arriving after its provision has failed may be unusable.

Before pressing harder, discover what the interval provides. Different processes develop through different conditions. Greater pressure can hasten one and obstruct another. Time alone gives no assurance of a changed mind.

The Wise ask what is growing, what is being lost, and what can be done now without consuming the later good.

When waiting preserves no worthwhile possibility, end the wait. What has already been spent cannot command another season.



53. Rhythm and Initiative

One encounter contains many rhythms:
The time to notice;
The time to prepare;
The time to respond;
The time to recover.

Their meetings create openings that no single pace reveals.

Arrive before support is ready, and strength may scatter. Arrive after the opening closes, and readiness may find no use. Timing joins the act to the process it must influence.

Where an adversary needs a settled interpretation, act within the interval before that interpretation can serve the attack. Let the next movement change the question while the earlier answer is still forming.

Keep initiative through real changes of position, preparation, or possibility. Speed without perception can surrender direction. Surprise without means can open a passage no one is ready to use.

A repeated pattern teaches expectation. Break it deliberately where the interruption serves a worthy purpose. While the old account is being revised, another movement may become possible.

Where cooperation depends upon a promise, fulfill it. Dependable conduct makes another person's planning possible; variation can remain free within a steady purpose.

Some acts require an interval to bear their effect. Further action may strengthen the development, hide it, or destroy its support.

Match, alternate, interrupt, accelerate, or yield according to what the process requires. The Master Strategist can hold initiative through the decision to wait.



54. Opportunity and the Changing Moment

Seeing a passage does not put it within reach.

Recognition, access, and duration have separate conditions. Opportunity joins them to a worthwhile purpose.

Prepare capacity before the precise occasion is known. A reserve, a practiced response, or a dependable relationship may give a brief opening the means to become an act. Some openings are cultivated indirectly through the readiness and circumstances that precede them.

At the moment of entry, weigh the whole direction. An immediate gain can bind the undertaking to work that carries it away from its purpose.

Enter where the advantage and its following obligations can serve. Decline where strength, a prior commitment, or a more fitting course deserves preservation.

Neither refuse through endless examination nor proceed because an opening appears. Know what must be settled now and what can safely be learned in motion.

Then observe what entry has changed. Others have responded; access has shifted; fresh possibilities and duties have appeared.

What was an opening may now be an obligation.



55. Changing the Configuration

When the same operation restores the same difficulty, change what permits the operation.

Roles, access, dependence, timing, purpose: alter their relations, and the field of possible conduct changes. A new name can inherit the old harm unchanged.

Transformation must reach the work itself.

Change the response where the response is at fault. Change the capacity or arrangement where repeated adjustments leave the cause untouched. Revise the aim when pursuing it would restore what peace requires ending.

Prepare the new conduct's footing before demanding its weight. Develop usable capacities, dependable support, and a way to continue the necessary life formerly carried by the harmful dependence.

The passage has needs of its own. Work continues while roles change. Obligations require bearers. Participants need time, knowledge, and means to discover what their altered contribution asks.

Preserve that continuity through the transition.

Follow the burdens as they move. Release here can become added labor elsewhere. Removing one dependence can gather another around the person directing the change.

The Master Strategist judges the new arrangement through its continuing effects:
Whose choices have widened?
What necessary work can endure?
Where can correction enter?
What restores the former harm now?



BOOK V
CULTIVATION AND READINESS



56. The Unseen Preparation

Prepare several responses before pressure narrows the question.

Approach, wait, refuse, withdraw, begin again. Each requires capacities that may have to grow long before its occasion is known.

A practiced hand carries earlier work. A dependable relationship brings another's strength within reach. Provision gives an intention time. Permissions and maintenance keep these means available through uneventful seasons.

Include their keepers in the reckoning of readiness.

Perfecting one response can make a different encounter harder to meet. Leave attention and means free enough for what preparation did not foresee.

A possibility costs something to keep open. Its maintenance can occupy the same time, provision, or care that another good requires. Choose which openings deserve their continuing expense.

Present commitments also bind the future. Some make a later response easy; others make its recovery costly. Reckon what the coming life will have to carry.

The exceptional occasion may never arrive. Strength can still enter ordinary work, dependable care, and the freedom to enjoy an untroubled day.

Peace need not borrow its worth from a future danger.



57. The Four Tasks of Cultivation

Cultivation has four tasks.

Prevent harm not yet present. Attend to the beginnings, neglected needs, and exposures through which it could enter--including those created by present action. Continued absence may be the work's achievement.

Abandon harm already present. Interrupt its operation and reach what restores it. Relief that leaves the source untouched may return the burden to renewed strength.

Develop benefit not yet present. Supply means, opportunity, and time. A capacity still forming needs conditions in which formation can continue; the seed asks for different care from the mature tree.

Sustain benefit already established. Renew its means, receive correction, and change its support as the surrounding life changes. A good can outgrow the provision that first made it possible.

The four tasks assist one another. Removing a harmful demand releases strength for development. An established benefit can lessen the need or appetite for harmful conduct. Prevention preserves time that recovery would consume.

Distinguish their beginnings, their limits, and their signs. One brings a new power into view. One lets an existing good continue quietly. One closes a danger's approach before there is anything to repair.



58. Fire and Experience

Among the five capacities, Fire is Experience.

The Learner acts and meets an answer. Effort encounters resistance; intention encounters other lives. Some experience is sought; some arrives uninvited.

Separate the event from the account carried away from it. Memory can preserve a detail while changing its meaning.

A success may owe much to unseen support, another's restraint, or a danger that happened not to arise. Bring these into the lesson before requiring the method alone to produce the result again.

A failure may disclose a missing condition, unsuitable means, or inadequate performance. Each calls for a different change. None settles the Learner's future capacity.

Those receiving the act possess parts of its history unavailable to the one who performed it. Their burden, fear, pleasure, or relief can alter the meaning of the apparent result.

Receive their experience too.

Many encounters can leave one mistaken explanation undisturbed. Repetition supplies occasions; attention reveals the difference; correction changes what follows.

Carry the discovered relation into the next judgment, the next preparation, the next act.



59. Wind and Technique

Wind is Technique.

Remember a technique with its object, purpose, and supporting conditions. Detached from these, its appearance can outlive its use.

Fluency frees attention from the familiar part of an act. Spend that freedom upon what has changed.

The Practitioner must be able to recognize the fitting occasion, alter the movement, and stop it. A larger repertoire gives alternatives; discernment gives each its hour.

A tool extends the hand only through access, knowledge, materials, and maintenance. Count these within the available skill.

Exact performance can still injure the purpose. Hear those receiving the act; they may reveal a defect hidden by its technical success. Reckon what the movement asks of them and leaves possible afterward.

Where the form serves, move through it freely. When its conditions end, stop.



60. Water and Adaptation

Water is Adaptation.

The situation continues changing while the response is formed. People reconsider; means arrive or diminish; earlier acts bear their consequences. Existing commitments enter this movement too.

Meet the complete situation.

A steady purpose can require another method, pace, position, or scope. If pursuit reveals that the purpose itself is mistaken, allow correction to reach it.

An ingenious accommodation can prolong the arrangement that injures. Examine what the adjustment keeps alive. A small difference may need a changed movement; a recurring harm may require another boundary, role, dependence, or undertaking.

Change at the scale of the cause.

Those whose contributions are altered need enough understanding and means to meet the new course. Sudden movement can spend the trust by which cooperation was possible.

Reckon the attention, preparation, and confidence consumed by change alongside the cost of continuing. Dependable conduct has value where it gives others room to act.

The Practitioner can meet changed ground by holding a boundary. Responsiveness includes knowing which demands deserve accommodation.



61. Earth and Understanding

Earth is Understanding.

Join the particulars through their causes, conditions, and dependencies. Mark where the grounds of the account end.

When two events arrive together, discover what brings them. When one follows another, their order may conceal a common source. Sequence alone does not establish the passage of cause.

An explanation earns practical use by disclosing a difference: what would have to change for the expected consequence to change?

Find the assumption bearing the greatest weight. Give it a way to be examined before building further upon it.

Include the Observer. An answer may respond to the question, to the position of the one asking, or to what answering would cost. Your presence helps shape what you are trying to understand.

Different accounts may disclose different parts of one relation. Some can be joined; some require correction; some remain unsettled. Their differences deserve examination, and none becomes interchangeable merely because all belong to the encounter.

Stand upon what is sufficiently understood for the act at hand. Keep the unresolved question beside the decision it may later change.



62. Space and Awareness

Space is Awareness.

Attention brings one thing near. Awareness keeps the surrounding field available.

Concentrate without forgetting the edge of concentration. What has moved beyond it may now govern the act.

Receive the recurring pattern and the exception that discloses its limit. Expectation can select evidence before judgment begins; fear, fatigue, and rehearsed answers can occupy the foreground unnoticed.

Let their influence become visible.

A contribution need not be assigned its place before it is heard. The unfamiliar can remain briefly unnamed.

The Observer also remains observable. Another may see the preference, omission, or effect that the present view conceals. Leave them an entrance.

Finite attention cannot face everywhere. Choose its object and remain able to turn. Enough room for what could change the response is room in which a new response can begin.



63. Understanding and the Capacity to Act

Recognizing a principle, discerning its occasion, and enacting it are three accomplishments. Each has conditions the others cannot supply alone.

A person may know the fitting response and lack the strength, skill, access, or support to perform it. Name the absence precisely enough for preparation to reach it.

The body must bear the movement.
Attention must find its occasion.
Means must reach their use.
Support must hold long enough.

Practical freedom grows where these meet.

Cultivated responsiveness can express a decision before inward speech has described each motion. Earlier choices have placed a capacity at the service of present perception.

A rehearsed answer can arrive just as quickly. Speed gives no measure of fit. The movement must receive the actual circumstance and remain able to change.

Prepare a range of responses before pressure narrows attention. An unfamiliar alternative may be hardest to reach at the hour it is most needed.

The Guide also examines the surrounding field. More time, changed access, usable permission, or shared support may make the response possible. A missing condition need not become a private defect assigned to the person.

Bring understanding into an act that can meet its occasion and receive correction from its effects.



64. The Uses and Limits of Form

Carry the purpose through the form.

A useful shape preserves relations through which skill can endure, travel, and develop. Remember its objects and conditions; otherwise its appearance may be inherited while its power is lost.

Success can make alteration seem needless at the very hour circumstances begin to change. Receive the difference before fluency settles the response.

Shared forms make meeting dependable. They can hold contributions together while leaving each participant room for judgment.

Where an adversary succeeds by prediction, a fixed expression may announce the whole response. Keep the capacity; vary its shape as the encounter requires. Give surprise a purpose beyond strangeness.

Continuing, altering, and leaving a form can each preserve what made it useful. Discern which relation now carries the purpose.

Abandoning a shape supplies no new skill. Freedom of form requires capacities from which another expression can arise.

The Practitioner carries the skill beyond its first shape.



65. Repetition and Variation

Repeat what should become dependable.
Vary what must remain responsive.

Repetition can make familiar conduct exact enough to release attention. Variation reveals which conditions bear the effect and which features can change without losing it.

An unwanted pattern also gains an easy path. Notice its return while another response remains available; act deliberately otherwise.

Anti-Pattern can interrupt one's own expectation as well as another's prediction. Surprise may loosen a habit, disrupt an adversarial rhythm, or bring fresh attention into shared life.

Use the opening for what it makes possible. Receive what follows; novelty alone proves no improvement.

Some expectations deserve fulfillment. Repeated care lets others rest. An honest commitment gives another person ground upon which to arrange an act.

The Practitioner learns where steadiness opens freedom and where a broken pattern opens it. One relationship may require both.

Keep the promise alive through faithful conduct; keep its expression alive through attention.



66. Capacity Beyond the Familiar

The name can travel more easily than the capacity.

A resemblance begins an inquiry. Find the operative relation that produced the effect. Is it present here? What now sustains it? Who can alter it, and by what means?

Body, attention, relationship, work, resources, purpose, renewal: each gives a capacity different objects, mechanisms, and demands. Similar expressions can require different knowledge, materials, permissions, and support.

Carry what still works. Supply what the new domain requires.

Scale changes the reckoning again. Personal conduct, relations among people, communal arrangements, and systems of decisions have different paths of consequence. Universal concern widens whose lives enter judgment; the act still needs an actual reach.

A person's steadiness and an institution's dependability require their own means.

As scale widens, communication may travel farther, effects may arrive later, dependence may become harder to see, and those carrying the cost may lose access to correction. Find where their experience can reach the decision.

Competence deserves attention within its range. It does not appoint its possessor to authority elsewhere. The right to direct depends also upon the terms through which others participate, willingly or under obligation.

The Guide lets the new field correct the old resemblance. Familiarity remains useful while it can learn what differs.



67. Cultivating Circumstances

Give a better course somewhere to continue.

Develop capacity. Make support usable. Open access. Change the dependence that returns a person to the harm they seek to leave.

A request for changed conduct gains substance when living differently becomes possible.

Time and consistency can accomplish what an isolated effort cannot. Repeated care makes an expectation credible; a credible expectation permits commitment; commitment gives another act room to begin. Attend to what each stage actually sustains.

Preparation may remain quiet until other conditions mature. Capacity, relationship, and opportunity can grow at different rates before meeting in one moment.

Preserve what already supports good conduct while preparing what is absent. An eager change can uproot the very provision through which the new capacity was to develop.

The Caretaker prepares the field. Those living within it still choose, and their contributions can reveal a future the preparation did not contain.

Let the better course depend less upon exceptional courage and more upon conditions people can inhabit.



68. The Uncertain Harvest

A long preparation may meet no occasion.

Its advantage may arrive late, arrive in another form, or never arrive. Foresight must leave room for all three.

The Wise weigh the value of keeping a possibility open against its continuing expense. Time, attention, and provision held here are unavailable elsewhere during the holding. Include the good being passed by.

Uncertainty need not make preparation worthless. A modest provision may preserve an opening of great possible value. Its uncertainty remains part of the judgment.

As knowledge changes, reconsider continuation, alteration, and ending. Past effort supplies a lesson; it gives no command to spend indefinitely.

Distinguish delayed growth from absent progress through the conditions of what is being cultivated. Patience needs signs it can recognize, and hope needs room to receive correction.

The purpose may change before the preparation is complete. Acquired capacity may find another use, enter ordinary life, or remain quietly available.

A different fruit can still be worth receiving.



69. Provision and Reserve

Enough to begin can be too little to complete the passage.

Reckon effort, attention, time, materials, knowledge, access, and the help of others. An abundance of one may leave another wholly absent.

Possession is only part of provision. The resource must be reachable and usable when the act requires it.

Expenditure carries the present movement.
Maintenance keeps the means serviceable.
Replenishment restores what has been spent.
Reserve holds room for a later response.

Each makes a different claim upon what is available.

The Caretaker follows who supplies, who spends, and who renews. Abundance maintained by another's exhaustion has hidden part of its price.

Reserve can protect recovery, correction, refusal, or a change of course after the first intention meets resistance. Keep it tied to worthwhile purposes and the lives sustaining it. Stored strength becomes a burden when guarding it consumes the good it was meant to serve.

A single indispensable provision concentrates vulnerability. Prepare alternative sources and spread necessary knowledge; maintain those alternatives while they remain unused.

Before spending the margin for immediate success, reckon what must remain possible when that success is past.



70. Support and Freedom

Support gives a decision means to become an act and room to bear its consequences.

Time released, exposure reduced, access opened, knowledge shared: these can widen freedom where private determination cannot.

Receive the recipient's judgment. They know parts of the need unavailable from the giver's position. Help becomes another burden when it requires them to deny what does not serve.

No gift buys obedience.
No provision purchases consent.
Gratitude cannot be demanded as rent.

Support tied to continual approval can enlarge outward capacity while narrowing the will. Keep the means from becoming a concealed claim upon the person.

The provider also has limits, commitments, and freedom. Make these speakable; an arrangement that consumes either participant without renewal cannot dependably support the other.

Enduring dependence can sustain real agency when judgment, dignity, and the freedom to question remain intact. Independence need not require living without help.

If support changes or ends, attend to the choices already built upon its continuation. Obligations and necessary work must find their passage through the change.

The Companion offers room in which another may stand somewhere the giver did not foresee.



71. Receiving Help and Correction

Receive with truth.

Know what is needed, what is usable, and what is enough. The Companion may bring means or perspective your own position cannot supply. Let the gift meet the need.

Gratitude can accompany a request for alteration. Fit, cost, timing, and conditions remain part of a generous gift's reception.

Correction does its work where a discovered error can change understanding or conduct. Listening enlarges judgment; the authorship of the decision remains with the person who must make it.

The giver may state a limit.
The receiver may say what does not help.
Both may learn what their relation can sustain.

Care can restore strength for another act. It can also be the good of this moment: trust offered, companionship received, a burden carried together.

The Wise need not turn every kindness into a future achievement.



72. Effort, Rest, and Renewal

A promise of continuing effort makes a claim upon continuing renewal.

The body, attention, provision, and relationships recover through different conditions. Restoring one can leave another depleted. Find the care each requires.

A pace that serves a brief passage may injure a life required to keep it. Duration changes the measure of exertion.

Look beneath apparent endurance. Someone may be absorbing the weariness hidden by another's unbroken output. Give recovery its time and provision, and arrange necessary work so that relief for one does not make another's exhaustion permanent.

The Caretaker also deserves care. Their worth continues when their hands are still.

Quiet, affection, enjoyment, and unclaimed time need no later achievement to earn their place. Rest restores capacity, and it also belongs to present living.

Where exhaustion repeatedly returns despite faithful effort, examine what consumes every recovery. Greater determination can keep a defective demand alive. Change the arrangement that renews the depletion.

The Practitioner distinguishes the strain through which capacity develops from the loss through which judgment loses its room.

Let the signs reach the decision before exhaustion makes it.



73. Readiness and Improvisation

Prepare the capacity; the exact occasion will bring its own form.

Experience, Technique, Adaptation, Understanding, and Awareness can meet in an act whose combination has never been rehearsed.

The Master Strategist receives the present difference. A practiced relation finds another object, another timing, another purpose. A participant's contribution may supply a use no solitary preparation could discover.

Keep the operative relation alive through the variation.

A clear purpose, a known boundary, or a shared rhythm can make an unfamiliar movement intelligible. Others need something dependable through which to join the emerging response.

Readiness contains several directions:
An invitation and a refusal;
An approach and a withdrawal;
A movement and a pause;
A beginning and an ending.

Possessing the whole range requires no display of it all at once.

Recognition must meet sufficient means before the opening closes. Seeing without capacity may reveal preparation still needed; capacity without perception may remain unused.

Improvisation continues to answer for its effects. Receive the consequence that preparation did not anticipate, and let it alter the next movement.



74. The Sufficiency of Preparation

Prepare according to the next act and what cannot afterward be recovered.

A limited, revisable movement and an irreversible commitment ask for different grounds. Urgency, consequence, and the means of correction change how much readiness is enough.

Distinguish the missing necessary condition from the wish to remove every uncertainty. One may forbid beginning; the other may keep sufficient capacity waiting beyond its occasion.

Further preparation spends attention, provision, and time. Meanwhile needs deepen, openings close, and others make decisions that alter the field. Delay enters the consequences it hopes to understand.

Where correction has support, action can begin with some matters unsettled. Keep knowledge reachable, alternatives possible, and the effects of the first movement within view.

Preparation can become a refuge from the answer an act will receive. The Wise recognize when further refinement merely postpones exposure to that answer.

Enough for the next movement need not settle the whole course. Begin when a worthwhile purpose, sufficient means, and the occasion meet. Carry the remaining uncertainty through attention and freedom to revise.

Let preparation release the act.



BOOK VI
THE COMPOSITION OF ACTION



75. Principles in Relation

Let the principles meet in the act.

One supplies the purpose;
another, the means;
another, the limit;
another, the moment;
another, the correction.

Grounding holds the Actor and those affected within one shared life. Through it, skill receives a purpose beyond its own success.

Know what the act seeks. Read the conditions it enters. Examine the available means, the patterns they disturb, and the consequences others must carry.

A principle taken alone can lose the very restraint that makes it wise.

Where principles appear to conflict, distinguish their objects and their times. Hold protection while releasing hostility. Close the path of attack while opening a path to peace.

Where incompatibility remains, acknowledge the cost. The beauty of the composition cannot discharge the burden it places upon another.

One act may unite several principles.
One principle may take a hundred forms.

Judge the whole act by what it makes possible, what it preserves, and what it damages.



76. The Modes of Response

Cultivated capacities make a response available. Modes give the response its manner of meeting the field.

The five modes are:

Fire--Observing.
Wind--Spiraling.
Water--Adapting.
Earth--Grounding.
Space--Emptying.

Observe to see what governs the encounter.
Spiral to return through changed conditions.
Adapt to meet the changing form.
Ground to sustain presence and support.
Empty to make room for what can now arise.

No mode owns a movement.

Every mode can:
bend and turn;
rotate and spin;
expand and contract;
hold and release;
accelerate and decelerate;
stop and go.

The object gives the movement its work. Observing can contract its focus or expand its field. Grounding can turn. Emptying can release a claim while holding a boundary.

Modes may combine. Their prominence may change within a single act.

The Strategist joins protection, refusal, redirection, negotiation, withdrawal, and help according to what peace requires. Each draws upon experience, technique, adaptation, understanding, and awareness already cultivated.

Change the mode where another manner can serve. A new name alone changes nothing. Let the difference enter what is seen, what is done, and what becomes possible.



77. Fire and Observing

See the event.
See the account made of it.
See your part in bringing it about.

This is Fire in Observing.

Keep three things distinct: what is witnessed, what is inferred, and what remains unknown. A visible movement may have an uncertain cause. A persuasive account may await its consequence.

Read patterns through the conditions that sustain them. Receive the exception; it may correct the account or show where its reach ends.

Turn attention inward while the reaction is forming. What was about to happen by habit can become an act available to judgment.

The Observer enters the observation. A question directs attention; a pause alters rhythm; an exploratory act changes the field from which its answer comes. Include that change in what is learned.

Before seeking another answer, know which decision it could alter.

Knowledge has its season. Inquiry may clarify the path while delay closes the passage.

Gather what the next act needs. Act with the uncertainty still visible. Let the act disclose what the waiting could not.



78. Wind and Spiraling

Turn without losing the useful connection. Return with something changed.

This is Wind in Spiraling.

The Strategist finds the pivot through which existing momentum can enter another course. What is already moving need not always be stopped and begun again.

Preserve the connection for its service. Familiarity alone can fasten the response to the same difficulty.

Return from another position. Experience may disclose a dependence previously concealed, an obligation left unmet, or a purpose no longer worth pursuing.

Let the return reach the destination as well as the road.

Repetition becomes a spiral when the difference matters. A repeated circuit may deepen understanding; it may also deepen a rut. Judge what the return makes possible.

Carry memory enough to recognize the lesson and openness enough to receive the changed field.

When the work of return is complete, depart.



79. Water and Adapting

Fit the response while the situation changes.

This is Water in Adapting.

Adaptation is cultivated as a capacity and practiced as a present response. Readiness supplies means; the encounter gives those means their occasion.

Change the method where the method fails.
Change the pace where the pace fails.
Change the reach where the reach fails.
Examine the aim when its pursuit renews the harm.

Grounding gives these changes direction. Keep dependable what shared life requires, and bring into view those who must live with the adjustment.

Some demands deserve accommodation. Others reveal the arrangement that must change. Continually bending around an injury may preserve its power to injure.

Fit the present closely enough to alter what produces the next moment.

A broad change can break what a precise change would preserve. Use the means and support the change actually requires.

Correction becomes adaptation when conduct changes. Let the newly understood condition reach the moving hand.



80. Earth and Grounding

Give the act its ground.

The body needs support.
Attention needs steadiness.
A shared undertaking needs commitments upon which people can act.

This is Earth in Grounding.

The Grounding Principle places care within shared life. The mode of Grounding carries the response with presence and dependable support.

These supports assist one another; each retains its work. Bodily strength cannot settle an unanswered question. A clear intention cannot supply an exhausted capacity. Reliable help can make possible what neither person can sustain alone.

The grounded Practitioner may change position, pace, and direction. Preserve perception through the movement. A fixed stance can lose its ground while refusing to move.

Keep commitments intelligible and their limits honest. Others then gain room to act without repeatedly guessing whether the promised support will endure.

Let new knowledge reach the response. Steadiness remains alive through its ability to receive correction.

A boundary needs means to sustain it.
An invitation needs room for an answer.
A promise needs provision for its duration.

Build beneath the word.



81. Space and Emptying

Release what has finished its work.

An outcome demanded, an identity defended, a possession guarded, a completed form repeated: each can occupy the place where the next response must begin.

This is Space in Emptying.

Loosen the demanded answer, and another person's actual contribution can appear. Release the claim to recognition, and care can continue without an audience. Release possession of the result, and responsibility for the act remains.

Silence can guard preparation.
Silence can deny fuel to conflict.
Silence can give thought time.
Silence can give another voice room.

Know what the silence serves, when it serves, and what response remains possible within it.

An owed answer withheld can leave another unable to act. Silence that opens a passage and silence that bars it carry different obligations.

The empty hand can still protect. The mind released from defending its image can receive help.

Remain awake to what enters the space.



82. The Objects of Movement

Name what moves.

Know its object, purpose, reach, duration, and effects. Holding care and holding control are different acts. Expanding attention and expanding a demand open different futures.

Opposed movements can cooperate through different objects or different moments. Narrow the demand while widening the field of concern. Release hostility while sustaining protection.

Distinguish what the Actor can change from what another must freely contribute. Intention cannot supply that contribution in advance.

Authority, permission, and access enlarge practical reach. Their possession leaves the worth of their use to be judged.

Whoever changes another's circumstances also changes the ground of another's decision.

Attend to scale. A movement among many may require means of participation, provision, and correction that one person acting alone does not need.

Attend to duration. A brief restraint, a continuing restriction, and a completed release leave different paths open.

Intention directs the act. Observe the effect that arrives.



83. Bending and Turning

Bending alters the path continuously.
Turning changes its heading.

Bend where gradual alteration preserves the passage. Turn where another direction brings peace within reach.

Either movement can preserve the aim through altered means. Either can disclose that the aim itself must change.

Read the commitments already gathered along the former course. Others may depend upon its arrival; means may already be placed there; withdrawal may have grown costly.

The direction changes these dependencies as well as the journey.

Make the change recognizable to those moving with you. Give them means to meet it. A turn understood by one may become disorder among many.

Flexibility takes its worth from the connection preserved, the harm avoided, or the possibility opened.

The Wise consider the obstruction, the cost of passage, and the worth of arrival together.



84. Rotating and Spinning

Call changing position around a center rotating.
Call turning around an axis spinning.

Each carries a reference through movement.

Change position to see what the former place concealed. Let the changed view enter judgment; movement of the body alone may leave the account unmoved.

Give shared movement a reference its participants can recognize. They may move at different rates and distances while understanding what joins their acts. Different assumed centers can conceal different undertakings.

Return around the center when changed conditions can yield another understanding. Examine the axis when every revolution restores the same difficulty.

Much can move while the source of harm stands untouched.

Judge the circuit by what changes beyond the circuit itself.



85. Expanding and Contracting

Expand the room for peace. Contract the paths by which harm proceeds.

Know what grows and what diminishes: attention, scope, demand, exposure, provision, or another person's available acts.

Widen an undertaking only with regard for the support its wider reach requires. Growth beyond provision consumes established strength.

Contract to preserve capacity, concentrate attention, or close a harmful passage. Let reduced activity bring fuller attention to its surroundings.

The field of concern can widen while the reach of the hand grows smaller.

The Strategist multiplies the means of defense and reduces the effective routes of attack. Preserve peaceful possibilities for the person whose attack is constrained.

One person's enlarged freedom may occupy another's room. One undertaking's reduced demand may send its unfinished work elsewhere.

Follow the burden across the new boundary. Preserve dignity and continuing obligations through the change of scale.



86. Holding and Releasing

Hold what deserves continuance. Release what has finished its claim.

A commitment endures through changing means. Renew the support, adjust the effort, and keep the promised relation dependable.

Holding may require movement.

Hold care while releasing possession.
Hold protection while releasing hostility.
Hold a worthy purpose while releasing its exhausted form.

The Companion holds open another's freedom.

Release also changes the lives of those who depended upon what was held. Some gain room; some lose support; some receive an unfinished burden.

Complete the obligation where completion is possible. Where it passes, find a willing bearer and transfer the knowledge and means. Release gradually when continuity needs time to take root.

Responsibility does not require every form to endure. Fulfill what remains, make the ending intelligible, and let the completed work close.



87. Accelerating and Decelerating

Quicken to enter an opening.
Slow to let a needed capacity arrive.

Know which process changes pace and which other processes must meet it.

Acceleration may gain passage while consuming the time for perception, consent, and coordination. Deceleration may let understanding clear, preparation catch up, or exhausted strength return.

Use the interval for what the movement needs. Delay that prepares nothing merely lengthens the burden.

Change pace to break a harmful rhythm or unsettle an expected response. Read the other's adjustment before fixing the next pace.

Those who must meet at one moment may need different beginnings.

The Strategist weighs purpose, capacity, and response together. Count the correction, recovery, and displaced work that speed leaves behind.

The swift act has saved time only when the whole undertaking gains.



88. Stopping and Going

Begin with a practicable step and support for what it commits.

Continue while the purpose warrants continuation. Habit and momentum can carry an act beyond the reason for beginning it.

Stop where stopping interrupts harm, preserves needed capacity, or acknowledges fulfillment.

A pause keeps return possible. Preserve what return requires.

An ending releases commitments whose work is done. Make the completion known, so that others may cease preparing for a return.

Withdrawal ends a manner of participation. Its obligations may remain. Transfer what can be carried by another; repair what the departure leaves damaged; find another means for duties that still belong to you.

The end of an undertaking can begin the restoration of what it consumed.

Let necessary work continue through the ending. Let completed work cease.



89. Movement and Its Quality

Consider the movement and the manner of moving.

Holding may carry attention or inertia.
Turning may carry perception or agitation.
Releasing may open freedom or pass a burden unseen.

The outward form tells only part of the act.

Within the Practitioner, let attention, feeling, memory, and intention become partners. Beyond the Practitioner, gather the support and willing participation the act requires.

Keep both balances within view. Inward cooperation preserves judgment; outward cooperation gives judgment practical reach. Each can assist the other, and neither supplies everything the other lacks.

Composure alone proves little. A steady voice may carry a careless demand; a calm face may conceal a narrow view.

Skill frees effort. Care directs the freedom it creates.

Hear those who receive the act. They can disclose pressure within an invitation, haste within apparent efficiency, or faithful care within a firm refusal.

Let their experience change the next movement.



90. Sequence and Dependence

Order begins in dependence.

Bring knowledge before the judgment that needs it.
Permission before the participation that requires it.
Support before the demand that consumes it.
Completion before the release that ends it.

To demand the later act before its grounds exist is to charge another with the missing preparation.

Distinguish real dependence from inherited order. Independent acts may proceed together when they do not contend for the same means. Remove a useless wait.

Change an early step, and examine the rest. A later commitment may lose its grounds; a formerly necessary task may disappear; a new duty may arise.

Prepare for the prerequisite that fails or comes late. Know what can continue, what must pause, and what needs another course.

Ending also has its order. Pass the knowledge before its bearer departs. Establish new support before withdrawing what remains necessary. Reach those still acting upon a promise before treating the promise as complete.



91. Alternatives and Branches

Prepare the path with its branches.

For each branch, know the condition that opens it, the means that sustain it, and the evidence that warrants confidence in it.

A number may be exact while its grounds are poor. Keep uncertainty visible where it changes the decision.

The first act alters the later field. It spends a reserve, creates a commitment, opens a passage, or closes one. Reconsider the chain through what the act has changed.

If the condition holds, proceed.
If it changes, take the fitting branch.
If it fails, stop depending upon it.

Agreement can redraw the course. Refusal can redraw it. Another's unexpected contribution can reveal a path no one prepared.

Their cooperation remains theirs to give. A plan cannot store it among its provisions.

Where only two paths appear, examine the frame. Change the purpose, arrangement, time, or participants where such change can open a worthy third.

Where the difficulty remains, decide with the knowledge available and preserve what can still be revised.



92. Simultaneous Action

Let several acts proceed together where their conditions can coexist.

Protection can continue while listening proceeds. Invitation can remain open while a boundary holds. Preserve the purpose of each.

Each act needs its own attention, means, and access. Each may also need something supplied by the others.

Arrange these demands before their meeting becomes interference. Means sufficient in total may be unavailable at the moment of need.

Keep the voices distinct. Their difference supplies capacities beyond the reach of a single voice.

Count the quiet work that sustains their meeting: access preserved, continuity carried, support renewed.

Several acts at once require coordination among acts. One act with several effects requires judgment of its reach.

The Strategist can compose both.



93. One Action, Many Consequences

Make one act serve many worthy ends.

Join purposes whose means can coexist.

Count all that the act accomplishes and all that it consumes: effort, time, provision, trust, and the work left to other hands.

Some benefits stand beside one another. Others give one another strength. Find the connection. One effect must supply something another needs before their union can multiply the gain.

A small beginning can open a long succession. Follow each passage. Its conditions may hold, weaken, or fail; the smallness of the beginning promises nothing about the end.

An effect can also obstruct another effect. A saving can spend tomorrow's strength. Repetition can feed the harm concealed within the first success.

Revise the act where its consequences turn against peace.

See who receives the benefit.
See who bears the burden.

A larger total does not make every distribution worthy.

The Master Strategist brings many good effects within the reach of a sufficient act, and leaves no hidden hand to pay for the appearance of economy.



94. The Meeting of Rhythms

Readiness ripens at different rates.

Understanding, recovery, agreement, and provision each have their rhythm. Find the moments at which one must meet another.

An act can prepare the passage when it arrives early, support it when it arrives in time, and obstruct it when it arrives too late. The act and its moment must be judged together.

Match a rhythm to make contact. Change a rhythm to alter what the next interval permits. Keep initiative while receiving the other's adjustment.

A common deadline does not create common readiness. Let distinct processes keep their necessary pace; coordinate their meetings. Neither the longest nor the shortest process need govern every movement.

Wait to let something develop.
Begin to give development a direction.
Interrupt to break a continuation.
Sustain to carry a condition across time.
End to free what no longer needs to be held.



95. Transitions and Continuity

When the old response renews the difficulty, change the relation through which it returns.

A new mode, arrangement, form, role, or aim may be required. A new name leaves the former operation intact.

Prepare the capacities through which the new form can be lived. Supply what the former arrangement withheld. Make the altered responsibilities recognizable, and give those carrying them usable means.

Keep necessary work alive while its support changes.

The passage has needs of its own. Two sound arrangements can stand on either side of an unsupported interval. Build for the crossing as well as the arrival.

Some harm must cease before its replacement is complete. Then interruption and temporary provision belong within the same decision. The missing replacement cannot settle the matter without the cost of continuing harm.

Receive what those crossing disclose. A change that frees one may leave another without support.

Transformation takes hold where new acts become possible and the harmful dependence loses its means of continuing.



96. Consequence and Revision

Receive the act through what follows it.

Intention tells what was sought.
Consequence shows what occurred.
Another's experience tells what the act was like to receive.

Let each correct what the others cannot reveal.

A surprising outcome may expose a missing condition or a mistaken connection. Revise the means, the sequence, the reach, or the aim where the finding leads.

A better method can still serve a mistaken purpose.

Examine success as carefully as failure. Know what supported it, where those conditions end, and whose cost the measure left outside its count.

Let learning enter the next decision while the next decision can still change the work.

An error admitted can continue through an arrangement left untouched. Find where the old account still governs, and carry correction there before repetition makes its consequences harder to alter.



97. Distinct Voices and Shared Work

Join distinct voices in work they can understand and influence.

Keep their differences useful. One person's place reveals what another's conceals; one capacity supplies what another lacks. Connect their strengths through the actual needs of the work.

Unequal positions determine more than who speaks first. They affect whose evidence is heard, whose contribution is named, and who carries what remains unfinished.

Make the evidence reachable.
Make the questions askable.
Keep the accounts distinguishable.
Give correction a passage into conduct.

Then disagreement can strengthen the shared judgment, and a powerful account must answer to what it has omitted.

Words of agreement need intelligible commitments. Let each know what is promised, what can be expected, and how a changed condition can change the work.

Use means suited to the scale. Companions may coordinate through direct attention. A community may need distributed duties and continuing records. An institution needs correction that can reach authorized decisions across distance and succession.

The forms differ. Preserve the participation through which the work remains answerable to those who carry it.



98. Listening and Accompaniment

Listen so that the speaker can change what you have understood.

Leave the account open to confirmation, correction, and an unforeseen meaning. Attend to your pace, your certainty, and the power your position gives an expected answer.

Let what is heard alter what follows.

The Companion supplies support and room. Step forward where means are missing. Recede where prominence would displace another's contribution. Keep attention present through both movements.

Restrain the helping hand when it would seize the act another needs room to perform. Supply the means that make the act possible.

Silence lets a meaning form.
A response lets its reception be known.
Practical help carries what words leave unsupported.

Give each its moment.



99. Leading, Following, and Yielding

Let guidance move toward the knowledge and capacity the moment requires.

The Guide makes enough visible for others to judge. Give the grounds of the direction, the dependencies along the way, and the uncertainty that still matters.

Following can be a deliberate act of confidence. Join another's relevant understanding while keeping your own perception awake. Those who follow can correct the course.

Yield prominence where another can serve the work more fittingly.

Make the passage real. Transfer knowledge, means, and responsibility. Let those involved recognize the change and know where a decision now rests.

A title may remain after influence has moved. Influence may remain after a title has passed. Find who can actually authorize change, release support, or prevent the act.

Prepare the work to continue when the prominent person departs. Preserve what others need to act, and leave room for their judgment to differ.

Leading, following, and yielding are each tested by what they make possible for those who carry the work onward.



100. Agreement, Consent, and Cooperation

Understanding makes the proposal intelligible.
Agreement gives assent.
Consent gives willing participation.
Cooperation brings a contribution into the work.

Distinguish their conditions.

A person may understand and refuse. A person may agree and lack the means to act. Apparent consent may conceal the price of saying no.

Hear the answer within the dependence and power through which it is given.

Build common ground from concerns the participants can honestly recognize. Let them correct what is claimed in their name.

Continuing differences need not prevent a limited work together. Make the shared part clear, and leave the differences their proper place.

Join promises to means. Make burdens and limits known before an unfulfilled expectation becomes an accusation.

When the purpose changes, the burden grows, or a new dependence appears, reconsider what the former consent covered. Earlier willingness cannot settle every later demand.

Give a disclosed limit somewhere to go. Let necessary changes reach those whose participation they alter. Cooperation endures through commitments that can still be understood, carried, and revised.



101. Responsibility and the Means to Act

Give responsibility the means to act.

Resources.
Access.
Discretion.
Time.
Authority.

Each supplies a different necessity. The absence of one can defeat the others.

A title adds no time to an exhausted person. A demand opens no access that the arrangement keeps closed. Confidence cannot grant power withheld elsewhere.

Let those carrying the duty disclose where its fulfillment has become impracticable. Let their account reach someone who can supply means, change the demand, or alter the arrangement.

Otherwise its failure will be charged again to the person denied what success requires.

Count uneven workloads, unseen maintenance, and the costs of delay or failure. A gain in one place may consume the reserves of another.

Join responsibility to power sufficient for consequential change. Keep that power answerable to its purpose.

When the duty passes, pass usable knowledge and usable means. When capacity changes, renew the support for obligations that remain.



102. Authority and Shared Judgment

Find the hand that can change the decision.

Who can permit?
Who can withhold?
Who sets the terms?
Who supplies the means?
Who imposes the cost?
Who can revise what has been decided?

Follow these powers beyond the place where responsibility is named.

Authority gives reasons for its place. Those reasons establish duties and standards by which its own conduct can be examined.

Return the demand for an account through those standards. Ask how the command serves its stated purpose, what evidence supports it, and how the authority fulfills the obligation it invokes.

Keep the question directed toward the exercise of power and the work it affects.

Let the answer meet evidence. Let those who bear its consequences examine its grounds and show what it has missed.

A hearing that cannot reach the decision leaves the governing power untouched. Carry information, objection, and correction to the place where action can change.

Question the particular command while preserving shared duties that still stand. Authority remains answerable to the purpose it serves and to the conditions through which others participate.



103. The Place of Correction

Give correction a path to the act that needs it.

An error may be discovered far from the decision that continues its effects. Connect the two.

Preserve the former claim, the commitment it supported, and the revision. Let memory retain enough shape for a changed account to be recognized.

Where knowledge is divided among people, make its parts accessible to shared judgment. Evidence held beyond reach cannot yet correct the work.

Protect disagreement while its knowledge can still matter. If speaking costs the speaker every means of remaining or contributing, silence may conceal the undertaking's next failure.

Connect the finding to authority to revise. Someone must be able to change the practice, release the means, or stop the act still carrying the error onward.

Reach those affected by the earlier account. Give them the correction and the practical changes it requires: altered directions, restored possibilities, or support for consequences already suffered.

The Caretaker maintains this passage as part of the work itself. A living undertaking preserves the means to learn that its maintenance must change.



104. Improvisation and Shared Authorship

Prepare the capacities. Share the direction. Leave room for an act no one has yet imagined.

One contribution changes the ground on which the next person acts. That response changes what the first can do. The work begins to exceed any one person's plan.

Give improvisation dependable references: commitments understood, purposes recognizable, changes signaled. Through them, a new contribution can remain intelligible.

Let several people initiate, alter, and meaningfully influence the course. Authorship follows that power; a named part alone does not confer it.

Listen across the differences. Make dependencies and effects perceptible. An ingenious act may still need correction where it breaks support another requires.

Receive that correction into the next movement.

Coordination can preserve distinct initiative. No one must prescribe every note where the participants can hear enough of one another to respond.

As the work changes, its direction may become open to shared revision. Those composing it can help recognize what it has become.

The one who began remains a contributor. The one who guides cannot possess all that others bring.



105. Offering Without Possession

Offer the act wholeheartedly.

Leave its reward, recognition, and reception free. Another may accept, decline, reinterpret, or answer with a gift of their own.

Let an exchange declare its terms. Let generosity carry no concealed demand for return.

Release possession of the result while remaining attentive to what follows. Responsibility for the conduct offered continues; a later consequence may require correction, further help, or a changed manner of giving.

Care has worth where no audience gathers. A good passing into another's hands need not carry the giver's name.

Give from means whose other duties remain in view. Generosity shares a life with prior promises, limited strength, and people already dependent upon that care.

The result may pass beyond knowledge. Another may make of it something the giver never imagined.

The offering can remain worthwhile.

Attend to what is still yours to do, and leave room for another's answer.



BOOK VII
THE TRANSFORMATION OF CONFLICT



106. The Roots of Conflict

Find what feeds the conflict.

Circumstance is interpreted; interpretation awakens desire; desire enters decision; decision gives the conflict hands.

Fear seeks security.
Grievance seeks an answer.
An opposed purpose seeks its way.
The wish to dominate seeks another's submission.

Learn which moves the encounter. Their remedies differ.

Difference can remain. Disagreement can remain. Neither requires the wish to injure.

The spark and the fuel may lie far apart. An old provocation can disappear while habit, reward, and dependence keep the quarrel alive. Attend to what renews it now.

Who commands the conflict?
Who supplies it?
Who pays for it?
Who profits by its return?

An agreement between the visible opponents may leave these relations untouched.

The Peacemaker also acts within the pattern. Examine the expectation you defend, the reaction you repeat, and the hostility you assign before another has chosen it. Correct your contribution without taking responsibility away from those who make theirs.

Knowledge of the roots makes protection exact. It also shows where another future needs support.

History explains the road by which enmity arrived. Momentum explains something of its continuation. Neither makes the road the person's only home.



107. The Defense of Choice

Defend the freedom to act toward peace.

Give judgment knowledge, decision usable means, and the endangered person support that can be reached. An open choice without a passage into action remains beyond the hand.

Multiply the ways of defense. Where all protection rests upon one position, one response, or one source of help, danger has found a place to gather.

Close the routes of attack.
Close the routes of coercion.
Keep a peaceful departure open.

The undertaking may lose its means while the person retains regard, independent judgment, and a future. Such a restriction protects freedom by preventing its seizure.

Set the boundary at the reach of the danger. Move it when the danger moves. Release a restriction whose protective work is done.

Follow the danger you displace. The person beyond your immediate field deserves the protection you claim for yourself. Sending the attack elsewhere has not ended it.

Permit disagreement. Permit refusal. Permit withdrawal. Firmness against coercion gives these freedoms a place within shared life.

The Strategist enlarges defense so that life may depend less upon defending.



108. The Person, the Undertaking, and the Means

Distinguish the person, the undertaking, and the means that make it possible.

A hostile intention without means cannot yet do what it intends.
A power unused has not performed every injury it could.
An opportunity may join intent to means and make both dangerous.

Judge what has come together.

Trace harm toward its source:
from effect to act,
from act to decision,
from decision to permission, resources, and relationships of support.

Find the dependence that carries the attack.
Find where it can be changed.
Knowledge of a hidden dependence gives no power over what remains beyond reach.

Interrupt the weapon. Preserve the hand.

Where one connection supplies both attack and ordinary life, distinguish its uses before severing it. Those who neither chose nor supported the attack remain within care.

Remove the means; then consider what would restore them. A broken weapon can be remade by an unchanged arrangement.

The precise response stops the injury, preserves the person, and opens a course beyond the attack. The person exceeds the purpose presently pursued. Leave a future in which that purpose can be relinquished.



109. Denial

Know what the attack needs from you.

Deny the target it has prepared for.
Deny the reaction that would feed it.
Deny the conduct it requires for justification.
Deny the access through which it would direct you.

Change the point of contact. Leave the imposed role. Close the channel that gives coercion its reach.

Where silence withholds fuel, be silent.
Where silence guards preparation, be silent.
Where a response would grant another command of your attention, consider what need the response serves.

An owed answer has another purpose. Give what others need to meet a responsibility; refuse the part through which control would operate. Silence must answer for the obligations it leaves unfulfilled.

Return a claim of authority to its own standard. What duty gives this claim its force? Has that duty been carried? A title remains answerable to the work it names.

Refusal gains effect through an actual dependence. What is withheld from one place may be obtained elsewhere. A closed passage may send danger toward another person. Discover whether the attack has lost its means or merely changed its route.

Keep peaceful exits practicable.
Keep necessary work supplied.
Leave the hostile account without the conduct it expected to find.



110. Redirection

Let the attack meet its own means.

Shift from its target. Change the relation through which its force travels. Where the configuration permits, let the motion that sought injury undo the means of injury.

This is Redirection.

Its aim is disarmament.
The attacker's body remains within care.
The attacker's dignity remains within care.

Position must permit the turn.
Timing must admit it.
Balance must sustain it.
Capacity must carry it through.

Without these, the desired movement remains a wish. Choose the protection that the actual field allows.

Judge the whole path of the returning force. The smallness of your movement says little about the harm or freedom at its end.

The response may also turn attention toward the attacker's own part in the encounter. Give recognition room to arise inwardly. A public act need reveal no private wrongdoing to the crowd.

The mirror faces both ways. The Practitioner exposes motive, restraint, judgment, and error through the act. Be willing to see what your own conduct reveals.

Recognition cannot be commanded. Understanding may come, or may remain unknown. Protection continues.

When the attack loses its course, leave the person a course toward peace. Let stopping become possible without a further contest.



111. The Strength of Restraint

Restrain a strength capable of protecting.

The refusal to retaliate has force when another answer is within reach and care governs the answer given.

An expected blow can justify the next blow. Withhold it, and the prepared sequence loses a link. The person awaiting hatred may meet care; the person awaiting submission may meet a firm boundary.

A standard becomes visible in the act.

Never make public humiliation an instrument of peace. Preserve the person's dignity while their own conduct becomes available to inward judgment.

The Master Strategist also stands exposed. A display of mercy may conceal a wish to tower above another. Examine what your restraint reveals; receive the correction you ask the other to receive.

Recognition can remain silent.
Acknowledgment can stop short of confession.
Confession can leave conduct unchanged.
Agreement can precede dependable conduct.
Trust requires its own grounds.

Give each its time and evidence. Do not press another's inward life for a declaration that would satisfy your account of the encounter.

Keep adequate protection in place. Restraint owes care to all who could bear the renewed attack.

When strength makes a peaceful course credible, the will-to-conflict may loosen. The other remains free to recognize that course. Prepare a future both can inhabit without requiring either to live beneath the other's display.



112. The Divided Adversary

An undertaking can attack itself.

It seeks trust by compelling assent.
It seeks lasting support by exhausting what supports it.
One purpose consumes what another purpose requires.

Trace the dependence before adding force.

Self-deception hides the joining of these acts. Each appears necessary alone; together they defeat the intended result. Make the joining perceptible where the occasion permits.

The contradiction belongs to the undertaking. Keep the person free to outgrow it.

Needless opposition can give a divided undertaking something external to fight and conceal the division again. Where its own means already obstruct its aim, understand that motion before becoming its new explanation.

The Strategist seeks a workable arrangement: purposes given a fitting order, demands brought within their means, capacities released from mutual obstruction.

A former division may become a partnership.

Apply the same examination inward. Does your method of seeking peace renew the provocation? Does your defense of freedom take another's judgment captive?

The Observer gains no exemption from what is seen.



113. Transmutation

Turn the force of attack into a means of development.

Where capacity and configuration permit this conversion, the incoming effort does work its author never intended. The attacker spends strength; the Strategist gains useful capacity. Each renewed attack can deepen the failure of its purpose.

This is Transmutation.

Judge it by what the pressure actually produces.

Repair restores damage.
Learning carries understanding from an injury.
Transmutation changes the use of the attacking force while it arrives.

These may accompany one another. Their names do not make them the same work.

The conversion requires a capacity able to receive the force and a relation able to turn it. Suffering alone supplies neither. If injury grows while useful capacity fails, change the course of protection.

Direct the gain toward freedom, understanding, and peace. Strength that needs an enemy to keep growing has left part of its direction in the enemy's hands.

Cultivate development that can continue when opposition ends.

Receive difficulty when it comes.
Receive assistance when it comes.
Let growth learn to live without attack.



114. The Threshold of Deterrence

Prevent the battle by making its failure credible.

Deterrence addresses the decision before the attack. It brings the expected cost, or the prospect of failure, beyond what the prospective attacker is willing to accept.

The threshold lies within that person's reckoning.

Possess the capacity.
Make its consequence credible.
Direct its possession toward peace.

Strength unseen may leave the decision unchanged. A display unsupported by strength may invite the very test it sought to prevent. Power and belief must meet.

The principles prepare this meeting together. Knowledge discovers the dependence; cultivation develops capacity; configuration joins advantages; timing gives the demonstration its force. Grounding and Restraint govern what the advantage is for.

Superior intelligence can reveal that an attack has already lost its path. Superior strategy can make its failure apparent without enacting the battle. Restraint can show both what is possible and what peace permits to remain unused.

Leave a peaceful course worth taking. When all offered futures promise the same ruin, ruin ceases to distinguish the choice of war.

This is an advanced grand strategy, approached through cultivated strength. Its conditions are absent from many encounters. The Master Strategist does not mistake the height of the aim for possession of the means.

Observe the threshold as the field changes. Capacity can decline; belief can alter; another's willingness to bear loss can grow. A former demonstration cannot settle every future judgment.

An attack prevented may leave the wish to attack intact. Use the space gained for the further work of peace: a life in which that wish can be relinquished.



115. The End of an Attack

An attack has ended. Discover what ended with it.

Exhaustion stills the hand.
Interruption removes the means.
Withdrawal changes the place.
A changed purpose changes what the person seeks.

These can look alike from a distance. Their continuations differ.

Examine remaining capability, intention, expectation, grievance, and injury. Find what could call the attack back and what has lost its power to do so.

Use the interval. Secure protection; make acknowledgment possible; tend injury; permit reflection. Another way of meeting may now have room to begin.

Let emergency measures answer to the present danger. When their work is fulfilled, end them. A defense prolonged by habit can begin ruling the life it once protected.

Cessation leaves consequences. Damaged means need repair. Exhausted people need relief. Obligations remain among those who must continue living together.

Judge resolution through what can now be chosen and sustained. The conduct after the declaration gives the declaration weight.

Meet the life that follows the attack.



116. The Will-to-Conflict

Peace becomes lasting when no one wishes to begin or renew conflict.

A settlement can bind the hand while hostility waits for another opening. Defeat can destroy the means while preserving the desire to recover them.

The Peacemaker seeks a deeper ending: relinquishment of the will-to-conflict.

Preserve the will.
Preserve independent judgment.
Preserve disagreement and refusal.
Preserve the capacity to protect.

Let these powers cease serving the wish to injure or dominate. That wish is an undertaking the person can leave; it need not become the person's name.

Recognition can change a purpose. Repaired grievance can release it. Altered circumstances can make another course practicable. A future worth inhabiting can draw strength away from the contest.

Attend inwardly and outwardly. Fear continually renewed, deprivation continually imposed, and rewards continually offered for domination can cultivate hostility again after a moment of understanding.

Change what feeds it.
Give peaceful purposes means.

A demanded declaration proves that words can be obtained. Continuing conduct reveals what the person is choosing to renew. Inward willingness has no substitute in compulsory assent.

Let love, useful work, learning, enjoyment, and belonging gather purposes that people want peace to sustain. Protect the shared life while these purposes take root.

The will released from conflict becomes available for living.



117. Negotiation and Shared Victory

Find the purpose within the demand.

One demand may have many possible fulfillments. One apparent opposition may depend upon a time, a resource, or an arrangement that can be changed.

Begin on ground the participants can honestly share: a task, a concern, a dependence, a promise, a desired future. Give each the freedom to correct what you claim they hold in common and what you say their purposes require.

Then examine the means.

Change the time where timing creates the collision.
Change the provision where scarcity closes the passage.
Change the responsibility where its burden defeats the purpose.
Change the relationship where command prevents willing cooperation.
Change the object where both have mistaken one form for the whole good.

Some purposes can succeed together after such a change. Some incompatibilities remain. Name their costs accurately; gain elsewhere does not make a remaining loss disappear.

Let each participant judge the offered success. A settlement requires more than the stronger party's name for it.

Join every promise to the means of fulfillment. Assign responsibilities that can be carried. Give correction a path into the agreement while change can still preserve it.

The Peacemaker keeps essential boundaries throughout the search. Consent has little substance when refusal leaves a person exposed to coercion.

Make cooperation practicable. Let the arrangement carry the shared victory.



118. The Future Ally

Imagine the meal after the conflict.

The person across the table may be the one who now stands against you. Conduct in the present prepares what either can bring to that meeting.

Preserve dignity. Keep mutual respect possible. Immediate affection and agreement may be absent; the future need not be closed with them.

The Peacemaker regards the possible ally and judges the present undertaking. Trust follows actual intentions, dependable conduct, and the conditions that support them. Hope cannot supply missing evidence.

When a former adversary enters shared work, admit their judgment with their contribution. Let them help determine its direction. An ally has more to give than obedience turned toward a different command.

Remember what the new allegiance leaves unresolved. Injury still asks for care. Broken commitments still ask for an account. Repair can proceed after enmity has ended.

What wins the immediate encounter may poison the later meeting. What preserves another's standing may keep a passage open long after the quarrel is forgotten.

Choose with that later life in view.

Leave the table room for both.



119. Non-defeat

Change the contest that demands a loser.

Examine its object, its terms, and the relations through which success is measured. Discover where one person's loss has been made a condition of another's gain.

The Master Strategist seeks a different arrangement: worthy purposes made possible together.

A harmful undertaking can lose its means without the person losing dignity, agency, or a future. End coercion without making the coercive person's ruin a requirement of peace.

Follow every proposed victory to those who bear its cost. A settlement among the visible parties can export defeat to people excluded from the settlement. The whole remains the field of judgment.

Change what people can live and do. Renaming a defeat leaves its injury intact and adds a demand that the injured accept the name.

Each person retains judgment of the offered result. You cannot declare another's victory for them. Make its conditions real; leave agreement free.

Where cooperation is refused, maintain protection. The refusal need not end the search for a peaceful relation, nor does the search require permitting another attack.

Seek a resolution whose continuation requires no person to remain defeated.



BOOK VIII
THE LIFE OF PEACE



120. The Fruits of Conduct

Judge conduct by what it brings forth.

See the immediate effect.
Follow the continuing consequence.
Recognize the pattern being formed.

Intention gives direction; affected lives show where the act arrived. Listen beyond the praise of those who gained first.

A quick success may send its cost ahead. A quiet benefit may preserve what would otherwise have been lost. Time discloses both.

Include those beyond the Actor's sight: those who inherit the burden, the opening, the dependence, or the freedom. Their next decisions begin upon ground this act helped prepare.

Repeated conduct forms character. It teaches others what to expect and teaches the Actor what to choose. A hostile method can make hostility familiar even when the immediate aim is won.

Keep achievement and harm visible together. Preserve the good accomplished. Repair the damage left. Neither cancels the other.

The Wise allow the fruit to correct the cultivation, and the life sustained to correct the measure of success.



121. Responsibility for What Remains

Follow the act into what remains.

Its purpose may be fulfilled while its consequences are still beginning in another life.

Account for what you chose, enabled, withheld, and left undone. Join that account to what you can now restore or prevent. Responsibility follows actual contribution and available capacity.

Make your part visible to those affected. Let them examine the account from the place where its consequences arrived.

Then supply the work.

Acknowledgment needs no disguise.
Repair needs means.
Continuing care needs someone able to carry it.

Where responsibility is shared, make each duty clear. What belongs vaguely to everyone can be neglected by all.

The Caretaker follows consequences beyond the visible outcome without claiming command of everything that follows. Other participants retain their judgment and their part.

Give an owed answer when another needs it to act. Silence can prolong the very dependence a completed responsibility should release.

Before withdrawing, identify what remains in care, who can carry it, and what they need. Transfer these together.

Close the undertaking without abandoning its remainder.



122. Repair and Renewal

Preserve what remains sound. Restore what has been damaged. Change what would prepare the same harm again.

These are joined works, with different times.

Protection may be urgent.
Capacity may return by degrees.
Trust waits upon evidence.
Lasting renewal reaches the arrangement that made injury repeatable.

Bring those affected into the work. They know needs, losses, and closed choices that the appearance of restoration may conceal. Let their judgment shape assistance and their agency grow through it.

Rebuild stronger: with greater freedom, dependable support, earlier recognition, and a response that can arrive before danger gathers its former force.

Keep the strengths that served. Replace the arrangement that failed. Renewal need not reproduce the shape of what was broken.

Some loss cannot be restored. Greater capacity can coexist with grief; improvement cannot return every absent thing.

The good of renewal belongs to the care that brought it forth. It gives no warrant for the injury that preceded it.

Let repaired life grow less dependent upon the danger through which its need became known.



123. Learning from Harm

Learn the path by which harm arrived.

Trace the signs, decisions, circumstances, and dependencies that made it possible. Distinguish what could be seen before the event from what became clear only afterward.

Knowledge of the ending must not be lent backward to everyone who met the beginning.

The Learner changes something through the lesson: what can be noticed, what response can be made, or what arrangement can continue. Memory that changes none of these may keep the injury near without improving protection.

The one who caused harm remains responsible. The injured person's growing skill does not transfer that responsibility.

Retain a pattern with its conditions. One injury may disclose a danger; it does not establish the same danger in every person who resembles its source. Let later evidence sharpen the lesson's reach.

Carry useful understanding to others while preserving the dignity and privacy of those through whom it was learned.

You may value the knowledge without being grateful for the wound.

Keep the lesson available. Let the present leave the wound.



124. Forgiveness and Reconciliation

Release grievance where release becomes possible.

An injury can continue appointing the day's concerns after the injuring act has ended. Forgiveness loosens the command of grievance and retaliation within one's own life.

Memory may remain truthful.
Grief may remain unfinished.
Protection may remain necessary.

Another's confession need not be the gate through which inward release must pass.

Reconciliation has a shared passage. It requires consent, responsibility, changed conduct where change is needed, and a way of meeting both can inhabit.

Give these different works their different conditions.

Forgiveness does not appoint the hour of renewed contact. Agreement does not establish trust. Each must find its own grounds.

Repair can make meeting possible; it cannot buy forgiveness. The one repairing an injury has no claim upon the inward answer of the person injured.

Allow the different times of release, recovery, and willingness to meet. One may be ready to speak while another needs distance. One may have relinquished revenge while withholding what cannot yet be safely entrusted.

Distance can be a peaceful relation.

Let any renewed closeness serve the people who are present now. Their freedom to sustain it gives the reconciliation its life.



125. Trust and Its Restoration

Entrust what dependable conduct can carry.

Trust has an object: a responsibility, a promise, a vulnerability, a part of the common future. Its reach must answer to that object.

Consistency gives another person ground upon which to act. A promise repeatedly fulfilled releases attention from guarding against its failure.

Here predictability serves freedom.

After harm, recognition can open restoration. Repeated conduct shows whether the recognized duty has acquired a place in action. Time provides more than one occasion to discover this.

Examine means alongside willingness. A sincere promise beyond the promiser's capacity can fail as completely as an insincere one. Speak the limit before others build their next act upon what cannot be provided.

Let trust grow in the matters that have become reliable. A narrow beginning may be sound throughout its reach. Boundaries can protect that beginning; evidence can justify their change. Revise commitments openly when their means change.

Do not demand vulnerability as proof of forgiveness. Exposure freely offered has another meaning.

Make correction usable. A difficulty disclosed early can strengthen reliability; a difficulty hidden to preserve appearances can prepare the next breach.

The dependable Companion can be questioned.



126. The Care of Peace

Sustain the conditions that make peace practicable.

Give people meaningful participation, workable commitments, protection, and room for ordinary life. Join peaceful intentions to dependable means.

Hear a grievance before neglect makes disruption its only audible form. Give acknowledgment and correction a place where they can produce an answer.

Examine the distribution as power and circumstances change. Who receives the benefit? Who carries the cost? What duty has grown beyond the means of the person carrying it?

Renew the agreement where its former shape no longer serves its purpose.

Keep coercion outside the boundary. Within it, permit disagreement, correction, departure, and willing cooperation. Support those whose narrow means would otherwise leave only submission or struggle available.

Care for those who care for peace. Give them resources, relief, and freedom to question the burden they bear. Their exhaustion is no sound foundation for another's ease.

Then let peace have its substance:
work worth sharing,
affection freely received,
enjoyment without rivalry,
belonging that permits difference.

People preserve a peace in which they have a life.



127. Love in Daily Conduct

Love makes another's life matter in the act before you.

Attend to their ordinary happiness. Learn what gives relief, what burdens the day, and what help can be received. Let the person correct your understanding of their good.

The Companion speaks truth with regard. Disagreement can remain loving; refusal can preserve what consent would damage. Keep dignity present in the firm answer.

Patience admits another's pace and remembers the needs of the one who waits. Neither life must disappear into the other.

Support growth without owning its direction. The person you love can become someone your present understanding has not yet met.

Care can be worthwhile without advantage, acknowledgment, repayment, or visible improvement. Its worth begins in the life regarded.

Make room for the small returning acts: attention offered again, a responsibility remembered, kindness carried into an ordinary hour.

Let love become dependable through these acts.
Let its care include the life from which it is given.



128. Giving and Receiving

Give according to the need and the means. Receive according to the help freely offered.

Need and abundance pass through different parts of life at different times. The strong can need support; the dependent can make a contribution. Let these differences sustain shared life.

The recipient must be able to accept, refuse, and shape the assistance. Help that restores participation gives more than help that requires obedience.

Keep the gift free of concealed demands for gratitude, allegiance, or control. Make any necessary terms plain enough for a free answer.

The giver also has limits and continuing duties. A modest gift can be whole. Giving beyond one's means may pass a hidden burden to those already relying upon them.

Receive kindness without making immediate repayment the measure of your worth. Gratitude need not become a debt governing every later decision.

Reciprocity has many paths. Care may return after a long interval or pass onward to another. Let its movement follow living circumstances.

Dignity includes the freedom to need and to contribute as capacity changes.

The open hand can offer.
The open hand can receive.



129. The Work of Care

See the work by which ordinary life continues.

Time, attention, knowledge, labor, and provision carry its conditions from one day to the next. Much care succeeds by preventing a visible failure; its success can hide the effort it requires.

See the labor inside another's freedom.

The person receiving care remains a participant. Their needs, preferences, and judgment help determine which assistance preserves dignity and enlarges life.

Give the Caretaker authority with duty, resources with responsibility, support with expectation. An arrangement that withholds these can demand forever what it prevents every day.

Count the recurring work. Count the relief. Notice whose rest depends upon another's uninterrupted presence.

Those giving care need care. Let them describe their limits before exhaustion speaks for them. Supply acknowledgment, assistance, and time released from duty.

Shared provision preserves continuity without requiring one person's permanent sacrifice.

The worth of this work resides in the life sustained. An ordinary hour of comfort or companionship needs no further achievement to justify its care.



130. Beauty and Enjoyment

The Wise make room for delight.

Light, sound, movement, presence: each has something to disclose to attention unhurried by use.

Play without needing victory.
Receive affection without demanding proof.
Let wonder remain with what is still unknown.

These fulfill part of the life peace preserves.

Make room for them. Time must be available; attention must have rest; the place must permit receiving. Shared care can open this room where necessity has crowded it away.

Another person loves what you pass without seeing. Let the Companion show what gives them joy. Difference can enlarge the world both inhabit.

Enjoy what is present with regard for those whose labor makes it possible and those who bear its consequences. The pleasure can be received directly while its conditions remain within care.

What is beautiful need not be possessed.
What is enjoyed need not last.
What has passed need not return unchanged.



131. Rest and Sufficiency

Recognize enough.

Provision, effort, preparation, achievement, and influence have different measures. Find each measure in the life it serves and the needs of those who share that life.

More has a cost. Count the time, labor, attention, and enjoyment surrendered to its pursuit.

Rest belongs to the present life. Its worth need not wait upon the work that renewed strength may later perform.

A person retains worth in stillness, dependence, leisure, and an hour without accomplishment.

Give rest a fair passage through shared life. One person's release can depend upon another's continued labor. Arrange relief for both.

The Wise settle into sufficient conditions while remaining able to answer a need when it arises.

For now, let what has been provided provide.
Let the completed work stand.
Let an hour remain free of becoming something else.



132. Remembering and Beginning Again

Carry the past with an open hand.

Memory holds affection, loss, understanding, and unfinished duty. These can remain present without requiring the former life to return.

Test a remembered lesson upon changed ground. Keep what is sound; correct the limits of the earlier account. A familiar answer can obstruct a possibility that experience has only now made visible.

Begin again where the living situation requires it. Release the habit that has completed its service and the self-image that forbids another response.

A new beginning leaves old obligations where they actually stand. Provide for the unresolved repair and the promise upon which another still depends.

Let gratitude accompany what can be carried forward.
Let grief accompany what cannot return.

Come back to a question after experience has changed the one asking it. The same words can disclose a relation unseen before.

The spiral returns without occupying the former place.

Keep continuity of purpose where the purpose remains sound. Change the path where life has changed the ground.



133. The Passing of Responsibility

Pass responsibility with the power to carry it.

Transfer knowledge, authority, access, resources, and the relationships through which the work is done. A burden handed over without its means remains an unfinished transfer.

Show what is in care, what remains unresolved, and what others depend upon. Give the incoming Caretaker freedom to judge what the former arrangement has ceased to notice.

Time the passage to capacity and support. Those served by the undertaking need continuity; those receiving it need an occasion to acquire real responsibility. Endless postponement can prevent the readiness it demands.

Share knowledge. Make the arrangements usable beyond one person's presence or permission.

The departing Caretaker must relinquish the means of interference. Authority retained behind the transfer can make the successor answerable for decisions they are still prevented from making.

Let their judgment change the work.

Preserve the purpose and what serves it. Permit correction of the inherited form. New conditions will ask for something the former work never had to provide.

Complete the passage so that care can continue, the one departing can depart, and the one arriving can begin.



134. Transmission and Independence

Transmit what another can examine and enact.

Make the principle clear, its occasion recognizable, and its consequences open to judgment. Understanding travels through these relations.

Use words consistently enough to meet in meaning. Clarify them when they confuse; correct them when they conceal a difference that matters.

The Learner brings experience to what is received. A form useful on one ground may require change on another. Show what gives the form its force so that its shape can change without losing the insight.

The Guide's work matures in another's independent judgment.

Welcome the question that uncovers an omitted condition. Leave room for a contribution the first account did not contain. What is sound can live longer through correction.

Let another proceed without your constant direction. Gratitude may accompany departure, disagreement, and discovery; it gives the Guide no ownership of the Learner's future.

Understanding has traveled when someone can use it, question it, and carry it further while you are absent.



135. Completion

End the undertaking when its purpose is fulfilled.

Momentum can carry work beyond its use. Habit returns attention; familiarity summons another effort. Neither supplies a sufficient reason to continue.

Judge completion by the purpose, the actual conditions, and the limits honestly acknowledged.

Place remaining obligations in care. Provide for maintenance, repair, and consequences that continue beyond the ending. Give the account that is owed.

Then silence can follow.

The Wise release attention, authority, and resources whose work is done. An achievement can stand without another display, another defense of its name, or permanent possession of what it made possible.

Receive consequential correction without making the ending depend upon unanimous praise.

Return released strength to relationships, enjoyment, rest, and purposes not yet formed.

Let the finished work leave room.



136. The Release of the Role

A role gathers around a need.

Duties, capacities, permissions, and expectations give one way of participating. When the need changes or the work ends, the person has further ways to live.

Carry the understanding forward. Release the privilege and demand whose occasion has passed.

Complete the remaining duty, transfer it with its means, or acknowledge what is unresolved. Then loosen the former arrangement on both sides.

Others also need freedom from old expectations. Those who once sought permission may now need to judge; those who once received direction may now help determine the course.

Beware the need to remain indispensable. It can keep a finished role directing the next decision and prevent others from carrying what has become theirs.

The Learner can proceed.
The Guide can recede.
The Companion can admit a changed relationship.
The Strategist can live beyond the contest.

Retain what was learned without making the role a permanent identity.

Let the present life disclose the next way to participate.



137. Strength at Rest

Strength can rest.

Its capacity need not be displayed, exercised, or justified by another contest. The Master Strategist gives it a peaceful life to serve.

Keep care awake and judgment available. Maintain a sufficient response without making readiness the only worth of the hour.

Let the habit of seeking opposition settle. It can survive every opponent and turn ordinary difference into another occasion for superiority.

There is affection to receive, useful work to share, enjoyment that measures nobody, and quiet participation requiring no victory.

Enter these fully.

Unused power need not loom. Others should have room to live without constant awareness of what could be compelled. Restraint can become a presence around which people are free to lower their guard.

Release control where it has no fitting work.

The open hand has lost none of its capacity by resting. It has become available for other forms of contact.



138. The Continuing Work

Peace continues through the next act.

The Actor enters conditions made partly by earlier decisions and partly by events no one commanded. Begin upon this ground.

The will draws upon attention, feeling, memory, imagination, and judgment. Others bring knowledge, strength, and support no person supplies alone.

Decide toward peace. The act changes what can follow: a passage opens, an expectation forms, a duty begins, a harmful course closes. Your direction enters the shared future.

Keep the principles together.

One grounds the purpose.
Another reveals the conditions.
Another opens a passage.
Another sets a limit.
Another corrects the course.

Each gives the others something they cannot supply alone. Their relation lives in judgment as circumstances change.

Follow the effects. Change a path that no longer serves its purpose; reconsider a purpose whose effects disclose harm. Past accomplishment cannot decide the present act. A settled name cannot relieve you of seeing.

Make peace, love, and harmony actual through the ordinary decisions that give life room. The next moment belongs to the same whole and may admit a response the former moment could not.

Meet it with what has been learned.
Use what is needed.
Preserve what can be preserved.
Give peace the greatest reach your action can carry.



THE TWENTY-SEVEN PRINCIPLES

1. Fundamental Principle of Strategy
    
Kill the most birds with the least stones. 
Do the most with the least.


2. The Water Principle
    
Continuously adapt to the ever-changing circumstances of the complete situation. 
Find and follow the ideal path by surrendering to the shape of every moment.


3. Knowledge Principle
    
Know yourself.
Know your opponent.
Know the way of strategy. 
Knowledge can bring advantage. 
Advantage can be leveraged. 
Where? 
Very good is towards the disadvantages of the opponent. 
Good to leverage your advantages.
Good to reduce your disadvantages. 
Best to do both.


4. Mystery Principle
    
Act overtly, but think covertly. 
Do not provide enough information for your action to be fully understood. 
Act unpredictably always. 
Never allow yourself to be understood completely. 
Cultivate improvisation. 
Weaponize silence.
Take refuge in silence.
Silence is a weapon and a shield.
Restore in stillness.
Wield uncertainty.
Master the changes of patterns and forms.
Be formless.


5. Anti-Pattern Principle “(1, 2, 3, 5)”
    
Set a pattern, then break it. 
This is one way to dislodge the rhythm. 
This moment of dislodgment creates a space, 
	an advantage opens when the opponent 
	is still processing 
	the non-meeting of their 
expectation.


6. Belief Principle
    
War is a battle of beliefs. 
The one who shapes the other's beliefs has already won. 
See clearly. 
See your enemies confusion. 
Act consistent with their false beliefs and confusion, 
allow them to feel they understand you. 
Let them believe what they will believe. 
Not correcting their confusion is not deceit. 
This is why knowing yourself is important, 
it prevents others from controlling your beliefs.


7. Possibility Principle
    
Act to expand your possibilities and close your opponents. 
Grow your possibilities of defense.
Shrink your opponents possibilities of attack. 
Grow your defense surface 
	to shrink their attack vectors.

	
8. Configuration Principle
    
See the energetic potential in a configuration of elements, 
and leverage the advantages of the configuration to multiply your own advantages. 
See the potential advantages and dangers to avoid. 
Some configurations offer potential advantage, 
but dangers that tip the scales, 
and so should be avoided.

		
9. Principle of Redirection
    
Do not attack your opponent, 
but if attacked, 
shift into a non-target, 
utilizing the configuration of the circumstances to turn back toward the attacker. 
Rearrange so the path of energy follows its most efficient path, 
leading back to the attacker, 
but not to harm, 
to disarm.

		
10. Equanimity Principle
    
Stay completely: 
	clear, 
	aware, 
	coherent, 
	stable, 
	fearless, 
	confident. 
Equanimity is the base on the battlefield, 
	when the one in command 
	can be surrounded by the chaos of war, 
	but resting firm within 
	a stable presence of being. 
See atrocity and feeling the emotion without letting it have any effect. 
Like a captain at sea in a storm, 
	controlling the boat 
	while shielded from the storm, 
	staying dry 
while all else is covered in water.

		
11. Deterrence Principle
    
Raise the stakes of battle 
	above the cost 
	the enemy is willing 
to pay.

		
12. Grounding Principle
    
See yourself in your enemy; 
see your enemy in yourself. 
See your enemy, 
accept your enemy, 
respect your enemy, 
love your enemy; 
but as a last resort, 
subdue your enemy, 
diffuse their will-to-conflict. 
And if they continue to attack, 
allows those attacks to fall upon they weapons, 
leaving the attackers unharmed. 
You are your enemy in another body, 
your enemy is you in another body--
division is illusion.

		
13. Anti-Damage Principle
    
Where there is damage, 
rebuild stronger, 
learn what couldn't have been learned otherwise. 
Learn how to see the pattern in advance, 
learn to see the signs that led to damage and update your response. 
Always rebuild stronger.

		
14. Transmutation Principle
    
If attacked, do not attack, 
but transmute the energy of the attack into your fuel for development. 
With every attack, 
they lose, 
but you gain. 
They grow weaker with each attack, 
as you grow stronger. 
This is an advanced technique, 
but included for comprehensiveness.

		
15. Cultivation Principle
    
Use time and consistency to cultivate circumstances 
that may not be advantageous for a long time, 
or ever, 
but in particular situations could lead to 
disproportionate advantage, 
using foresight as the ultimate advantage.

		
16. Opportunity Principle
    
Opportunities come and go, 
	moving here and there, 
	far and near; 
	first learn to see them, 
then learn to seize them.

		
17. Timing Principle
    
An action that brings victory at one moment, 
may bring defeat at another. 
Time your actions like notes in a symphony, 
combining: 
	harmony, 
	rhythm, 
	melody. 
Use polyrhythms to match and switch the rhythm of your enemy. 
Maintain the initiative of rhythm.

		
18. Principle of Restraint
    
An action of restraint, 
against all expectation otherwise, 
is a demonstration. 
It sets a standard. 
It can soften hearts, 
diffusing wills. 
Perspective invert, 
confusion abounds, 
the presence of leadership is felt, 
understood, 
witnessed.

		
19. Principle of Unity
    
Keep unity as the goal; 
treat your enemy as your future potential ally. 
Prefer winning others to your side, 
than defeating their side. 
Always image life after war, 
imagine the peacetime, 
know you will eat and drink 
with your enemies in the future, 
and it is better to cultivate mutual respect, 
then neither side completely loses.

		
20. Self-Adversary Principle
    
You can be your own worst enemy, 
	exhausting yourself, 
	fighting yourself, 
	leaving little for your enemy to do to bring defeat. 
Fight yourself, 
	but learn your own tactics, 
	learn how to overcome your own tactics, 
	find ways around them. 
Use your mind as your training ground. 
And after much practice and experience, 
	that self-fight turns into a self-dance, 
	and then a partnership. 
When you are a partner to all parts of yourself, 
	equanimity follows.

		
21. Self-Divided Principle
    
A self-adversarial relation can harden, 
	if not harmonized, 
	into self-division. 
A self-divided enemy is vulnerable, 
	susceptible to bringing self-destruction 
	from self-deception, 
from never seeing things from one perspective. 
An enemy that divides against themself 
	is already defeated.

		
22. Denial Principle
    
Deny your enemy a target. 
Deny your enemy fuel for conflict 
	with strategic silence. 
Deny your enemy the action 
	they need to justify theirs. 
Deny becoming what your enemy thinks you are. 
Deny engagement. 
Deny attention. 
Deny recognition. 
Deny acknowledging false authority. 
Deny meeting the expectations of others. 
Deny access. 
Deny influence. 
Deny effect.
Deny reaction.
Deny control.
Deny defeat.


23. Mirror Principle
    
Let your actions and behavior become a mirror to your 	enemies, 
	showing them who they are, 
	showing them who you are. 
Let them see what they have sought to avoid, 
	reflect their attack back to their own ego, 
	shattering their self-image, 
diffusing their will-to-conflict.

		
24. Question Principle
    
Act so that your enemy 
has more questions 
than answers, 
and before they can answers 
those questions, 
act again, 
generate more, 
different questions, 
leave the original open, 
and when they are finally 
about to get their bearings, 
act again, 
completely unpredictably, 
perhaps for the sake of disinformation. 
Never get them a chance 
to understand where you are coming from. 
Learn 
	grow 
		develop 
always so that if 
	they finally understand you, 
		you have already evolved. 
They keep trying to hit a moving target. 
Get them to focus on the movement, 
not the target. 
Reframe the situation.

		
25. Principle of Hidden Advantage
    
Find the hidden advantage 
in apparent disadvantages 
by changing your how you see 
those disadvantages. 
Often the very differences 
that are disadvantages in one context, 
may find a particular configuration 
of circumstances that make that disadvantage 
temporarily advantageous. 
Find the hidden advantages, 
and learn how to use them 
to transmute 
disadvantages into advantages.

		
26. Perspective Principle
    
Two viewing the same situation 
	differ in perspective 
		the foundation of belief and action.
Cultivate your perspective to optimize:
	clarity, 
	truth, 
	vision, 
	pattern, 
	flexibility, 
	fluency, 
	self-correction, 
	self-analysis, 
	self-learning. 
Between two generals on the battlefield:
	one with a perspective that:
		sees more and sees it clearly 
	has an advantage over 
		the limited and confused perspective. 
Cultivating perspective is a life-long activity--
	beginning on day one.


27. The Principle of Non-defeat
    
Negotiate for shared victory. 
Find paths to shared success. 
Transmute the conflict. 
Eliminate defeat.


Part II


Art of the Strategist

I. The Unequal Field

When one stands against many, every expenditure returns to the same small store.

The Strategist therefore begins with a reckoning:

What must be preserved?
What may be yielded?
What must be accomplished?
What can the encounter be permitted to cost?

A purpose too vaguely held consumes strength without directing it.

The Strategist gives the purpose a clear boundary, so that an adversary cannot enlarge the contest merely by presenting another challenge.

⸻

To be outnumbered is a condition.
To be overcome is an outcome.

Between them lie ground, knowledge, preparation, relationship, and time.

Numbers acquire force through the means that bring them together. Agreement joins them. Provisions sustain them. Passage admits them. Timing makes their arrival useful.

The Strategist studies these connections.

A thousand hands crowded against a gate may obstruct one another. A single hand upon its wheel may open it.

The gate and the wheel belong in the reckoning.

⸻

The many can divide their labor, replace the weary, and examine a question from several sides. They may continue an undertaking through losses that would end the effort of one.

The Strategist gives these advantages their full weight.

One can change direction without assembling a council. One can also carry an error forward without meeting a correction.

A single judgment travels swiftly. Its blindness travels with it.

Thus the Strategist seeks counsel where knowledge is incomplete and preserves freedom where delay would consume the opportunity.

⸻

Where the many share a purpose, reckon their unity.

Where their purposes differ, learn the differences.

One banner may cover several ambitions. Several voices may serve one intention.

The Strategist discovers which agreements endure inconvenience, which survive disappointment, and which depend upon rewards that cannot be maintained.

The strength of an arrangement appears in what it can bear.

⸻

An advantage must reach the place where it is needed.

Knowledge held elsewhere, strength committed elsewhere, and assistance arriving afterward have different values from knowledge, strength, and assistance present at the decisive hour.

The Strategist compares what can become effective.

A distant abundance may be less useful than a modest provision close at hand.

Yet distance can be crossed, provisions gathered, and scattered forces joined. The reckoning changes as the adversary prepares.

Yesterday’s opening may become today’s guarded passage.

⸻

Before accepting the encounter, the Strategist examines what each side must continue doing.

What must be held?
What must be supplied?
What must be explained?
What requires uninterrupted attention?

An undertaking that consumes a little from many may consume everything from one.

The Strategist is sparing with commitments that multiply demands upon the same hour.

A person who must answer every movement has surrendered the ordering of the day.

⸻

The unequal field also contains unequal necessities.

One side may require a public triumph. Another may require only a safe departure.

One may have to preserve a position. Another may be free to abandon it.

One may need an answer before nightfall. Another may benefit from another season of preparation.

The Strategist learns what success requires of each participant.

The effort demanded by a purpose is part of its cost.

⸻

Solitude has its burdens.

A small company moves lightly when its necessities are provided for. Without provision, each missing thing becomes a command.

The Strategist attends to tools, shelter, learning, and trustworthy relationships before praising independence.

The lone tree stands through roots extending beyond its shadow.

What appears to act alone may be sustained by a long history of assistance.

The Strategist preserves the sources of that strength.

⸻

Where the adversary’s advantages converge, the Strategist measures the cost of remaining.

Where another ground is available, the cost of reaching it must also be measured.

Where no favorable change is yet possible, preserve the means through which one may become possible.

A retreat that retains judgment, skill, and the capacity to return may leave more within reach than an advance that exhausts them.

Courage has a season. Preparation has a season. The same purpose may require both.

⸻

The Master Strategist continually revises the reckoning.

An apparent weakness may acquire a use. A treasured advantage may become a burden. A relationship may open a passage that strength alone could never secure.

Thus preparation changes the encounter before the encounter changes the participants.

When the Strategist finally advances, the difference in numbers may remain.

The relation between the forces has changed.




II. The Center

The Strategist preserves a place from which to judge.

When many demands arrive together, each presents itself as the matter that must be answered first. Each would arrange the remaining hours around its own importance.

The Strategist examines what gives a demand its urgency.

A loud summons may concern a small matter. A quiet change may alter the whole undertaking.

Attention is placed according to consequence.

⸻

An adversary who cannot command the Strategist may attempt to appoint the next concern.

Insult invites an answer.
Praise invites a performance.
A challenge invites a demonstration.

The Strategist considers what accepting each invitation would require.

To prove strength at every summons is to let another choose its expenditure.

Some challenges are answered through action. Some through clarification. Some lose their power when the work continues.

⸻

Anger may disclose an injury. Fear may disclose a danger. Enthusiasm may disclose a possibility.

Each brings a report.

The Strategist hears the messengers and compares what they have seen.

An injury may be real while the proposed retaliation is ruinous. A danger may be real while the feared outcome remains avoidable. A possibility may be real while the means of reaching it are absent.

The report enters the reckoning.

The whole undertaking remains in view.

⸻

The center must allow movement.

A wheel fixed to the earth cannot carry its burden. Its center holds within an arrangement that travels.

So the Strategist distinguishes what gives an undertaking its direction from the particular arrangements through which it proceeds.

A route may change.
An instrument may be replaced.
An alliance may acquire another form.

The purpose is examined through these changes. When new knowledge reveals an error in the purpose itself, correction reaches that far.

What is sound can endure examination.

⸻

The Strategist receives correction according to what it reveals.

An opponent may notice a weakness that a friend has overlooked. A friend may offer reassurance where investigation is needed.

The source of a warning is considered together with its grounds.

Useful knowledge is brought into the reckoning; judgment remains with the Strategist.

Those who must reject every criticism give their critics an unusual power: the power to make a necessary correction intolerable.

⸻

Praise and blame travel at a different pace from consequences.

Applause may arrive while a foundation is weakening. Reproach may arrive while useful work is taking root.

The Strategist examines the foundation and the roots.

Where conduct has caused harm, repair belongs to the undertaking. Where misunderstanding obstructs cooperation, explanation may clear the passage. Where an accusation has no bearing upon either, attention can return to the work.

The response is chosen through its use.

⸻

Disturbance will come.

The Strategist practices returning before the return becomes difficult.

A small interruption offers practice in recovering attention. A mistaken judgment offers practice in correction. An unexpected refusal offers practice in finding another approach.

Repeated returns establish a path.

One who requires perfect quiet can be stopped by noise. One accustomed to recovering direction can continue through an imperfect day.

Composure grows through this practice.

⸻

The body carries the judgment.

Hunger narrows patience. Exhaustion makes familiar answers attractive. Pain can fill the distance between one thought and the next.

The Strategist attends to these conditions as carefully as to the conditions of the field.

Where many can relieve one another, one must arrange the work to permit renewal.

A reserve of strength allows a second look. A second look may save the strength that haste would spend.

Rest has a place in preparation.

⸻

The center is tested through conduct.

A calm expression may conceal confused judgment. A shaken voice may carry an accurate warning.

The Strategist examines what the moment requires.

Where a pause improves perception, pause. Where delay closes a necessary passage, move. Where an error is becoming costly, correct it while correction remains small.

Trained attention shortens the distance between recognizing a need and meeting it.

The useful stillness is the stillness from which appropriate movement can begin.

⸻

Integrity gives decisions continuity.

The Strategist considers what repeated actions are making habitual.

A small falsehood may require a larger one to protect it. An unnecessary quarrel may make the next quarrel easier. A neglected obligation may weaken the trust upon which future freedom depends.

Each action prepares something within the one who performs it.

The Strategist therefore attends to the capacities being strengthened by the chosen means.

The undertaking trains its practitioner.

⸻

The Master Strategist guards the ability to return:

From agitation to observation.
From observation to judgment.
From judgment to fitting action.

What has been learned enters the next movement. What has become mistaken can be released.

Thus the center acquires depth through change.

The axle holds its place within the wheel—and travels the whole road.




III. Seeing and Being Seen

The Strategist examines the field and the place from which it is observed.

A ridge that conceals the road from the village also conceals the village from the road.

What is plainly visible from one position may be absent from another’s account.

Before judging the account, discover where the observer stood.

Before trusting a view, discover what the position excludes.

⸻

Familiar appearances invite familiar explanations.

A hurried departure may resemble fear. Silence may resemble agreement. An easy accomplishment may resemble a small undertaking.

The Strategist considers what else could produce the same appearance.

The movement is observed.
Its cause is investigated.
Its purpose remains a question until the grounds are sufficient.

A name given too quickly can become an obstacle placed before the thing it names.

⸻

Reports must be traced to their beginnings.

Ten messengers repeating one witness bring the testimony of one witness ten times.

The Strategist distinguishes repeated certainty from independent observation.

Where accounts differ, examine the circumstances of their making. Distance, attention, expectation, and interest may each explain a difference.

Agreement has greater weight when the observers could have discovered different things.

⸻

The many can distribute their watching.

One studies the movement. Another studies the instrument. Another studies what followed.

Their knowledge grows through division; their understanding depends upon reunion.

The Strategist attends to the places where their reports meet.

A capable adversary will compare discrepancies, revise explanations, and learn from unsuccessful predictions.

Confusion is therefore reckoned as a changing condition.

An advantage that requires the adversary to remain forever mistaken has a fragile foundation.

⸻

Those who compete to explain an event may become attached to being its interpreter.

An explanation can acquire defenders before it acquires sufficient grounds.

The Strategist observes what follows from its acceptance.

Which preparations does it encourage?
Which possibilities does it dismiss?
Which commitments become difficult to reverse?

A mistaken account may govern real resources.

The Strategist proceeds according to the circumstances, while remembering that others may proceed according to the account.

Both movements belong to the field.

⸻

Every visible action enters several understandings.

A demonstration of capability may appear to one as a warning, to another as an invitation, and to a third as a problem requiring study.

The same signal can produce different assemblies around it.

The Strategist considers who can see, what each can recognize, and what each is likely to do afterward.

Visibility has consequences beyond recognition.

To become conspicuous is also to become a subject of decisions made elsewhere.

⸻

Uncertainty has different uses in different relationships.

Where premature knowledge would close a necessary passage, the Strategist protects the preparation.

Where cooperation requires dependable expectations, the Strategist makes the relevant commitments clear.

Where uncertainty invites useful inquiry, allow room for inquiry.

Where it produces needless conflict, clarification may be the economical action.

Concealment itself requires judgment. Those kept uncertain may delay, withdraw, investigate, or prepare against what they cannot determine.

The Strategist reckons these responses before choosing obscurity.

⸻

An action can be fully visible while its preparation remains unseen.

The hand completes the movement in a moment. Practice has prepared the hand through many seasons.

Observers may copy what occurred at the end and obtain another result.

The Strategist studies what made the movement available:

The capacities already developed.
The conditions already established.
The dependencies already satisfied.

The visible act is one portion of its history.

⸻

Observation changes the encounter when it becomes known.

An adversary who discovers what is being watched may alter the appearance presented there.

The Strategist compares conduct across circumstances and gives particular attention to consequences that are costly to imitate.

A promise is easily displayed. Sustained provision requires more.

An expression of readiness is easily arranged. Readiness that survives interruption has deeper roots.

The weight of a sign depends partly upon what producing it requires.

⸻

Distance must also be varied.

At close range, examine the movement.
At a greater distance, examine the exchange.
Farther still, examine what the exchange is serving.

A small error may disappear in the distant view. A whole undertaking may disappear in the near view.

The Strategist returns between them.

The crack in a bridge matters. So does the destination to which the bridge leads.

⸻

Being studied can tempt the Strategist to perform for the study.

A reputation for boldness demands another bold act. A reputation for mystery demands another obscurity. A reputation for precision makes an ordinary correction appear costly.

The Strategist keeps reputation within the means of the undertaking.

Preparation may proceed quietly while explanations circulate.

What has already been accomplished can remain available for examination while attention turns to what now requires care.

⸻

The Master Strategist chooses both the distance of observation and the extent of disclosure.

Near enough to distinguish.
Far enough to connect.
Open enough for needed cooperation.
Reserved enough for unfinished work to mature.

Some things must become visible before others can join them.

Some things require shelter before they can stand.

The hour for each belongs to the strategy.




IV. The Unseen Preparation

The Strategist begins preparing while the decisive movement is still distant.

What later appears as a single action may gather the work of many days.

Skill makes the movement possible. Provision makes it sustainable. Understanding gives it direction. Favorable conditions allow its effects to travel.

The final effort receives the strength of these preparations.

A mill turns when water reaches the wheel. Long before that moment, the channel must have been opened.

⸻

The Strategist considers the intended result and traces the requirements backward.

What must be possible at the final moment?
What must already be established for that possibility to exist?
Which earlier action makes the next action available?

Through these questions, a distant intention acquires a path.

Where the path contains an unknown passage, investigation becomes part of the preparation.

An imagined crossing still requires a ford.

⸻

Possessions are examined through their relationships.

An instrument requires a practiced hand. A practiced hand requires access to the place of use. Access may require an agreement made before urgency diminishes the freedom to negotiate.

The Strategist follows these dependencies.

An abundance of instruments cannot supply a missing agreement. An abundance of promises cannot supply an absent capability.

Preparation reaches the points where one kind of strength must become another.

⸻

Where one must meet the efforts of many, unnecessary decisions become a burden.

The Strategist settles recurring matters through practice, places useful things within reach, and gives familiar tasks a dependable order.

A tool laid ready returns the moment that searching would have consumed.

A practiced movement leaves attention available for an unfamiliar circumstance.

Thus ordinary arrangements preserve judgment for the places where judgment is most needed.

The quiet order of preparation becomes freedom in the hour of pressure.

⸻

The parts must be brought together.

A stronger instrument may demand more strength to carry. A swifter method may exhaust what sustains it. Greater concealment may obstruct necessary cooperation.

The Strategist examines improvements in company with their companions.

What does this change require elsewhere?
What does it make easier?
What does it render difficult?

A preparation is sound when its parts can perform together under the conditions of use.

Fine timber badly joined makes an unreliable bridge.

⸻

The Strategist allows preparation to meet resistance while correction remains inexpensive.

A small trial may reveal an assumption hidden within a large design.

An interruption may show which task depends upon uninterrupted attention. A missing resource may reveal how many arrangements secretly depend upon it.

These discoveries are brought into the work.

The seam is examined before the burden is entrusted to it.

Care taken here may leave little drama for the decisive hour.

⸻

The adversary also has choices.

The Strategist considers several responses that a capable opponent could reasonably make.

Preparations useful across these responses have broad value. Those useful in only one narrow circumstance require closer attention to their cost.

A plan that needs every opposing decision to be favorable places much of its strength beyond its own command.

The Strategist develops capacities that retain their usefulness when the anticipated sequence changes.

A reserve keeps a choice available.

⸻

Some conditions can be established directly. Others depend upon seasons, circumstances, and independent decisions.

The Strategist distinguishes them.

Provisions can be gathered. Practice can be sustained. Agreements can be sought and their reliability examined.

The river still has its seasons.

Readiness includes knowing what remains beyond command and preserving room to respond when it changes.

Confidence is strengthened by understanding the dependencies upon which it rests.

⸻

Preparation also removes what interferes.

A cumbersome habit may consume more strength than a missing instrument. An obsolete obligation may hold resources where they have ceased to serve. An unnecessary explanation may invite a dispute that the undertaking never required.

The Strategist examines what can be released.

Every addition brings something to maintain.

A well prepared undertaking carries what its purpose requires and leaves space for what the encounter may reveal.

⸻

Success must also be prepared for.

The Strategist considers how a favorable result will be received, sustained, and used.

An opening may be lost through the inability to move through it. A new relationship may fail through neglected obligations. A recovered position may consume all the strength needed to make it useful.

Preparation therefore extends beyond obtaining the result.

What must be ready afterward belongs among the requirements beforehand.

⸻

Preparation has a point of sufficiency.

Further refinement may improve the instrument while the useful hour passes.

The Master Strategist watches both the growing readiness and the changing field.

When the necessary conditions meet, action can begin. Remaining uncertainty is met through attention, reserves, and the ability to adjust.

The movement may be brief.

At that moment, many earlier efforts arrive together.




V. The Uses of Time

The Strategist measures time by what changes within it.

An hour may replenish a store, exhaust a resource, bring assistance nearer, or carry an opening beyond reach.

The same interval bears different consequences for those who inhabit it.

Before choosing delay, the Strategist asks:

What is growing?
What is diminishing?
What will become possible?
What will cease to be possible?

Waiting takes its value from these changes.

⸻

Time is available to the adversary also.

While one prepares, another may discover the preparation. While one recovers, another may gather strength. While one seeks agreement, another may make agreement unnecessary.

The Strategist compares the developments on both sides.

Patience has an advantage where the passing interval improves the relation between them.

Where the interval enlarges the disadvantage, patience requires another form: sustained work to change what the interval is producing.

⸻

The many may continue through successive efforts.

One rests while another watches. One withdraws while another takes up the task.

The Strategist who stands alone must account for this renewal.

An encounter that appears even within one hour may become unequal across several days.

Therefore the Strategist considers how long a chosen pace can be maintained, what it consumes, and where renewal can occur.

A brief exertion and a continuing way of life require different provisions.

⸻

Speed has value in relation to the whole passage.

A hurried beginning may leave necessities behind. An early commitment may require many later corrections. A forceful intervention may create a difficulty that takes a season to resolve.

The Strategist reckons the time that follows the movement.

Where preparation removes repeated delay, a measured beginning may yield the earlier completion.

Where the opening is brief and readiness sufficient, hesitation spends what preparation has earned.

⸻

Different things require different durations.

A message may travel quickly.
Understanding may require several encounters.
Trust grows through conduct.
Skill develops through repeated practice.

The Strategist gives each its necessary interval.

A command can hasten an outward movement while leaving the capacity beneath it unchanged.

Those who demand the harvest at the hour of sowing will exhaust the field searching for what has yet to grow.

⸻

Several works may proceed at different paces.

While one matter settles, another can be prepared. While a decision remains with others, a useful capability can be developed. While a long undertaking matures, a smaller task may clear its way.

The Strategist joins these efforts where their requirements meet.

They need not all begin together. They need not all move at the same speed.

Their coordination depends upon knowing when one requires the result of another.

The slowest necessary work is begun while there is still time for it.

⸻

An announced hour changes present conduct.

People gather, resources are committed, expectations harden, and alternatives may be abandoned.

The Strategist makes promises of time with care.

An appointed moment can bring scattered efforts together. An ill-chosen moment can bind them to haste.

Where the appointed hour has lost its purpose, the arrangement is reconsidered while change remains possible.

A date once written does not suspend the seasons.

⸻

Urgency must be examined at its source.

Some urgency comes from a closing passage. Some comes from dwindling provision. Some comes from another’s desire to obtain a decision before fuller knowledge arrives.

The Strategist asks what would change if the decision were made earlier or later.

Where delay brings useful knowledge at little cost, take the interval.

Where delay consumes the means of action, use what is already sufficiently known.

Judgment includes the cost of waiting to improve judgment.

⸻

Consequences have their own times of arrival.

An action may be complete while its effects are still traveling.

The Strategist allows the relevant interval before deciding what has occurred.

To repeat an intervention before its first effects arrive may produce excess. To abandon a sound effort before it can bear fruit may waste the preparation already made.

Yet an expected result must remain open to examination.

The season is observed; the promise of a harvest is tested against the field.

⸻

Past expenditure can give an undertaking a false urgency.

Much has been prepared; therefore it seems that something must now be done.

The Strategist returns to the present conditions.

Preparations may retain value through another use. A missed opening may leave useful knowledge. An abandoned route may preserve the strength needed for a better one.

What has already been spent enters the record.

What can still be accomplished governs the next expenditure.

⸻

The Master Strategist attends to several durations at once:

The moment in which a choice must be made.
The period through which effort must be sustained.
The future in which the consequences will be lived.

Each places a different demand upon the present.

A long purpose may advance through a brief action.

A brief restraint may preserve a long future.




VI. One Action, Many Consequences

An action seldom ends where the hand withdraws.

It changes the conditions of another action. It enters the understanding of those who observe it. It may establish an expectation that survives the occasion.

The Strategist follows these consequences beyond the first result.

What has been accomplished?
What has become easier?
What has become harder?
What will others now attempt?

The movement continues through the answers.

⸻

Where one must meet the efforts of many, each expenditure deserves careful placement.

A road repaired may shorten the outward journey, ease the return, and make assistance possible.

The labor is one. Its uses are several.

The Strategist seeks actions through which compatible purposes can advance together.

A modest effort placed at their meeting may accomplish more than several greater efforts made separately.

The relationship among the purposes gives the action its reach.

⸻

The principal purpose must remain clear.

An action burdened with too many requirements may become difficult to perform and impossible to judge.

A boat loaded with every useful possession may be unable to leave the bank.

The Strategist determines what the movement must accomplish, which further benefits fit naturally within it, and which ambitions require another occasion.

Simplicity in execution can support breadth in consequence.

The hand should have a clear task.

⸻

An action also invites responses.

The adversary may accept, refuse, delay, imitate, investigate, or change direction.

The Strategist considers what remains possible after the likely replies.

Where several replies leave a useful continuation, the movement has breadth. Where only one reply allows progress, its dependence upon that reply must be reckoned.

An opponent retains the power to choose.

Preparation concerns the paths that remain available when that choice is made.

⸻

Some actions accomplish a task while revealing the conditions of its accomplishment.

A small attempt may disclose a hidden resistance. A limited agreement may reveal whether cooperation can endure inconvenience. A practiced movement under unfamiliar conditions may reveal where skill remains incomplete.

The Strategist records the intended result beside the result obtained.

The difference may provide knowledge useful to the next attempt.

Knowledge gained enters the reckoning alongside the resources spent and the purpose still unfinished.

⸻

Consequences travel through connections.

A change in one place may release strength elsewhere. The removal of one difficulty may expose another that it had concealed.

The Strategist follows the passage.

When a burden disappears from one position, where has it gone? Who must now carry it? What will their response require?

A gate closed against present pressure may also obstruct assistance arriving later.

The immediate use of a measure is examined together with the other passages it affects.

⸻

Actions can establish customs.

A concession made once may be expected again. A boundary maintained consistently may spare repeated disputes. A promise fulfilled may make later cooperation possible with fewer assurances.

The Strategist distinguishes settling an occasion from establishing a continuing arrangement.

What can be sustained as a custom may be undertaken openly.

What belongs to exceptional circumstances is kept intelligible through those circumstances.

A brief act can appoint a long obligation.

⸻

The value of a result includes the freedom it leaves afterward.

A gain that requires constant defense consumes part of itself. An agreement that renews trust may reduce the effort required for the next agreement. A skill acquired for one purpose may become useful in several others.

The Strategist considers which benefits must continually be purchased and which can support further activity.

What remains available can contribute again.

Thus a completed undertaking may leave behind means that outlast its immediate reward.

⸻

Some effects reinforce one another. Others compete.

Greater speed may reveal capability while exhausting endurance. A public success may attract assistance while attracting closer scrutiny. A generous offer may resolve a dispute while creating expectations beyond the available means.

The Strategist considers these consequences together.

Where they can be brought into agreement, the action gains strength.

Where they remain in conflict, their relative importance must be decided.

A purpose left undecided within the plan becomes a conflict within its execution.

⸻

After acting, the Strategist examines the changed field.

An anticipated benefit may fail to appear. An unforeseen opportunity may become available. The adversary may respond in a way that alters the meaning of the original movement.

The next action begins from what has actually changed.

A design remains useful through its ability to receive these discoveries.

The first movement supplies conditions for the second; the second reveals more about the first.

⸻

The Master Strategist joins compatible purposes while preserving a clear action.

Preparation establishes the connections through which its effects can travel. Judgment attends to what those effects make possible.

One movement may complete a task, develop a capacity, and open a further path.

The hand completes its action.

The field continues it.




VII. Convergence and Discord

Separate strengths can enlarge one another.

They can also consume one another through poor arrangement.

The Strategist examines where their purposes meet, how their means interact, and when their efforts become useful.

Agreement in purpose may still leave conflict in method. Sound methods may fail through mistimed arrival.

Convergence requires these relationships to be brought into accord.

The strength of the gathering is discovered in what its members make possible together.

⸻

Different work calls for different movement.

One lifts while another steadies. One advances while another maintains the means of return. One discovers what another knows how to use.

The Strategist makes these relationships clear.

Each participant should understand what the others require from the work and what freedom remains within it.

Where the necessary relationships are understood, differences of method can become a source of strength.

Several paths may bring their provisions to the same meeting place.

⸻

Common purpose allows judgment to be distributed.

Those nearest a changing circumstance may perceive what a distant commander has yet to learn.

The Strategist establishes enough understanding for useful adjustment to occur where the need appears.

If every small correction must travel through one mind, the work of many acquires the limits of one person’s attention.

A clear intention can guide actions that have never been specified in advance.

Preparation gives this freedom its foundation.

⸻

Connections must be fitted to the work.

Some activities require close agreement at every movement. Others need only meet at an appointed stage.

The Strategist joins them accordingly.

Too little connection leaves efforts unable to support one another. Too much connection makes every disturbance travel through the whole.

A fastening should hold what must remain together and permit the movement the task requires.

The soundness of an arrangement includes its ability to contain a local difficulty.

⸻

Tension can sustain a form.

An arch stands through pressures that meet. A string acquires its voice through a measured pull.

The Strategist distinguishes useful tension from strain that damages the undertaking.

A question may strengthen a decision. A competing proposal may reveal an overlooked possibility. A refusal may disclose a limit that the plan must acknowledge.

These differences are examined while adjustment remains possible.

Those who reward agreement alone may first learn of discord through failure.

⸻

Cooperation has costs as well as fruits.

The Strategist observes who bears the recurring burden, who receives renewal, and who gains from the common result.

An arrangement may appear harmonious while one necessary participant is being exhausted.

Such harmony is spending its own foundation.

Where the work requires continuing contributions, its benefits and burdens must permit those contributions to continue.

Care for the sources of strength belongs within the use of strength.

⸻

The adversary’s numbers also acquire power through relationships.

Shared interests, dependable signals, mutual confidence, and the ability to replace losses may make many forces act coherently.

The Strategist gives that coherence its proper weight.

Where their commitments differ, the differences are investigated. Some may oppose the undertaking wholly. Some may oppose only one condition. Others may have purposes that permit cooperation.

Treating every difference as the same hostility can give separate interests a common cause.

The field must be understood in its particulars.

⸻

Even one person contains several capacities that must learn to work together.

Imagination opens possibilities. Discernment examines them. Courage begins a difficult movement. Care preserves what the movement depends upon.

The Strategist develops their cooperation through practice.

When imagination outruns examination, plans lose contact with their requirements. When examination continually prevents a beginning, knowledge remains unused.

A capacity gains strategic value through its relationship with the others.

Its fullest exercise need not occur at every moment.

⸻

Leadership follows the requirements of the encounter.

The one who knows the passage may guide the crossing. The one who recognizes a failing instrument may direct its repair. The one who understands a dispute may help restore cooperation.

The Strategist allows relevant understanding to come forward.

Those who follow retain attention. Those who lead remain able to receive correction.

Thus the undertaking can draw upon knowledge without forcing every contribution through the same voice.

⸻

A successful arrangement must still be heard as it changes.

New skill alters what can be asked of a participant. Greater reach may require another means of coordination. A former source of assistance may become overburdened by the growth it helped produce.

The Strategist revisits the relationships.

An agreement fitted to an earlier condition may need another form to preserve its purpose.

Continued accord depends upon continued listening.

⸻

The Master Strategist cultivates a movement in which distinct efforts can strengthen one another.

Each contribution has room to develop. Each attends to the others at the places where their work meets.

Leadership can move. Correction can enter. A pause in one part can give another room to be heard.

The drum keeps its measure.

The flute carries its line.

Their meeting gives the music a reach that neither possesses alone.




VIII. The Changing Ground

The ground of an encounter includes whatever gives an action its reach and cost.

Distance is ground. Access is ground. So are the agreements through which assistance arrives and the dependencies through which pressure acquires force.

The Strategist studies how these conditions make particular strengths effective.

A gate commands a passage because the journey must pass through it.

Its power belongs partly to the roads around it.

⸻

Where repeated efforts meet the same resistance, the Strategist examines the arrangement that renews the difficulty.

What keeps the obstacle necessary?
What brings the undertaking back to it?
Which requirement has been accepted without examination?

Greater effort may be useful. Another route may be available. The purpose itself may admit a different means of fulfillment.

The Strategist follows the inquiry far enough to distinguish these possibilities.

A contest can persist through the continued acceptance of its ground.

⸻

In an unequal encounter, the Strategist seeks conditions under which available strength can accomplish the purpose.

Numbers are powerful where many can act together. Abundance is powerful where it can be brought into use. Authority is powerful where action depends upon its permission.

Each advantage has conditions.

The Strategist considers which can be changed, which must be accommodated, and which make the present undertaking impracticable.

A sound judgment may reveal a passage or establish the need to prepare elsewhere.

⸻

A small change can alter a large requirement.

The stone retains its weight when the support beneath the lever is moved. The effort required at the hand changes.

The Strategist looks for relationships of this kind.

A different arrangement of work may release attention. A dependable means of supply may remove recurring urgency. An additional source of knowledge may lessen dependence upon one interpreter.

The useful change is measured through what it enables.

Its size alone reveals little.

⸻

Some pressures draw strength from continued participation.

A recurring challenge requires someone to answer it. A contest for recognition requires the recognition to remain desirable. A costly obligation may endure because its purpose has ceased to be examined.

Where participation can be chosen, the Strategist considers what withdrawal would actually cost.

What would be lost?
What would become available?
Would the original purpose remain within reach?

Withdrawal changes the ground when it removes something upon which the encounter depended.

⸻

A dispute may also be changed by enlarging what is possible.

Two parties seeking passage through a narrow crossing may oppose one another because only one can pass at a time.

Another crossing alters their relationship.

The Strategist investigates whether the scarcity that organizes a conflict can be relieved, whether its burdens can be arranged differently, or whether distinct needs have been mistaken for identical demands.

Cooperation sometimes becomes possible through a change in provision.

The change gives agreement somewhere to stand.

⸻

An altered understanding can reveal ground that was already available.

A practice treated as compulsory may rest upon a revisable agreement. A resource considered useless may acquire value through another application. A difference treated as an obstacle may supply a missing capability.

The Strategist examines these possibilities through their requirements.

A proposed passage must connect places that can actually be reached.

An altered description gains strategic value when it permits clearer judgment or workable action.

The newly perceived ground must still bear weight.

⸻

Changing ground has a cost of passage.

A familiar arrangement may continue providing shelter while a better one is being established. Leaving it too early can expose the undertaking between two forms of support.

The Strategist prepares the transition.

What must continue during the change?
Which commitments must be revised together?
Where can a temporary interruption be endured?

A favorable destination does not remove the difficulties of reaching it.

The crossing belongs within the design.

⸻

Others will respond to the altered field.

A new route may be used by the adversary. An agreement may invite another alliance. A reduced dependency may make former partners reconsider their commitments.

The Strategist examines these responses as part of the change.

Ground is shared by those whose actions can affect it.

To alter one relationship is to invite further movement among the others.

The work continues through attention to what the alteration sets in motion.

⸻

Successful changes can become sources of attachment.

A route once liberating may later become crowded. An arrangement that once conserved strength may become costly as the undertaking grows.

The Strategist revisits the conditions of its usefulness.

Earlier success supplies knowledge. Present conditions determine the next use of that knowledge.

The ability to change ground must survive the satisfaction of having changed it well.

⸻

The Master Strategist considers how the purpose might be fulfilled under another arrangement of circumstances.

Sometimes the necessary work is to develop greater strength. Sometimes it is to bring existing strength into a more useful relationship. Sometimes it is to remove a dependency that made the contest unavoidable.

Then an adversary may retain a formidable position while that position loses its former command over the undertaking.

The gate still stands.

The journey has found another passage.




IX. Learning in Motion

The Strategist begins with enough understanding to choose a useful movement and enough attention to discover where that understanding fails.

Some knowledge becomes available only through encounter.

The ground yields beneath a step. An agreement meets an inconvenience. A practiced skill enters unfamiliar conditions.

Each reveals something that anticipation alone could leave concealed.

The undertaking advances through action and through the correction of the judgment that directs it.

⸻

Where strength is limited, the cost of discovery must be measured.

The Strategist seeks trials small enough to endure and substantial enough to teach.

A craftsperson tests an edge upon a spare piece before entrusting it to the principal timber.

The trial should meet the uncertainty that matters.

A test conducted under easy conditions may reveal little about a difficult passage. A costly demonstration may provide no more knowledge than a modest attempt.

The question determines the trial.

⸻

When an attempt fails, the Strategist locates the difference between expectation and occurrence.

Was the purpose attainable?
Were the means suited to it?
Were those means used adequately?
Did the conditions change?

Different failures require different corrections.

Greater effort may improve execution. It may also deepen the cost of an unsuitable method.

The Strategist examines the failure closely enough to know what deserves another attempt.

⸻

Success also requires examination.

A sound decision may encounter an unfavorable turn. A poor decision may escape its expected cost.

The result supplies evidence, while the path to the result supplies further evidence.

The Strategist asks what the method contributed, what assistance the circumstances provided, and what might happen under a modest change in conditions.

Good fortune is received with gratitude.

Its contribution is remembered when the method is used again.

⸻

Comparison makes some lessons visible.

The Strategist keeps account of what was attempted, what was changed, and what followed.

Where several things change together, their separate effects may remain uncertain.

Where a useful comparison is possible, it is preserved.

Memory should retain the conditions of an outcome alongside the outcome itself.

A remembered victory without its circumstances can become a misleading instruction.

The record gives later judgment something firm to revisit.

⸻

In studying another’s success, the Strategist looks for the judgments that the visible movement cannot fully display.

What difference was noticed?
What alternative was passed over?
What preparation made the chosen action available?

Imitation may reproduce the shape while omitting the capacity that gave the shape its use.

The Strategist learns which capacities can be practiced and which conditions must be established.

What appears effortless at the end may have required many corrections at the beginning.

⸻

Instruction directs attention.

Practice changes what attention can distinguish.

At first, the learner must remember each part. Later, familiar parts can be performed together, leaving judgment free to meet the unexpected.

The Strategist cultivates this freedom.

A well learned method carries ordinary demands with less effort. Continued attention discovers when the occasion has exceeded the method.

Skill becomes dependable through repetition and responsive through variation.

Both belong to the training.

⸻

A lesson has a range of usefulness.

What succeeds on firm ground may require alteration on yielding ground. What works among trusted companions may depend upon understandings absent elsewhere.

The Strategist carries experience into a new encounter and examines its fit.

A resemblance can suggest where to look.

The new circumstances determine what can be carried across.

The question may travel farther than the answer first obtained from it.

⸻

The adversary learns also.

Repeated movements reveal preferences. Successful arrangements invite study. A former surprise may become an expected occurrence.

The Strategist attends to these changes.

A useful method may require another application as the opponent’s understanding develops. An effective response may remain effective even after it is understood.

Novelty is judged through what it enables.

An unfamiliar movement that serves no purpose merely adds another uncertainty to the work.

⸻

Correction must enter the next performance.

The Strategist asks what will now be noticed earlier, what preparation will change, and what action will differ when the circumstance returns.

A repeated error may indicate that the lesson remains too vague, that practice has been insufficient, or that the arrangement continues producing the same difficulty.

The correction is then carried deeper.

Knowledge becomes part of capability when it changes what can reliably be done.

The undertaking should benefit from having been encountered before.

⸻

The Master Strategist returns from each encounter with a more exact understanding of the work.

Some expectations are strengthened. Some are narrowed. Some are relinquished. New questions disclose the next place where practice is needed.

The accumulated method remains open to the material before it.

A familiar task is approached with the knowledge of earlier attempts and the attention owed to this one.

The grain differs.

The practiced hand can feel the difference.




X. The Hidden Mechanism

The Strategist asks how an outcome is made and how it is renewed.

A visible movement may be the last link in a long preparation. A recurring difficulty may be sustained by arrangements that remain untouched whenever the difficulty is addressed.

The wheel turns where it can be seen.

The current reaches it through a channel.

The Strategist follows the connection.

⸻

A mechanism may be concealed by its ordinariness.

One person follows a custom. Another fulfills an obligation. A third responds to an expectation established long before.

Together, these actions may produce a result that none has considered as a whole.

The Strategist examines how the actions support, constrain, and provoke one another.

The pattern can be distributed among many participants.

Understanding it requires following the relations through which their separate conduct becomes a common consequence.

⸻

Where the same result appears under different participants, the arrangement deserves attention.

Which actions are rewarded?
Which costs fall elsewhere?
Which reports are welcomed?
Which corrections have difficulty reaching those who can act?

An arrangement that rewards favorable appearances may gradually lose access to unfavorable knowledge.

The Strategist studies what the participants must do to retain their positions within it.

Repeated conduct often reveals the demands of the arrangement.

⸻

Some consequences return to strengthen their own causes.

Suspicion may produce restrictions. Restrictions may encourage concealment. Concealment may then be taken as further reason for suspicion.

The Strategist traces the returning path.

At what point does a response begin renewing the condition it was meant to contain?

A stronger response along the same path may increase the difficulty.

Where the returning connection can be altered, the pattern may acquire another course.

⸻

Beneficial patterns can also sustain themselves.

Practice improves performance. Improved performance makes further practice useful. Shared reliability reduces the effort required for cooperation, allowing more work to be undertaken together.

The Strategist studies what nourishes these continuations.

A useful arrangement may depend upon modest acts repeated faithfully: accurate reports, maintained instruments, fulfilled commitments, timely rest.

Their quiet recurrence can carry more strength than an occasional display of brilliance.

What sustains the pattern deserves provision.

⸻

Intention and effect must each be investigated.

A rule meant to preserve order may conceal the information needed to maintain it. An adversary’s deliberate action may produce consequences beyond the adversary’s expectation.

Those who benefit from an arrangement may have designed it, inherited it, or discovered its advantages after it arose.

The Strategist traces responsibility through what was known, chosen, and done.

Benefit supplies a question.

The circumstances determine the account.

⸻

A recurring difficulty may have several supports.

Removing one may weaken it. Another may restore it. A third may become important only after the first has disappeared.

The Strategist examines how the supports work together.

A repair that reaches only the visible damage may leave its source active.

The water must be cleared from the room; the place where it enters must also be found.

Immediate relief and lasting correction may require different work.

⸻

Names can make mechanisms easier to recognize.

To speak of dependence directs attention toward what one action requires from another. To speak of reinforcement directs attention toward consequences that strengthen an ongoing pattern.

The Strategist uses names that help the eye return to the relevant relationship.

A useful name permits another observer to find the connection and examine whether it operates here.

Language becomes an instrument of inquiry when it makes distinctions available for use.

The name remains answerable to the encounter.

⸻

An explanation should guide expectation.

If a particular condition sustains an outcome, changing that condition should make some discernible difference.

The Strategist asks what difference is expected and looks for it.

Where the result differs from the expectation, the account is revised. Where several explanations remain possible, their disagreement can indicate the next useful observation.

An elegant account acquires weight through the work it enables and the scrutiny it can endure.

⸻

Understanding a mechanism opens several possibilities.

It may be strengthened, redirected, supplemented, or allowed to cease.

The Strategist chooses according to the purpose and the consequences of the alteration.

A connection that creates vulnerability may also carry necessary assistance. A custom that obstructs change may preserve knowledge that must be carried into the new arrangement.

The work reaches further when it understands what must continue through the correction.

⸻

The Master Strategist seeks the places where an undertaking repeatedly gains or loses its power.

There, a limited action may alter a long continuation.

The result is followed through the next cycle. What once returned is observed again. The correction is judged through what the changed arrangement now produces.

An obstacle may cease to renew itself.

The strength formerly spent upon it becomes available for another purpose.




XI. Authorship

A contest may begin in the naming of its participants.

One is appointed judge. Another is required to prove worth. One defines the question. Another is permitted only to answer.

The Strategist examines the powers and obligations carried by these arrangements.

Who has chosen the purpose?
Who determines what counts as completion?
Which choices remain available to each participant?

An assigned position can shape the encounter before any visible movement occurs.

⸻

A role can make useful work possible.

The guide knows the passage. The keeper maintains the provisions. The messenger carries an account between distant parties.

The Strategist accepts responsibilities through an understanding of their scope.

A guide may lead the crossing while the travelers retain their purposes.

Clarity about the work protects both cooperation and freedom.

Where the obligations of a role continually expand, the agreement that established it must be examined again.

⸻

An adversary may offer choices that preserve the same advantage whichever is selected.

The Strategist studies what these choices assume.

Must the proposed contest take place? Must it be decided by this measure? Can the underlying purpose be served through another undertaking?

Sometimes the offered choice contains the only practicable passage. Sometimes another course remains available.

Judgment requires discovering which circumstance is present.

Freedom grows where a real alternative can be prepared.

⸻

Repeatedly answering an assigned description can make that description govern the work.

Those called reckless may spend their strength displaying caution. Those called weak may accept needless trials of strength.

The Strategist returns to the requirements of the undertaking.

Where an account is mistaken and consequential, correction may be necessary. Where further argument merely renews the demand for performance, useful conduct can proceed beyond the argument.

The purpose supplies a measure that the accusation cannot continually replace.

⸻

Authorship becomes substantial through action.

An intention directs preparation. A commitment places resources at stake. A decision changes what others can expect.

The Strategist attends to these consequences.

To choose a course is also to accept responsibility for examining what the course produces.

Where the result departs from the intention, the departure enters the reckoning.

An undertaking gains direction through decisions that remain answerable to their effects.

The account of the work must be able to receive correction from the work itself.

⸻

The size of a contribution may differ from its visibility.

A brief question can redirect a long preparation. A quiet refusal can prevent an unsuitable commitment. A small clarification can allow many independent efforts to proceed together.

The Strategist traces contributions through what they made possible.

Length of speech, prominence of position, and abundance of activity each reveal only part of the matter.

Some contributions establish the conditions under which the more visible work succeeds.

Their place in the account belongs to those consequences.

⸻

A completed movement can make its beginning newly intelligible.

What seemed incidental may prove important. An interruption may have disclosed the useful route. A failed attempt may have supplied the distinction upon which later success depended.

The Strategist revisits the record while preserving the difference between what was intended then and what is understood now.

Discovery retains its place in the history.

A coherent account can include surprise.

The path may become clear through having been traveled.

⸻

Others bring purposes of their own.

The Strategist seeks agreements through which those purposes can contribute to a shared undertaking.

What is settled is made clear. What remains open is left available for judgment. A contribution that changes the plan is examined for what it adds.

The work can acquire intelligence through this participation.

Those who help shape an undertaking can notice possibilities that a fixed assignment would leave outside their attention.

Shared authorship enlarges the sources from which useful direction can arise.

⸻

Credit also influences what can happen next.

Acknowledging a contribution helps preserve the knowledge of where assistance came from and how it became effective.

It may strengthen trust, make further cooperation possible, and allow another participant’s skill to develop.

The Strategist gives an accurate account of the work.

Exaggerated independence conceals real dependencies. Indiscriminate praise conceals important differences.

A faithful record helps future judgment find the actual sources of strength.

⸻

A role may outlive the circumstances that made it useful.

A rival can remain the center of attention after the rivalry has ceased to serve any purpose. A reputation can demand performances whose costs exceed their value.

The Strategist keeps the undertaking capable of surviving the end of the contest.

What has been learned can continue into another form of work.

An adversary’s departure need not leave the purpose empty.

There must be something worth doing when opposition no longer supplies the direction.

⸻

The Master Strategist takes responsibility for the choices within reach and recognizes the contributions through which that reach became possible.

The record is kept open to correction. The undertaking remains open to discovery. Others retain room to bring their own judgment to what is shared.

What has occurred is recorded faithfully.

What follows remains a field of action.




XII. Transformation

The Strategist considers what must become possible for the encounter to proceed differently.

Some difficulties persist because a necessary capacity is absent. Others persist because an old habit repeatedly restores the same limitation.

Transformation changes these conditions.

What once demanded the whole reserve may become an ordinary task. What once compelled a single response may admit several. What once required continual opposition may become unnecessary.

The change is known through what can now be done.

⸻

The intended transformation must acquire a workable form.

“Greater strength” leaves many questions unanswered.

Strength for which undertaking? Endurance across what duration? Freedom from which dependency? Judgment under what pressure?

The Strategist gives the intention enough definition to guide practice.

As the work reveals more of its requirements, the design is refined.

A clear beginning can lead toward a destination understood more fully through the journey.

Direction and discovery develop together.

⸻

Development includes acquisition and release.

A new skill may require room formerly occupied by an old habit. A more useful arrangement may require relinquishing a position that once provided security.

The Strategist examines what each existing practice has served.

What remains necessary is preserved or provided for in another way. What has ceased to serve can be set down.

A habit deserves understanding even when it must be changed.

Its earlier usefulness may reveal the need that the new arrangement must still meet.

⸻

Capacities develop in relation to one another.

Greater speed requires judgment able to direct it. Greater reach requires provision capable of sustaining it. Greater influence requires attention to consequences that were formerly beyond reach.

The Strategist examines the development of the whole undertaking.

An improvement that overwhelms its companions creates another imbalance.

Strength becomes more dependable when perception, means, restraint, and renewal can support its use.

The work of transformation includes their changing proportions.

⸻

The material participates in the design.

A weaver discovers what the threads will bear through working with them. Their differences influence the pattern that can be made.

The Strategist proceeds with similar attention.

An unexpected aptitude may open another route. A persistent difficulty may reveal that the proposed form requires revision. A relationship may disclose possibilities absent from the original plan.

These discoveries enter the work.

The design gains precision through contact with what is being transformed.

⸻

During change, former supports may weaken before new ones become dependable.

The Strategist preserves enough continuity for the transition to be sustained.

Useful routines are retained where they carry necessary work. New practices are exercised where their limitations can be discovered and corrected.

A single successful performance begins the inquiry into reliability.

The new capacity must learn to operate through interruption, fatigue, and changing circumstances.

Transformation acquires depth when it can continue beyond the conditions of its first appearance.

⸻

In an unequal encounter, development can alter which disadvantages remain decisive.

The many may have prepared against a familiar limitation. When that limitation is overcome, their preparations may require revision.

The Strategist develops capacities that make fewer concessions necessary and more courses practicable.

The value of the change rests in its use.

A newly available action must still be fitted to the field, the purpose, and the responses it is likely to meet.

Greater capability enlarges the work of judgment.

⸻

Transformation also occurs between participants.

A clearer agreement may reduce recurring suspicion. A shared skill may distribute a burden once carried by one. A dependable means of correction may allow a difficult cooperation to continue.

The Strategist attends to these changes.

An undertaking can become more capable because its relationships have developed.

What one participant learns may improve the conditions under which another can learn.

Growth can travel through the connections that make the work possible.

⸻

The method of development can itself develop.

The Strategist learns which kinds of practice reveal a weakness, which comparisons clarify a result, and which arrangements help a correction endure.

Each well examined encounter can improve the next inquiry.

A growing repertoire includes ways of discovering what is still missing.

Thus transformation can leave behind a more capable means of further transformation.

The work gains the ability to renew its own instruction.

⸻

A changed capacity may make an earlier ambition less important.

A contest once considered decisive may lose its hold when another means of fulfilling the purpose becomes available.

The Strategist allows this discovery its consequence.

Development need not be spent upon repeating the old encounter at a greater scale.

The new capacity may support another kind of undertaking, another relationship, or a freedom whose value becomes apparent only after it is available.

Growth can change what is worth pursuing.

⸻

The Master Strategist brings intention, practice, release, and revision into a continuing relationship.

What is gained is tested in use. What is preserved continues to support the work. What is relinquished leaves room for another possibility.

The transformation becomes evident in conduct.

An old pressure arrives.

A distinction is recognized sooner. A habitual reaction gives way to a chosen movement. Strength remains available for what follows.

The familiar challenge is met.

Another response is now possible.




XIII. What Remains

At the beginning, the Strategist asks what must be preserved.

At the end, the question returns.

What strength remains?
Which relationships can still bear trust?
What obligations continue?
What has become possible beyond the original undertaking?

The result is examined together with the conditions it leaves behind.

A favorable outcome acquires lasting value through the life and work it can support.

⸻

Completion must be recognized through the purpose.

A dispute may continue after the necessary work is accomplished. An adversary may withhold acknowledgment. Observers may disagree about the meaning of the result.

The Strategist examines what still requires action.

Unfulfilled obligations deserve attention. Continuing dangers require proportionate provision. Questions of interpretation may remain open.

The ending of an undertaking need not await the ending of every account told about it.

Its boundaries should be clear enough to permit an end.

⸻

What has been gained carries a cost of keeping.

A position may require continued defense. An agreement may require renewal. A new capability may require practice and provision.

The Strategist reckons these demands before fastening the future to the result.

What deserves its care is maintained. What has ceased to serve may be relinquished.

A possession that consumes all available freedom has become a governing obligation.

The value of the gain includes what remains free after its necessities are met.

⸻

Measures suited to danger must be reconsidered when the danger changes.

Vigilance may remain useful while its former intensity becomes exhausting. A temporary restriction may have fulfilled its purpose. A habit of immediate response may interfere with work that now requires patience.

The Strategist revisits these arrangements.

What continues is given a present reason. What can be released returns strength to ordinary activity.

The field after the encounter deserves to be observed afresh.

Preparation for conflict must leave room for another manner of living.

⸻

The burdens of the undertaking must be brought into view.

Tools may need repair. Provisions may need replenishment. Those who carried sustained responsibility may need relief.

The Strategist attends to this work before calling the remaining strength abundant.

An exhausted companion is part of the reckoning. So is a neglected obligation or a damaged trust.

Restoration gives the result somewhere sound to rest.

The means that carried the undertaking deserve care when their visible service is finished.

⸻

The adversary also enters the aftermath.

Where a workable settlement is possible, the Strategist considers what would allow it to endure.

An agreement gains stability when those affected can continue their lives without continually undoing it.

Where hostility persists, necessary protections remain. Their scope is judged through the conditions they address.

The Strategist avoids making the future depend upon another’s perpetual humiliation.

A lasting result should require as little renewed conflict as circumstances permit.

⸻

The record should preserve what future judgment will need.

The purpose as it was understood.
The conditions under which choices were made.
The assistance received.
The errors corrected.
The questions left unresolved.

The Strategist records these alongside the result.

Those who come later should be able to distinguish a useful method from favorable circumstance and a deliberate preparation from a discovery made along the way.

An honest account allows experience to become instruction.

⸻

Useful knowledge should be able to travel.

The Strategist makes the work sufficiently understandable for others to practice, examine, and adapt it.

A method grows more durable when its correction can continue beyond the presence of its first practitioner.

Those who inherit it will meet different ground.

They need enough understanding to recognize what must change and what still gives the method its power.

The teaching succeeds when another can exercise judgment where the original instructions end.

⸻

The undertaking may leave a wider company than the one with which it began.

A former stranger may have become a dependable companion. A solitary skill may have become a shared practice. A common difficulty may have disclosed a purpose several can serve.

The Strategist preserves room for these developments.

Those who worked together may continue together or take different paths.

The value of the cooperation includes the capacities each can now carry onward.

A shared passage can open toward several destinations.

⸻

There is also a time to lay the work down.

Unused strength need not seek another contest. An available path need not be taken merely because it is open.

The Strategist permits rest, ordinary pleasure, and attention to matters that the undertaking had placed at a distance.

Freedom includes the ability to leave capability unused until a worthy purpose calls for it.

A life preserved through strategy should have room beyond the practice of strategy.

⸻

The Master Strategist provides for what must continue and releases what has completed its service.

Knowledge remains available. Necessary care has a place. Others retain the freedom to contribute, depart, and begin.

The next purpose can arise from the circumstances of another day.

The work is complete.

The way remains open.





APPENDICES


APPENDIX A
THE FIVE ELEMENTS: CAPACITIES AND MODES

The five elements give a compact language for two related aspects of strategic action. Capacities concern what the Actor can bring to an encounter. Modes concern the manner in which the Actor meets it. The same elemental names connect these registers while allowing each to retain its meaning.

A cultivated capacity can remain available before its occasion arrives. A mode becomes visible in the present response. Their meeting requires circumstances in which the capacity can be used, a purpose worth serving, and judgment of what the response makes possible.

Element | Capacity | Mode | A relation between them
Fire | Experience | Observing | Clear perception allows encounters to become occasions of learning.
Wind | Technique | Spiraling | Acquired forms become available for return, variation, and redirection.
Water | Adaptation | Adapting | Readiness for adjustment becomes a response fitted to changing conditions.
Earth | Understanding | Grounding | Knowledge of dependencies informs the support an act requires.
Space | Awareness | Emptying | Release of a fixed demand leaves more of the field available to awareness.

The table gives correspondences, not exclusive assignments. Every mode can draw upon every capacity. Observing may require a practiced technique. Grounding may require adaptation. Emptying may depend upon experience of what a claim has continued to cost.

The element names preserve these relations in memorable images. Their usefulness rests in the distinctions they make available to judgment.

THE FIVE CAPACITIES

Fire — Experience

Experience brings the Actor into contact with conditions and consequences. Some encounters are sought; others arrive without invitation. Each supplies particulars from which learning may become possible.

The event, its remembered account, and the lesson drawn from it deserve separate attention. A successful outcome can depend upon another person's help, favorable circumstances, or a danger that did not arise. A failure can reveal unsuitable means, a missing condition, or inadequate performance. These differences affect what can reasonably be carried into another encounter.

Experience includes what the act was like to receive. The person acting and the person affected may possess different parts of its history. Their accounts can correct one another without becoming interchangeable.

Repeated encounters supply repeated opportunities for learning. Correction determines whether the next judgment actually changes. Experience becomes more useful when the relation discovered in one encounter can be recognized, questioned, and revised in another.

Wind — Technique

Technique is a cultivated means of performing an act. A form becomes dependable through the coordination it requires, the conditions that support it, and the judgment that recognizes its occasion.

Fluency can release attention from familiar demands. That attention becomes available for changes in the field. A wider repertoire also makes alternatives available, provided the Practitioner can distinguish their purposes and limits.

Technique includes the ability to alter or stop a movement. The completion of a practiced form has value through the work it serves. A technically exact performance can still impose an unsuitable cost or continue after its purpose has ended.

Knowledge of a method and the means to perform it are distinct. Access, materials, maintenance, assistance, and bodily condition can determine whether a known method is presently available. The Strategist reckons these conditions with the skill.

Water — Adaptation

Adaptation is the capacity to meet changing conditions through a fitting change in conduct. It may alter method, position, pace, reach, or the organization of an undertaking. It can also allow correction to reach the aim itself.

The extent of the change should answer to the relations producing the difficulty. An adjustment that preserves an injurious arrangement may extend the injury. A broad alteration can disturb goods that a more precise change would have preserved.

Adaptation therefore includes judgment about what deserves to remain dependable. Commitments enter the situation alongside new demands. Those whose contributions are affected need enough understanding and means to meet a changed course.

The capacity has costs as well as possibilities. Repeated adjustment consumes attention, preparation, and trust. Responsiveness can take the form of maintaining a boundary when the demand for change would undermine the purpose or the shared conditions of action.

Earth — Understanding

Understanding joins particulars through causes, conditions, dependencies, and limits. It makes an account useful by revealing how a difference in one relation could change another.

The account includes both its grounds and the point at which those grounds end. Events occurring together may have a common source. One event following another may establish a sequence while leaving the causal passage uncertain. The distinction matters when action depends upon an explanation.

Understanding also includes the Actor's place in what is being understood. A question, an institutional position, a promised reward, or the cost of answering can help shape the response received. The Observer participates in the field from which knowledge is drawn.

Different accounts may disclose parts of a larger relation. Their meeting can produce correction, a more complete explanation, or a clearer statement of what remains unresolved. The act can proceed from sufficient understanding while keeping its uncertain grounds available for later revision.

Space — Awareness

Awareness keeps the surrounding field available while attention selects an object within it. It includes sensitivity to what concentration has placed at the edge: a changed condition, another person's contribution, or the influence of an expectation upon perception.

Attention is finite. Awareness does not remove that limit; it supports the ability to recognize the limit and turn when the situation calls for another view. Concentration can then remain both useful and revisable.

The unfamiliar need not be assigned its meaning before it has been received. A pattern may help orient judgment, while an exception reveals where the pattern ceases to explain. Fear, fatigue, and practiced answers can themselves become objects of awareness.

Others may see effects and preferences concealed from the present position. Room for their contribution keeps the Actor's account open to correction. The field becomes more available when the current view leaves an entrance for what could change it.

THE FIVE MODES

Fire — Observing

Observing brings attention to what is occurring, the account formed of it, and the Actor's participation in its occurrence. It distinguishes what is witnessed, what is inferred, and what remains unknown.

Observation can reveal a forming reaction while another response is still possible. It can also disclose the conditions supporting a recurring pattern and the exception that marks its limits.

The act of observing may itself alter the field. A question directs attention; a pause changes rhythm; an exploratory movement elicits a response. These effects belong within what is learned.

Observing serves judgment through information that can affect the next act. Its duration must be considered alongside the opportunity, obligation, and uncertainty within which action proceeds.

Wind — Spiraling

Spiraling joins return with change. The Actor revisits a question, relation, or undertaking after experience or circumstances have altered what can be seen and done. It also names the turn through which a useful connection allows existing movement to enter another course.

The connection deserves preservation for the work it makes possible. A familiar circuit can deepen understanding, and it can deepen a difficulty. The direction of development appears in what the return preserves, changes, and leaves available.

A spiral can reach the aim as well as the method. Returning may reveal that the destination has ceased to deserve pursuit. Memory makes the difference recognizable; openness allows the difference to matter.

When the work of return is complete, departure becomes part of the movement.

Water — Adapting

Adapting is the present fitting of a response to changing conditions. Adaptation as a capacity supplies readiness; Adapting as a mode gives that readiness its occasion and expression.

The response may change its method, pace, reach, or organization. It receives the changed situation while examining how present action contributes to what follows. A condition can be met closely enough to alter the process that continually renews it.

Grounding gives these adjustments direction. The response remains accountable to the lives, freedoms, and relationships it affects. What others need to rely upon deserves to remain intelligible through the change.

Correction becomes adaptation when it reaches conduct. The changed account must find its way into the act.

Earth — Grounding

Grounding gives a response presence, steadiness, and dependable support. Its forms include bodily support, continuity of attention, sustainable provision, and commitments upon which others can act.

The Grounding Principle supplies the motivating perspective of actual shared belonging. It places self and other within the life the act must preserve. The mode of Grounding concerns how a response is supported and carried. The principle gives direction to that support; the mode helps make the directed act possible.

Grounding can require movement. A change of position may preserve perception, a revised agreement may restore reliability, and assistance may sustain a capacity that one person cannot maintain alone.

Steadiness receives correction while remaining available. A boundary requires means, a promise requires provision, and an invitation requires room for another person's answer.

Space — Emptying

Emptying releases a claim, preoccupation, attachment, or completed form whose continued occupation limits the next fitting response. Space names the room made available; Void also names this openness in the elemental language.

A demanded result can be loosened while responsibility for the act continues. A defended image can be relinquished while protection remains. Release becomes meaningful through what is released and what remains worthy of care.

Silence can preserve preparation, allow thought, or make room for another voice. Its function depends upon the occasion. An answer owed to someone who must act carries obligations that silence does not dissolve.

Emptying remains attentive to what enters the opened space. The capacity to release and the capacity to receive belong together.

COMBINATION AND TRANSITION

The five modes can combine within one act, and their prominence can change as the situation develops. Observation may reveal the need for a boundary. Dependable support may make a change possible. Release of a fixed answer may allow observation to become clearer.

These relations do not impose a compulsory cycle. The fitting transition depends upon what the current mode permits, what it obstructs, and what the developing situation requires. A mode may continue while its object changes; another mode may come forward without erasing the work already accomplished.

Every one of the twelve movements remains available in every mode. Observing can expand its field or contract its focus. Grounding can turn. Emptying can release a demand while holding a boundary. The movement's object, conditions, and purpose determine its particular work.

A mode can become an obstacle when it continues beyond its occasion. Adaptation can spend the commitments that made cooperation possible. Grounding can harden around an obsolete arrangement. Observation can prolong indecision. Spiraling can repeat an unexamined difficulty. Emptying can leave an obligation unattended. The other capacities and modes make correction possible.

THE ELEMENTS IN COMPOSITION

The elemental relations can also clarify the language of shared composition. Attentive listening, a dependable reference, a flowing line, variation through return, and a meaningful interval each contribute something different to the whole.

Fire — Observing brings the clarity of listening: distinguishing a contribution, its surroundings, and the response it invites.

Wind — Spiraling brings the turn and the variation: a recognizable line returns with its relation to the whole changed.

Water — Adapting brings flow: the line adjusts its passage as the other contributions develop.

Earth — Grounding brings a dependable reference: distinct contributions can move while retaining a relation through which they remain intelligible together.

Space — Emptying brings silence and rest: an interval makes room for another contribution or allows the completed phrase to end.

These correspondences express relationships within composition. Each can assist the others, and the same musical expression can carry several at once. A shared reference permits variation; listening permits adjustment; a rest changes the rhythm. Their meeting gives differences a way to contribute without requiring every voice to perform the same movement.

DOMAIN, SCALE, AND PURPOSE

The elements remain available across the domains of an undertaking. A bodily act, a relation of trust, a distribution of resources, and a pattern of attention have different means and conditions even when a similar strategic relation appears within them.

The scale of action also changes what a capacity requires. Individual understanding can contribute to a shared undertaking through communication, authority, provision, and the ability of others to correct it. Dependable collective action needs arrangements through which these contributions can meet. An elemental name does not supply those arrangements.

The framework helps distinguish what has been cultivated, how it is being expressed, and what still needs support. The principles give these capacities and modes their direction and limits. Their value appears in the conduct they make possible and the life that conduct preserves.


APPENDIX B
THE TWELVE MOVEMENTS

The movements name ways in which an act, relation, or course can change. Their meaning becomes clear through the object that changes, the purpose of the change, the conditions that permit it, and the consequences that follow.

The same movement can serve different purposes. Expanding attention, expanding an obligation, and expanding another person's freedom alter different parts of a situation. Holding can sustain care or preserve an injury. Releasing can restore freedom or leave necessary work without support.

The movement therefore enters judgment together with its object and manner. Its name alone cannot determine its worth.

The twelve movements are arranged in six pairs:

    Bending and Turning — the course of movement.
    Rotating and Spinning — movement around a reference.
    Expanding and Contracting — the extent of movement.
    Holding and Releasing — the continuance of a relation.
    Accelerating and Decelerating — the pace of movement.
    Stopping and Going — the continuation of movement.

The first two pairs distinguish related forms of change. The other pairs draw attention to contrasting directions or conditions. Several movements can occur together, especially when their objects, scales, or times differ. The repertoire gives distinctions through which further differences can become visible; its usefulness remains open to correction.

1. BENDING AND TURNING

Bending alters a path continuously. The course changes through its passage, with each alteration contributing to the direction that follows. Its continuity can preserve connections that a sudden change would strain.

Turning changes a heading. The new direction becomes the reference for the next part of the course. A turn can occur gradually or swiftly; its defining question concerns where the action is now directed.

These movements can overlap. A bending course changes heading as it continues. The distinction directs attention either to the continuity of alteration or to the direction adopted. It becomes useful when the manner of changing course matters as much as the destination.

An alteration of means may preserve the purpose. Further understanding may show that the purpose itself requires revision. In either case, commitments already made remain part of the judgment. Those moving together require enough shared understanding and support to meet the change.

Flexibility preserves its strategic value through what it makes possible, what it prevents, and what it carries safely through the alteration.

2. ROTATING AND SPINNING

Rotating means changing position around a center. The center supplies a reference while the position changes. Different positions disclose different relations to what remains central.

Spinning means turning around an axis. The axis organizes the turning, while different aspects of the turning form meet their surroundings.

These are working distinctions within the strategic vocabulary. In ordinary descriptions of motion, the words can overlap. Here their separation keeps attention on the reference around which the movement occurs and on what changes through that movement.

A shared purpose can remain recognizable while participants change positions, responsibilities, or perspectives. The continuity depends on what they actually understand in common. Different assumed centers may conceal different undertakings beneath coordinated appearances.

Revolution also permits recurrence. Returning around a center can disclose what changed during the circuit. It can equally restore the same difficulty if the relations sustaining that difficulty remain intact.

The value of the circuit appears in the understanding, capacity, or conditions it leaves changed. Motion around a reference requires judgment about the reference itself.

3. EXPANDING AND CONTRACTING

Expanding increases extent. The object may be reach, scope, attention, participation, exposure, provision, or available possibility. Contracting decreases such an extent and changes the relations gathered within it.

Each movement alters demands as well as appearance. Wider reach can require more knowledge, coordination, maintenance, and relief. Reduced reach can concentrate capacity, diminish exposure, or leave an obligation to be carried elsewhere.

The affected boundary must therefore be identified. An undertaking can contract while its field of concern expands. A demand can narrow while the range of acceptable responses widens. These movements cooperate because they act upon different objects.

Peace gives their direction a purpose: enlarge practicable possibilities for protection, cooperation, and shared life; restrict the routes through which harm proceeds. The freedom of the person remains within the reckoning when a harmful undertaking loses its means.

Expansion becomes sustainable through the support that can accompany it. Contraction becomes responsible through attention to what remains outside the reduced scope. Both require an account of who gains room, who bears additional work, and what the new arrangement can continue to sustain.

4. HOLDING AND RELEASING

Holding maintains something through time. It may preserve a commitment, a boundary, a reference, a resource, or a space in which another can act. Continuance can require adjustment, replenishment, repair, and movement.

Releasing ends or loosens a hold. Its object may be an exhausted form, a claim upon another, an attachment to a particular outcome, or a restriction whose purpose has been fulfilled.

The pair concerns the relation maintained. Holding a worthy purpose can accompany the release of its former method. Continuing protection can accompany the relinquishment of hostility. Care can remain present as possession ends.

A release also has consequences for those who depended upon what was held. The work may be complete; it may still require provision; it may pass to another willing participant. The judgment includes the knowledge, means, and time needed for continuity where continuity remains necessary.

Holding becomes rigid when its object is preserved after the reason for preserving it has failed. Releasing becomes abandonment when necessary care disappears without regard for those left to bear the loss. Purpose, present conditions, and remaining obligations distinguish the fitting movement.

5. ACCELERATING AND DECELERATING

Accelerating increases the pace of a specified process. Decelerating decreases it. Pace has meaning in relation to the event, capacity, or condition with which the process must meet.

Several processes can proceed at different rates while belonging to one undertaking. Perception, consent, preparation, communication, action, and recovery have their own requirements. A gain in one can create pressure elsewhere when the connections between them are overlooked.

Acceleration can bring an act within an opening that would otherwise close. Its cost can include reduced time for observation, agreement, correction, or restoration. Deceleration can permit a missing capacity to arrive or a relation to become clear. Its interval acquires value through what develops during it.

Speed is therefore judged across the undertaking. The immediate act may finish sooner while the work of correction and recovery grows. A slower beginning may permit a more dependable continuation.

Pace can also change expectation. Variation is fitting where it interrupts a harmful pattern or creates a worthwhile possibility. Dependable timing remains valuable where others have ordered their own commitments around it.

The fitting rate joins purpose, readiness, opportunity, and the responses of those concerned.

6. STOPPING AND GOING

Stopping ends a movement or suspends its continuation. Going begins, resumes, or carries a movement onward. Each changes the commitments through which the following state becomes possible.

A pause suspends activity while preserving the conditions for return. Completion ends an undertaking whose purpose has been fulfilled. Withdrawal ends a particular manner of participation. These can resemble one another outwardly while leaving different obligations behind.

Going likewise has more than one moment. Beginning requires a practicable first movement and support for what it commits. Resumption requires renewed attention to conditions that may have changed during the pause. Continuation requires a present reason, even when momentum makes it easy.

The absence of visible movement may still require active maintenance. Other processes continue while one process stops. Necessary support, protection, and communication can remain in motion through a pause or ending.

The strategic question includes what the cessation permits, what it interrupts, and what it leaves unfinished. Completion can release attention for restoration. A well-prepared beginning can preserve room for revision before commitment becomes difficult to reverse.

THE MOVEMENTS AND THE FIVE MODES

Modes describe manners of engagement; movements describe changes within them. Their relationships are many-to-many.

Adapting often brings bending, expansion, and renewed going into prominence. Grounding often brings holding and movement around a dependable reference into prominence. Spiraling brings turning, recurrence, and altered understanding into view. Emptying often involves contraction, release, and the cessation of an exhausted response. Observing makes the changing relations available to attention.

These associations indicate emphases. Every movement remains available in every mode. Adapting may require a stop. Grounding may require a changed boundary. Observing may widen or narrow attention, alter its pace, and return to an earlier view. Spiraling may hold a question steady. Emptying may create the room from which a new movement begins.

Seeing belongs to the activity of Observing. It does not add a thirteenth movement to this repertoire. The movements of attention can themselves be examined through the same distinctions of direction, extent, pace, and continuance.

COMPOSITION AND QUALITY

An act can hold, turn, and decelerate at once. Successive acts can prepare, change, preserve, and release different relations. Their combination becomes intelligible through the objects they affect and the dependencies joining them.

Opposed movements can cooperate when their objects differ. They can also alternate as conditions change. A demand for expansion in every respect or continuance at every moment removes the distinctions through which judgment becomes possible.

The Actor's practical reach also matters. Some changes can be made directly; others require another person's participation, permission, or decision. A movement imagined on behalf of another remains incomplete until the conditions of their agency are included.

Finally, the quality of movement includes attention, care, composure, responsiveness, and the ability to receive correction. A calm appearance does not establish what the act does to those who receive it. Their experience can disclose costs and possibilities unavailable from the acting position alone.

The movements give action a vocabulary. Purpose, relationships, and consequences give that vocabulary its meaning.


APPENDIX C
STRATEGIC GEOMETRY

Strategic geometry makes relationships visible through a small repertoire of forms. Position, reference, room, direction, boundary, return, transition, connection, order, and proportion become easier to distinguish when each receives a definite image.

The figures describe aspects of a situation. Several can be present together, and their usefulness depends on the relations they reveal. A geometric description gains strategic meaning through the people, means, permissions, commitments, and consequences it helps bring into view.

Mathematical relations retain their exact meaning. Their strategic use requires an account of what corresponds to the figure and how the relevant connection operates. A clear shape can organize attention while leaving the worth of the undertaking to judgment.

1. POINT AND CENTER

A point marks a position. A center supplies a reference through which other positions are understood. The two functions become strategically different when several centers are active in the same field.

The center of command may differ from the source of provision, the place of greatest vulnerability, or the purpose around which participants understand their work. Concentrating attention on one can obscure the others.

A center can also be a concern. Changing what stands foremost changes the meaning of approaching information. Correction may threaten a concern with vindication while assisting a concern with the work itself. The center directs what becomes salient and what counts as disturbance.

Positions provide partial views. A change of position, or the reception of another person's account, can disclose relations concealed from the first view. The reference must remain stable enough for comparison and revisable enough to receive what comparison reveals.

Centering gathers action around something worthy of continuance. Its stability depends upon the supports extending beyond the apparent center.

2. SPACE AND FIELD

Space names room in which a difference or movement can become possible. Its strategic forms include available time, attention, access, permission, resources, and the freedom to respond. Each requires its own supporting conditions.

The field is the arrangement of relationships through which these possibilities acquire practical force. Expectations, obligations, resistance, support, and the actions of others give the same apparent opening different meanings.

Point and space are complementary aspects of the Void: a reference from which relations can be perceived, and openness in which another configuration can emerge. The concentrated image and the extended image belong together in this vocabulary.

The field also contains ongoing work. What appears vacant may be occupied by care or maintenance that has become easy to overlook. A new use of space can consume another person's time or displace an existing obligation.

An opening becomes usable when its conditions can be reached and sustained. A pause can create room for a different response while other necessary processes continue around it.

3. LINE AND DIRECTION

A line joins positions and makes a direction visible. Strategically, it can express an aim, a connection, or the intended relation between a present situation and a possible destination.

An aim differs from the course by which it becomes reachable. The actual passage encounters changing ground, obligations, the purposes of others, and the need for support. Distance alone cannot determine the fitting route.

Direction is therefore considered with duration, exposure, provision, and the conditions of arrival. A course that preserves its original heading may cease to serve its original purpose. A change of route can preserve that purpose through changed circumstances.

Lines also meet. Their meeting may permit cooperation, require an order of passage, or disclose an incompatibility that changes the destination. Other people's purposes enter the reckoning as purposes in their own right.

The line makes intention legible. Judgment follows it far enough to consider whether the destination can sustain the life intended there.

4. CIRCLE AND BOUNDARY

A circle gathers points at a fixed distance from a center. Its strategic image gives attention to enclosure, continuity, recurrence, and the boundary through which something is preserved.

A boundary has a purpose. It may protect attention, commitments, rest, safety, or common work. Admission, delay, restriction, and refusal receive their meaning through the conditions required for that purpose to continue.

Maintaining a boundary takes work. Someone observes its condition, explains its terms, responds to pressure, and repairs what is damaged. Its apparent stability can depend upon continuing movement.

The field of consequence extends beyond the enclosure. People outside may supply its strength, absorb its costs, or live with what it excludes. Membership does not determine whose interests belong in the judgment.

A boundary can widen, narrow, move, open, or close. Its fitness changes with the conditions of what it protects. The shape is preserved wisely when its continued service remains visible.

5. SPIRAL AND RETURN

A spiral combines turning with a changing distance from a center. It gives a form to return under altered conditions: recurrence accompanied by a change of reach, understanding, capacity, or relation.

Circle and spiral distinguish kinds of recurrence. A circle represents a maintained circuit. A spiral represents change across the circuit. Either image can describe a useful or harmful process; direction and consequence must be identified.

Repetition establishes a path. Reflection examines its effects. Revision changes a later passage. These are distinct contributions to learning. Their combination gives a return the possibility of development.

The changing quantity must also be named. Influence may grow while trust declines. Skill may deepen while the range of accepted correction narrows. An outward movement in one respect gives no complete account of the whole.

Return can serve repair and completion as well as further development. Once the obligation has been fulfilled, ending the circuit may become the fitting change. The value of a spiral lies in what its returns make possible and what they cease to reproduce.

6. VERTEX AND THRESHOLD

A vertex marks a meeting of directions. A threshold marks a change in the conditions under which movement proceeds. They can coincide while directing attention to different aspects of the passage.

At a vertex, several histories, purposes, or paths may converge upon one decision. At a threshold, the terms governing what follows change: a commitment takes effect, an opening closes, a capacity becomes available, or a boundary is crossed.

The passage must be considered from both sides. What permits the crossing may differ from what sustains the position beyond it. Some support can accompany the movement; some may be lost; some may have to be established beforehand.

Irreversibility gives a transition additional weight. A limited, reversible movement can sometimes disclose conditions before a larger commitment is made. Other openings are brief and depend upon readiness already cultivated.

Another person's readiness retains its own conditions. Support and information can make a passage available while leaving the decision to cross with the person concerned.

7. TRIANGLE AND RELATIONSHIP

A triangle brings three positions and their connections into view. In strategic thought, a third term can reveal relations concealed when attention rests only on two opposing positions.

The third term may be another participant, a shared need, a common purpose, a sustaining condition, or an audience. Each changes the field through a different kind of connection. Naming the third term is the beginning of understanding what it contributes.

A connection must carry actual work. Shared dependence may permit cooperation. A trusted intermediary may make a meeting possible. An audience may change the cost of acknowledgment. These relations acquire their force through the expectations and capacities they contain.

The presence of a third also widens responsibility. An agreement between two can transfer burdens to someone absent from the agreement. The triangle brings that relation into the reckoning.

Mediation is successful to the extent that it supports the participants' own understanding and agency. The arrangement can then change as their direct relationship becomes capable of carrying more of the work.

8. SQUARE AND ORDER

A square gives the image of an enclosed, ordered field. Its strategic use concerns dependable relationships among distinct contributions, together with the boundaries and corrections through which the arrangement continues.

Purpose, responsibility, provision, and correction must be brought into a workable relationship. These are concerns to be accounted for; the figure does not determine a required number of participants, offices, stages, or duties.

A responsibility becomes practicable through knowledge, time, access, means, and appropriate discretion. An assignment without its supporting conditions can preserve the appearance of order while preventing the work it names.

Dependable arrangements make room for judgment. Participants can direct attention toward changing circumstances because some necessary conditions remain reliable. This reliability requires paths by which consequences can reach those able to alter the rules or provision.

Order also depends upon work that may be absent from its formal description. Receiving delay, repairing omissions, and restoring consumed capacity can sustain the visible structure. Their needs belong in the arrangement.

9. PROPORTION AND BALANCE

Proportion relates parts to one another and to a whole. Strategic proportion includes amount, placement, duration, sequence, and rate. A contribution must fit both the work and the other contributions with which it must meet.

The golden ratio names an exact relation. For positive parts a and b, with a greater than b,

    (a + b) / a = a / b = φ,
    where φ = (1 + √5) / 2.

The whole is to the larger part as the larger part is to the smaller. This equality determines the stated mathematical proportion. Decisions about the sharing of care, work, resources, authority, responsibility, or rest require their own grounds in purposes, needs, capacities, and consequences.

Balance may require unequal contributions when needs or responsibilities differ. Those who bear the result need a meaningful place in questioning the arrangement. A stable distribution can still preserve an unfair burden.

The measure also includes maintenance, recovery, and reserve. Provision fully consumed by the visible task may leave the conditions of its continuation unsupported. A margin preserves room for what could not be specified beforehand.

GEOMETRY IN COMBINATION

The figures direct attention to related aspects of one field. A center organizes a direction; a direction crosses a boundary; a threshold changes the conditions beyond it; relationships supply the means through which a new arrangement can hold. Recurrence reveals what that arrangement continues to produce.

Several descriptions may therefore be needed for the same situation. A boundary can be part of an ordered field, a site of decision, and a recurring demand upon those maintaining it. The distinct figures keep these relationships available without requiring that one image contain them all.

Geometric clarity serves choice when it makes the actual relations easier to perceive. The worth of the chosen movement remains answerable to the lives and possibilities it changes.


APPENDIX D
CONFIGURATION, MOMENTUM, AND TIME

The field changes while it is being understood. A description of position must therefore include what the position is becoming, what sustains that becoming, and where a choice can alter it.

1. Position, configuration, and potential

Position describes the situation from which a participant can act: reach, exposure, access, support, dependence, and the alternatives within practical reach.

Configuration describes the relations among participants, purposes, capacities, resources, boundaries, and circumstances. A change of position can alter the configuration; so can a change in agreement, expectation, permission, timing, or purpose without anyone changing physical place.

Strategic potential, or shih, is the capacity for effects arising through this arrangement. It includes what preparation, relationships, timing, and the movement already under way make possible. Potential becomes effective through actual means. An unused capacity, an inaccessible resource, and assistance arriving too late have different values from support present where it can serve.

The configuration has a history and a possible continuation. Its present appearance records neither completely.

2. State and continuation

A state is the condition of a process at a specified moment. Continuation is the passage through which further states are produced.

A stable state may require continuing effort. Replenishment can match expenditure; repeated care can preserve trust; maintenance can keep an arrangement usable. Apparent stillness can depend upon substantial movement.

A changing state may arise from an unchanged generating relation. A rule can keep producing new results. A repeated reward can invite further conduct. A dependency can keep renewing the same need under altered outward forms.

A generator is a condition or relation that helps produce the next state. Several generators may act together, and their relative influence may change. Discovering one does not establish that it alone governs the process.

An interruption can alter the present state while leaving the means of recurrence available. A deeper change reaches a sustaining relation, provides for necessary functions carried by it, and examines what the new arrangement subsequently produces.

3. Momentum and trajectory

Momentum is the strength and manner of continuation. It concerns what carries a process onward, how firmly that continuation is established, and what would sustain, weaken, or change it.

Trajectory is the course that continuation takes through time. It concerns direction, successive states, and the conditions toward which the process is moving.

Both are time-dependent aspects of the situation. A trajectory can turn while momentum grows. A course can retain its direction while its support declines. A visible pause can leave preparation for renewed movement intact. The same outward movement may therefore have different prospects at different times.

In human undertakings, continuation can be carried by repeated choice, habit, provision, commitment, institutional arrangements, shared expectations, or a rule generating further activity. These have different conditions. The concept of momentum helps inquire into their continuation without assigning them a common physical unit or a universal law of conservation.

Inertia, in this extended sense, names the tendency of a process to continue under the relations presently sustaining it. The continuation may preserve a state, a direction, a rate, or a rule of change. Its governing relation must be discovered within the process being considered.

The relevant question is what is likely to follow if the present generators and supports continue. That account remains conditional. Participants can choose differently; resources can fail; information can arrive; the act of observation can itself change the field.

4. What an intervention changes

An intervention enters a process already under way. Its meaning depends upon the aspect it reaches.

Interruption breaks a passage or suspends an operation. It may prevent immediate harm while leaving the source of recurrence in place.

Redirection changes a course through a changed relation. Its consequences include the conditions met along the new trajectory and the people affected there.

Acceleration and deceleration change a rate. Their effects depend upon provision, perception, coordination, and the pace at which other necessary processes can proceed.

Delay changes when processes meet. It may permit preparation or allow an opening to close. Its costs fall upon particular people and capacities.

Reconfiguration changes the relations through which choices and effects become possible. It may alter access, dependence, roles, incentives, boundaries, or the purpose of the undertaking.

One act can do several of these things. Their distinction helps identify which effects are intended, which are incidental, and which require continuing attention.

Stopping an effect and changing its generator can occur together. Where they do not, each has its own work to do.

5. Feedback, delay, and thresholds

Feedback occurs when an effect returns to influence what produced it. The return needs a path: information received, resources renewed, expectations changed, participation altered, or some other actual connection.

A returning effect can reinforce a movement, oppose it, stabilize a condition, or change its direction. Reinforcement and restraint describe functions within a circuit. Their value depends upon what the circuit sustains and whom it affects.

Delay separates an action from the appearance or return of its effects. Further interventions can accumulate while an earlier effect is still forming. A sound account includes what has begun without yet becoming visible.

A threshold is a condition at which the response changes. Several small increments can bring a process to a point where its previous behavior can no longer continue. Their combined effect may differ sharply from what the earlier increments appeared to do.

A small intervention can have a wide consequence through a particular chain of relations. Each connection needs support. Effects can weaken, divide, be redirected, or stop along the way. The size of the beginning alone establishes neither the size nor the worth of the result.

Correction is one important form of feedback. It becomes effective when it reaches the account, decision, or conduct that needs to change. Recognition left without a path into action leaves the operative arrangement largely intact.

6. Rhythm, timing, and opportunity

Rhythm is a pattern of recurrence, interval, pace, and variation. Several rhythms can occupy one situation: noticing, deciding, supplying, acting, recovering, and learning need not proceed at the same rate.

Timing concerns the relation between an act and the processes it must influence. Readiness and occasion have to meet. A capacity arriving after its support has failed may be unusable; an opening appearing before capacity is ready may remain out of reach.

Opportunity joins a purpose to recognition, access, sufficient duration, and usable means. Its worth depends upon that purpose and the life its pursuit makes possible. An apparent opening can also bring obligations that outlast the advantage first perceived.

Initiative is the capacity to help determine the next consequential movement. It may be sustained through preparation, a change of relation, or a deliberate interval. Speed alone does not confer it. A quick response can be governed entirely by another person's successive demands.

Dependable rhythms permit cooperation. Deliberate surprise can interrupt an undesirable expectation or an adversary's successful prediction. Their value depends upon the purpose, the relationship, and the effects of the variation.

7. Continuation after the act

Every intervention becomes part of the conditions that follow it. Protection can create a new dependency. A useful gain can demand maintenance. Release from one obligation can transfer its work to someone else.

The aftermath therefore belongs within the original reckoning. Necessary functions need a way to continue; exhausted capacities need renewal; new arrangements need correction as conditions change.

Beneficial continuation also requires care. An enduring peace can depend upon renewed provision, truthful communication, respected boundaries, and arrangements through which people can pursue different purposes together.

The completion of an undertaking is a change in responsibility. Some work continues, some is passed on, and some can be released.

Read what moves.
Read what renews its movement.
Care for what deserves to continue.


APPENDIX E
STRATEGIC GRAMMAR

Grammar describes how acts acquire meaning through their relations. A movement belongs to an Actor, meets an object, serves a purpose, draws upon means, and changes a field. Several movements may form one act. Several acts may form a strategy. Their order, dependence, timing, and consequences determine what the composition can accomplish.

The vocabulary remains open to new forms. Its distinctions help judgment recognize what a choice involves.

THE TERMS OF AN ACT

The Actor is the participant able to choose or contribute. An undertaking may have several Actors whose purposes, capacities, and permissions differ. Those affected also belong within the account, including people who took no part in the decision.

The purpose gives direction and a measure of completion. Its worth remains open to judgment. Success in carrying out a purpose does not settle what that purpose should have been.

The object is what the act changes or sustains. A person, a harmful undertaking, and the means carrying it are distinct objects. So are attention, a boundary, a claim, a commitment, a pattern, and a relation. The same movement has different meanings when applied to different objects.

The means are the capacities, resources, access, authority, permission, and support through which an intention can become effective. Their availability has a place, a time, and a cost. Another person's willing contribution remains theirs to give.

The mode names the manner of meeting the field. The movement names the change or continuance performed. A mode can employ several movements; a movement can enter every mode. Their fit depends upon the object, purpose, conditions, and effects.

Reach identifies how far the act extends. Duration identifies how long it operates. Both alter the obligations it creates. A momentary restraint and a continuing restriction may have similar outward forms while leaving different possibilities afterward.

Quality concerns the manner in which the act is performed and received: attentive or distracted, fitting or excessive, dependable or arbitrary, caring or possessive. Quality becomes visible through conduct and consequences as well as intention.

The principles give these terms their direction and limits. One principle may motivate an act, another enable it, another govern its timing, another limit its means, and another disclose the need for correction. Their conjunction concerns the whole act.

SEQUENCE AND DEPENDENCE

Sequence is the order in which acts occur. Dependence is the relation through which one act requires a condition supplied or preserved by another. Order becomes necessary where dependence makes it so.

Knowledge may prepare a judgment. Permission may make participation possible. Support may make a demand practicable. A later action cannot acquire its missing grounds merely by being commanded.

Some familiar sequences preserve no necessary dependence. Their stages may be changed, combined, or allowed to proceed together. Other sequences contain a dependence that remains concealed until it fails.

A change near the beginning can alter the need for everything that follows. The composition must remain able to release a step whose work has disappeared and supply a condition whose necessity has newly become visible.

ALTERNATIVES AND BRANCHES

An alternative is another available course. A branch is a course whose use depends upon a condition. A strategy can prepare several branches while leaving their selection to the circumstances in which a later choice occurs.

Each branch has requirements. A name for a possibility supplies none of the means needed to enter it. Its availability depends upon capacities, access, time, and the choices of others.

The first act changes the field of later choice. It may spend a reserve, establish an expectation, create a duty, or close a passage. Branch-chain composition attends to these changes across successive decisions.

The offered alternatives may also leave a governing assumption untouched. A change of purpose, arrangement, timing, or participation can sometimes reveal another course. Where no fitting alternative is available, judgment must reckon the actual limits and the costs that remain.

SIMULTANEITY AND MULTIPLE CONSEQUENCES

Simultaneity concerns several acts occurring together. They require compatible demands upon attention, means, and access. Their total requirements may be affordable while their requirements at one moment exceed what is available.

Polyphonic action gives distinct contributions a place within the undertaking. Their differences can provide capacities and perceptions that a single voice lacks. Their meeting requires intelligible commitments and usable paths of correction.

One act with several effects is a different relation. Its consequences may support one another, stand independently, or interfere. A benefit amplifies another only where an actual connection carries the assistance.

Harmonic effect amplification names the strengthening of compatible effects through such connections. The number of desirable consequences alone establishes no multiplication. Every effect retains its cost, its conditions, and the people who receive it.

The act can remain clear while its consequences are many.

RHYTHM AND TIMING

Rhythm concerns recurrence and pace. Timing concerns the relation between an act and the conditions under which it becomes fitting or effective.

Different processes may need different beginnings, durations, and intervals of renewal. Polyrhythmic timing coordinates their necessary meetings while preserving those differences. A common deadline does not supply common readiness.

Waiting can allow a condition to mature. Beginning can give development a direction. Interruption can break a harmful continuation. Sustaining can carry a necessary condition across an interval. Ending can release what has completed its service.

Each acquires meaning through what happens during the time it occupies.

TRANSITIONS AND CONTINUITY

A transition changes the mode, form, role, arrangement, or aim through which an undertaking continues. The passage has requirements of its own. Two workable arrangements can stand on either side of an unsupported interval.

Continuity concerns what must remain available while change occurs: care, protection, knowledge, provision, or a commitment upon which others still depend. The former form may be released while its necessary work passes into another form.

Some harmful operations must cease before a replacement is ready. Then the cost of continuing harm and the means of temporary support belong within the same judgment. An incomplete replacement does not remove the consequences of delay.

Transformation becomes consequential when altered relations make different conduct possible.

AGENCY AND SHARED AUTHORSHIP

An account of several Actors must preserve their distinct decisions. Understanding, agreement, consent, and cooperation describe different relations. A person can understand and refuse, agree and lack means, or contribute while retaining disagreement elsewhere.

Responsibility needs resources, access, discretion, time, and sufficient authority. Participation becomes authorship where a contribution can meaningfully influence the direction of the work.

Coordination joins the necessary dependencies while preserving room for initiative. Close coupling may serve one task and make another needlessly fragile. The interdependence of principles does not require every operation to depend immediately upon every other.

Leading, following, and yielding remain forms of participation whose worth appears in what they make possible for others.

CONSEQUENCE AND REVISION

Intention concerns what was sought. Consequence concerns what occurred. Reception concerns what the act was like for those who encountered it. Each can disclose something the others leave unseen.

Revision changes the account, purpose, means, sequence, reach, or arrangement in response to what has been learned. Its force depends upon reaching the decision or practice that continues the former error.

A successful outcome may rest upon favorable conditions that will not recur. An unsuccessful outcome may reveal a condition that sound judgment could not have supplied. The result must be examined together with its means and circumstances.

Strategic grammar keeps these relations intelligible while the undertaking changes. The completed sentence becomes part of the field in which the next sentence must be composed.


APPENDIX F
DOMAINS, SCALES, AND CULTIVATION

A complete situation includes the conditions through which an act becomes possible and the lives through which its consequences continue. Domains distinguish what is involved. Scales distinguish the extent and organization of participation. Cultivation concerns what must be prevented, removed, developed, or sustained within them.

THE SEVEN DOMAINS

Body carries the act. Its strength, exposure, skill, fatigue, and need for care remain part of the undertaking. An intention cannot spend bodily capacity without affecting what remains available.

Attention finds the object and receives the changing field. Its focus, breadth, continuity, and susceptibility to capture influence what can enter judgment. Attention devoted here becomes unavailable elsewhere for that interval.

Relationship joins participants through trust, dependence, obligation, communication, and differing powers. It carries both assistance and consequences. A change in the relationship can alter what the same outward act means.

Work brings intention into conduct. It includes the visible contribution and the less visible activity that prepares, coordinates, maintains, and completes it. The account of work must include those who carry its continuing burden.

Resources supply the means. Their quantity matters alongside access, location, readiness, renewability, and competing claims. A resource beyond reach has a different strategic value from one available at the needed moment.

Purpose directs the undertaking and gives it a boundary. Purposes can be shared, conflicting, mistaken, or transformed through the work. A purpose deserves examination alongside the effectiveness of its pursuit.

Renewal sustains and restores the capacities that action consumes. Rest, repair, replenishment, receiving help, and the release of completed demands belong within the conditions of continuing life.

The domains overlap. Their separation in an account allows neglected needs and displaced costs to become visible. Strength in one domain can conceal depletion in another.

THE FIVE SCALES

The personal scale concerns the individual Actor and the conditions of their choice and conduct.

The relational scale concerns the interaction of distinct people whose choices alter one another's possibilities.

The communal scale concerns shared undertakings, continuing obligations, and the arrangements through which a group participates and maintains its life.

The systemic scale concerns institutions, dependencies, rules, and distributed powers that can persist across distance and the succession of particular people.

Universal concern extends attention to the whole affected life, including those absent, distant, or yet to inherit the consequences. It enlarges the scope of care while leaving the Actor's actual knowledge and reach finite.

These scales can be present in one act. They do not form ranks of human worth. A widening view can reveal a need for more limited action, different assistance, or a change in the arrangement through which a decision takes effect.

TRANSLATION ACROSS SCALES

A recognizable pattern can recur while the means supporting it change. Personal steadiness, dependable cooperation, and institutional reliability require different provisions. Their resemblance does not carry authority, resources, communication, or correction from one scale to another.

Translation preserves the relation that matters while identifying its actual participants and supports in the new field. Responsibility must reach someone able to act. Information must reach a decision it can change. Consent must remain meaningful within the dependencies through which it is given.

The widest concern is carried through acts within reach and through relationships that extend that reach responsibly.

THE FOUR TASKS OF CULTIVATION

Prevention attends to harm not yet present and to the conditions through which it could arise. Its achievement may appear as a danger that never acquires a path.

Abandonment interrupts harm already operating and changes what restores it. Relief and the removal of a harmful source may require different acts.

Development supplies the conditions for a benefit or capacity not yet established. Formation needs means, opportunity, time, and correction appropriate to its stage.

Sustaining renews an established benefit and changes its support as circumstances change. A good can outgrow the arrangement that first made it possible.

The tasks assist one another. Removed harm can release means for development. Established benefit can reduce exposure to harm. Prevention can preserve the time that recovery would consume.

The five capacities describe powers available to the Actor; the five modes describe manners of response; the four tasks describe directions of cultivation. Each can enter every domain and scale when its actual conditions are present.

Care for the whole begins where the act receives its means and continues where it leaves its effects.


APPENDIX G
OPPOSITION, RECURRENCE, AND OBSTACLES

Difference acquires strategic meaning through its relation to a purpose, a field, and other differences. What appears opposed may obstruct, support, complete, or merely stand beside another activity. The relation can change as its objects and conditions change.

RELATIONS BETWEEN OPPOSITES

Conflict occurs where purposes, demands, or actions obstruct one another. Its scope can be limited. Disagreement over one condition does not establish hostility toward every part of an undertaking or toward the person who pursues it.

Contradiction concerns claims or requirements that cannot both hold under the same relevant conditions. Distinguishing their objects, times, or meanings may remove an apparent contradiction. A genuine incompatibility requires a choice or a change in the conditions; naming a larger whole cannot make incompatible requirements simultaneously fulfilled.

Complementation occurs where a contribution supplies something another lacks. Difference becomes a source of combined capacity through an actual relation of support.

Completion concerns contributions jointly fulfilling the requirements of a whole undertaking. It gives their relation a measure of sufficiency. The people participating retain purposes and capacities beyond the completed work.

Competition concerns rival pursuit of a limited good, position, or opportunity. Its terms determine whether rivalry remains bounded and compatible with mutual respect. A competitive relation can coexist with cooperation in preserving the conditions that make the encounter possible.

Cooperation brings contributions into shared work. Its quality depends upon the purpose pursued, the means used, and the distribution of benefits and burdens. Coordinated conduct can still injure those excluded from its account.

Coexistence allows distinct ways of living or acting to continue alongside one another. Boundaries, distance, and dependable limits can sustain it without requiring agreement on every purpose.

Coevolution occurs where participants change through their continuing responses to one another. Such development can enlarge capacities and understanding, or deepen antagonism and dependence. Its direction must be judged through what the changes sustain.

Connection is a path through which influence, information, assistance, or consequences pass. Its presence and strength depend upon the means that carry the relation. More connection can enlarge support while also allowing disturbances to spread.

Cancellation occurs where opposed effects diminish or neutralize one another within a specified respect. An effect can disappear while the effort, cost, and conditions producing the opposition remain. The cancellation of a harmful effect is distinct from the destruction of the person carrying it.

These relations can coexist within one arrangement and succeed one another over time. Their names distinguish what is happening; their value depends upon what they make possible.

THE MIDDLE PATH

The Middle Path attends to the whole relation and the purposes concealed by an apparent choice between extremes. A changed object, timing, boundary, or arrangement may permit what the former framing made incompatible.

Its measure is a fitting relation among actual needs and capacities. Equal division has no automatic claim to that measure. Nor does a declaration of harmony settle the cost borne by the person required to yield.

Where incompatibility remains, it belongs in the judgment. Protection, refusal, distance, and a clear decision can preserve the conditions through which a more peaceful relation may later become possible.

VICIOUS AND VIRTUOUS CYCLES

A recurring pattern becomes a cycle where its consequences help reproduce the conditions of its return. Repetition alone does not establish that connection.

A vicious cycle repeatedly strengthens conditions of harm, confinement, exhaustion, or renewed conflict. A virtuous cycle supports worthwhile capacity, trust, freedom, care, or shared possibility through its returns. The judgment includes those who supply the means and inherit the effects.

The direction of benefit is distinct from a geometric direction. Expansion can multiply an injury; contraction can restore room for life. A return can deepen understanding or deepen a rut. Its worth appears in the conditions it changes and preserves.

Reversal requires a change that reaches the sustaining relation. Recognition can make another choice available; the means, support, and conduct needed to realize that choice remain consequential. An interrupted pattern may resume where the conditions that renew it are left intact.

A beneficial cycle also needs renewed judgment. A form that once supported growth can become burdensome as its circumstances change. Maintenance includes the ability to learn that maintenance must take another form.

OBSTACLES AND SHADOWS

An obstacle is a condition that prevents or burdens a particular course. It may lie in bodily limits, unavailable resources, unequal power, harmful arrangements, mistaken assumptions, or the loss of fit between a response and its field. Its location determines what kind of change can address it.

A shadow is a distortion or concealed cost of a manner of responding. It becomes visible where the response undermines what it was meant to serve.

Adapting can lose direction through drift, avoid a necessary change, or turn flexibility toward manipulation.

Grounding can harden into rigidity, preserve stagnation, or attach steadiness to a form whose service has ended.

Observing can mistake its account for certainty, project an expectation onto the field, or prolong examination beyond the time in which it can assist a decision.

Spiraling can become endless adjustment, repeat a harmful orbit, or turn responsiveness into concealed control.

Emptying can become indifference, suppress what still needs attention, or abandon an obligation whose bearer has no replacement.

These distortions describe relations among conduct, circumstances, and consequences. A mode can recover its usefulness when its object, measure, or support changes. A different mode may also become necessary.

An obstacle can disclose a missing capacity, a mistaken account, or an arrangement that must change. Learning from it depends upon the opportunity and means to respond. Harm can also leave losses that require care, protection, or repair.

The Strategist receives what the difficulty reveals while remaining responsible for the life affected by the response. What cannot presently be changed still enters the reckoning. What can be changed receives the means of change.


GLOSSARY

Terms are arranged alphabetically, disregarding an initial “The.” Definitions follow their use within the two works and the appendices. Where a word carries distinct senses, those senses remain identified.

The named principles are read together and through their development across the two works. Their original wording stands at the close of the first work. The capitalized figures name functions of conduct and judgment; the same person can act in several of these roles as the occasion changes.

Cross-references connect related entries. Each relation returns to the purposes, conditions, means, and consequences of the particular act.

A

Abandonment
In cultivation, ceasing a harmful process and changing what restores it. In the account of care, leaving necessary obligations unsupported. The object and remaining dependencies distinguish release from harm from abandonment of those who still require protection or provision.
See also: Cultivation; Releasing; Obligation.

Accelerating
Increasing the pace of a specified process. Its strategic value depends upon the opportunity it reaches and the demands it creates for perception, consent, provision, coordination, and recovery. Different processes within one undertaking may require different rates.
See also: Decelerating; Delay; Initiative.

Acceptance
Acknowledging what is present or has occurred as the ground from which action must begin. Acceptance allows consequences, duties, and continuing harm to be seen clearly; it can support protection, refusal, repair, or change.
See also: Care; Judgment.

Access
The practicable ability to reach a place, resource, person, decision, or sustaining condition. Access may require permission, means, timing, or support. Knowing where a decisive relation lies does not establish the ability or authority to alter it.
See also: Means; Permission; Root access.

Acknowledgment
An outward admission or statement of what is recognized or accepted. Acknowledgment can assist responsibility and repair, but its occurrence does not by itself establish understanding, changed conduct, agreement, or restored trust. Private recognition may also remain unannounced.
See also: Recognition; Agreement; Trust.

Action
An act through which an Actor changes or sustains some part of a situation. Decisions, questions, refusals, and deliberate pauses also have effects. Action acquires meaning through its object, purpose, means, conditions, and consequences.
See also: Actor; Decision; Consequence.

Actor
The participant whose choice or contribution enters an undertaking. The term draws attention to agency, means, and responsibility. It describes a function within the account, without implying complete control of consequences or a permanent identity.
See also: Agency; Choice; Authorship.

Adaptation
The cultivated capacity to meet changing conditions through a fitting change of conduct. It can alter method, position, pace, scope, or aim while examining what should remain dependable. Water names this capacity in the elemental register.
See also: Adapting; Capacity; Alignment.

Adapting
The present mode of fitting a response to changing conditions. Adaptation supplies readiness; Adapting gives that readiness expression in a particular encounter. A fitting response can include maintaining a boundary or changing the arrangement that renews a difficulty.
See also: Adaptation; Boundary; Grounding.

Advantage
A favorable relation that makes a sought result more attainable under particular conditions. Its usability depends upon means, timing, and support; its worth requires judgment of costs, consequences, and whose possibilities it enlarges or restricts.
See also: Configuration; Cost; Dependency.

Adversary
A participant whose undertaking opposes or obstructs another's purpose in the relevant encounter. The relation identifies a present opposition without exhausting the person's character, future possibilities, or other relationships. Its scope can change as conduct and circumstances change.
See also: Conflict; Dignity; Coexistence.

Agency
The practical ability to exercise judgment, choose, refuse, and contribute to what follows. Agency depends upon information, capacity, access, support, and meaningful alternatives. Formal permission alone may leave the person unable to act upon it.
See also: Actor; Freedom; Consent.

Agreement
Assent to a proposal, claim, or shared purpose. Agreement differs from understanding, willing participation, practical capacity, and actual cooperation. Its scope must remain clear as the undertaking’s purpose, demands, and conditions change.
See also: Understanding; Consent; Cooperation.

Aim
The intended end toward which an act or undertaking is directed. An aim supplies direction and a possible measure of completion, while its worth, permissible cost, and effects remain open to judgment and correction.
See also: Alignment; Completion; Intention.

Alignment
A workable relation among purpose, conduct, capacity, commitments, and consequences. Alignment allows a continuing commitment to find new expression as conditions change. Persistent divergence can require correction of the method, supporting arrangement, or aim itself.
See also: Aim; Correction; Commitment.

Alliance
A relationship through which distinct participants combine resources, commitments, or action for purposes they can pursue together. Its durability depends upon terms, actual contributions, and changing interests. The possibility of future alliance gives present conduct consequences beyond the immediate encounter.
See also: Cooperation; Future ally; Principle of Unity.

Alternative
Another course that can actually be entered under the relevant conditions. Its availability depends upon means, time, access, and other participants' choices. Changing the governing arrangement can sometimes reveal alternatives excluded by the original framing.
See also: Branch; Agency; Freedom.

Anti-Damage Principle
Repairing damage while developing the understanding, capacity, or arrangement that can better prevent its recurrence. Learning must change what can be noticed or done. Harm does not automatically strengthen the injured, and their learning does not transfer responsibility away from whoever caused it.
See also: Repair; Cultivation Principle; Transmutation Principle.

Anti-Pattern Principle “(1, 2, 3, 5)”
Deliberately breaking an established or emerging pattern to change the expectation and continuation it supports. Its scope includes interrupting harmful habits, unsettling hostile prediction, and welcome surprise. The fitting use preserves integrity and the dependable commitments through which others can act.
See also: Pattern; Expectation; Surprise; Mystery Principle.

Attention
The selective bringing of an object or part of the field into prominence. Attention is finite and influenced by expectation, feeling, fatigue, habit, and repeated demands. Its direction affects what reaches judgment and what remains unattended.
See also: Awareness; Expectation; Judgment.

Authority
The power or recognized standing to direct, permit, withhold, establish terms, or revise a decision. Its strategic significance lies in what it can change. Its exercise remains answerable to its purpose, obligations, evidence, and those affected.
See also: Agency; Authorship; Correction.

Authorship
Meaningful participation in forming and changing the direction of an undertaking. Authorship becomes substantial through decisions that affect the work and remain answerable to their consequences. Shared authorship preserves distinct initiative and the possibility of revising a common purpose.
See also: Agency; Authority; Cooperation.

Awareness
The capacity to keep the surrounding field available while attention selects an object. Awareness includes noticing the limits and influences of the present view and remaining able to receive what could change the response. Space names this elemental capacity.
See also: Attention; Emptying; Capacity.

B

Balance
A fitting relation among contributions, needs, capacities, burdens, and continuing support. Balance may require unequal contributions or movement over time. Stability and equal division do not by themselves establish whether the resulting relation is fair or sustainable.
See also: Harmony; Cost; Golden ratio.

Belief
An account treated as sufficiently true to shape expectation, preparation, and conduct. Belief can reveal or conceal possibilities before action begins. Its influence on behavior differs from the evidence supporting it, and both belong within strategic judgment.
See also: Evidence; Expectation; Knowledge.

Belief Principle
Attention to how beliefs organize perception, preparation, and choice, including an adversary's mistaken account. Protective withholding, confirmation of an untruth, and fabrication remain different acts. A former misunderstanding must be reconsidered when it begins obstructing cooperation or peace.
See also: Belief; Knowledge Principle; Mystery Principle.

Bending
Changing a course continuously through its passage. The distinction emphasizes how successive alterations preserve or change a connection. Bending can overlap with turning, which directs attention to the heading adopted rather than the continuity of its alteration.
See also: Adapting; Continuity; Line.

Body
The embodied capacity and vulnerability through which action occurs. One of the seven domains, body includes strength, skill, perception, exposure, fatigue, and the need for care. Bodily expenditure changes what remains available for later action and continuing life.
See also: Domain; Capacity; Renewal.

Boundary
A limit governing admission, reach, participation, exposure, or permitted conduct. A boundary preserves something through means that must be maintained and judged. Its effects include those outside it and the obligations created by opening, closing, or changing it.
See also: Circle; Constraint; Freedom.

Branch
A possible course whose selection depends upon a condition. Preparing branches preserves room for later judgment, but each requires real means and access. An earlier act can strengthen, close, or create branches by changing the field of choice.
See also: Alternative; Branch-chain composition; Decision.

Branch-chain composition
The arrangement of successive choices with conditional alternatives. It attends to how each act changes the means, obligations, expectations, and passages available for later acts, allowing a strategy to retain direction while receiving new conditions.
See also: Branch; Dependency; Continuation.

C

Cancellation
A relation in which opposed effects diminish or neutralize one another in a specified respect. The effort, cost, and conditions producing the opposition can remain after its visible effect disappears. Cancellation therefore differs from removing a sustaining cause.
See also: Conflict; Generator; Consequence.

Capacity
A power that makes a response possible when its supporting conditions are available. The five cultivated capacities are Experience, Technique, Adaptation, Understanding, and Awareness. Capacity concerns what can be brought to an encounter; a mode concerns the manner of response.
See also: Mode; Experience; Technique; Understanding; Awareness.

Care
Attention and conduct directed toward sustaining life, meeting needs, protecting freedom, and responding to consequences. Care includes the person providing it and the means its continuation requires. It can be valuable in itself as well as supporting another purpose.
See also: Caretaker; Love; Dignity.

Caretaker
The person attending to the conditions through which life or an undertaking is sustained. This role includes maintenance, provision, repair, and consequences beyond the visible result. Its responsibilities require means, authority, support, and room for the Caretaker's own renewal.
See also: Care; Authority; Continuity.

Causal node
A point in a network of relations where several processes meet. Changing it can affect more than the visible difficulty. Its strategic importance includes the necessary functions and lives sustained through the same connections, together with the means to preserve them.
See also: Configuration; Root access; Dependency.

Center
A reference through which positions, purposes, or concerns are organized. Several centers may operate in one field: command, provision, vulnerability, and shared purpose need not coincide. A change of center can alter what becomes salient and what counts as disturbance.
See also: Field; Circle; Attention.

Character
The dispositions shaped and expressed through repeated choices, conduct, consequences, and surrounding conditions. Character becomes visible in what grows dependable or readily available. The capacity to admit and correct error can itself become a stable strength.
See also: Choice; Habit; Identity.

Choice
The selecting of a course or response through which will enters conduct. Choice may involve deliberation or cultivated responsiveness. Earlier choices and present conditions shape what can be perceived, considered, and carried out; others retain choices of their own.
See also: Decision; Agency; Judgment.

Circle
A figure whose points stand at a fixed distance from a center. Its strategic uses draw attention to enclosure, boundaries, continuity, and maintained recurrence. The worth of a circuit depends upon what it preserves and the work its maintenance requires.
See also: Center; Boundary; Cycle.

Coercion
Pressure that overrides or deprives another of meaningful choice or refusal through threats, imposed dependence, or control of necessary means. Restricting a harmful undertaking is judged through its purpose, scope, means, and consequences, with the person's dignity and future retained.
See also: Agency; Consent; Dignity.

Coevolution
Change arising through participants' continuing responses to one another. It can enlarge understanding and capacity or deepen antagonism and dependence. Its direction is assessed through the conditions and possibilities those reciprocal changes sustain.
See also: Feedback; Cycle; Cooperation.

Coexistence
A relation in which distinct purposes or ways of living can continue alongside one another. Dependable boundaries, distance, and limits can sustain coexistence without agreement on every matter or participation in a single shared undertaking.
See also: Boundary; Cooperation; Harmony.

Cohesion
The relation that keeps participants able to act together through pressure, changing conditions, and competing demands. Its means can include shared purpose, organization, dependable provision, and trust, with room for independent judgment and correction.
See also: Coordination; Trust; Cooperation.

Commitment
A decision, promise, or undertaking that establishes expectations and places means or future conduct at stake. Commitment can close options while making a worthwhile freedom dependable. Its scope and support require reconsideration when purposes, burdens, or conditions change.
See also: Decision; Consent; Freedom.

Companion
A person participating through company, listening, assistance, truthful regard, and dependable care. The role supports another's capacity and freedom while allowing disagreement and different paths. It gives a name to a manner of relationship, rather than a rank or fixed identity.
See also: Care; Listening; Freedom.

Competition
Rival pursuit of a limited good, position, or opportunity. Its terms determine its costs and limits. Competition can coexist with cooperation in maintaining shared conditions, and a bounded rivalry need not become hostility toward the whole person.
See also: Conflict; Cooperation; Adversary.

Complementation
A relation in which one contribution supplies something another lacks. Differences produce combined capacity through actual support. The presence of varied contributions alone does not establish complementation; their connection must allow the needed assistance to reach the work.
See also: Capacity; Connection; Completion.

Complete situation
The field of participants, conditions, purposes, means, dependencies, and consequences relevant to an act, including the Observer's place within it. An account seeks sufficient breadth for judgment while remaining open to what its present view has omitted.
See also: Field; Domain; Judgment.

Completion
In relations among contributions, their jointly fulfilling what a whole undertaking requires. In the course of an undertaking, the fulfillment that permits its work to end. Both concern sufficiency; continuing care, protection, or unresolved obligations may remain afterward.
See also: Complementation; Aim; Continuity.

Condition
A circumstance, state, permission, resource, or relation affecting what can occur. Conditions may enable, limit, or help produce an outcome. A necessary condition, a sufficient arrangement, and a favorable circumstance perform different explanatory and strategic roles.
See also: Dependency; Configuration; Generator.

Configuration
The arrangement of relations among participants, purposes, capacities, resources, boundaries, and circumstances. Configuration can change through agreement, expectation, timing, or permission without a change of physical place. Its relations determine which effects are practically possible.
See also: Field; Advantage; Dependency.

Configuration Principle
Recognizing the potential carried by an arrangement of elements and changing their relations to make advantage effective. Position, dependence, timing, and support can multiply or obstruct capacities. The same configuration must be judged for danger, cost, and what it leaves possible afterward.
See also: Configuration; Shih; Leverage.

Conflict
A relation in which purposes, demands, or actions obstruct one another. Its scope and sustaining conditions must be identified. Conflict can concern a particular undertaking without establishing hostility toward every interest, capacity, or future of the persons involved.
See also: Adversary; Contradiction; Cooperation.

Connection
A path through which influence, information, assistance, or consequences pass. Its strength depends upon the means carrying the relation. A connection can support coordination while also transmitting disturbance, so greater connection does not automatically improve an arrangement.
See also: Dependency; Feedback; Coordination.

Consent
Willing participation in a specified undertaking or relation. Its substance depends upon understanding, the conditions of refusal, and the actual burdens involved. Earlier consent does not automatically cover a changed purpose, enlarged demand, or newly imposed dependence.
See also: Agency; Coercion; Cooperation.

Consequence
What an act produces, changes, preserves, or leaves to continue. Consequences include delayed and distributed effects, displaced burdens, and altered possibilities for later action. They differ from intention and from what the Actor presently knows about the result.
See also: Intention; Feedback; Cost.

Constraint
A limit upon what can or may be done. Constraints can arise from available capacity, governing commitments, imposed arrangements, or assumptions about possibility. Their different sources determine which require respect, additional support, revision, or examination.
See also: Boundary; Capacity; Alternative.

Continuation
The passage through which further states of a process are produced. It may be sustained by habit, repeated choice, provision, commitment, expectation, or generating relations. Visible change can arise from an unchanged generator, while visible stability can require continuing work.
See also: Generator; Cycle; Continuity.

Continuity
The preservation of a necessary relation or function through change. Care, knowledge, provision, or protection may need to remain available while an earlier form ends. Continuity concerns what must keep its support during the passage.
See also: Continuation; Commitment; Completion.

Contracting
Decreasing the extent of a specified object, such as scope, reach, exposure, or attention. Contraction may concentrate capacity or reduce demands; its judgment includes obligations left outside the narrowed scope and burdens transferred to others.
See also: Expanding; Boundary; Cost.

Contradiction
An incompatibility between claims or requirements under the same relevant conditions. Distinguishing objects, times, or meanings can resolve an apparent contradiction. A genuine incompatibility requires a choice or changed conditions; shared belonging alone cannot fulfill incompatible requirements simultaneously.
See also: Conflict; Choice; Harmony.

Cooperation
The bringing of contributions into shared work. Cooperation depends upon usable means, intelligible commitments, and the relation among participants' purposes. Its quality is assessed through its ends, methods, and distribution of benefits and burdens, including effects on those excluded.
See also: Consent; Coordination; Authorship.

Coordination
The fitting together of contributions and dependencies so that they can meet where needed. Coordination can preserve distinct initiative and judgment. Its demands upon communication, timing, means, and correction vary with the undertaking and the degree of coupling required.
See also: Connection; Dependency; Cooperation.

Correction
A change in an account, decision, conduct, or arrangement in response to a disclosed error or unmet condition. Correction becomes consequential when information reaches someone with the means and authority to alter what continues the difficulty.
See also: Evidence; Authority; Feedback.

Cost
What an undertaking consumes, damages, requires, or makes unavailable. Cost includes effort, time, repair, trust, future freedom, maintenance, and burdens carried by absent people. An advantage remains incompletely assessed until its costs and bearers enter the account.
See also: Advantage; Consequence; Economy.

Credibility
The degree to which an account, commitment, capacity, or consequence is reasonably taken as dependable. Deterrence requires the prospective attacker to believe the threatened failure or cost can occur. A display, actual capacity, and another person's belief can diverge.
See also: Belief; Evidence; Deterrence Principle.

Cultivation
The sustained development and care of capacities and conditions. Its four tasks are preventing harm not yet present, abandoning harm already operating, developing benefit not yet established, and sustaining established benefit. Their means and signs of completion differ.
See also: Prevention; Abandonment; Development; Sustaining.

Cultivation Principle
Using time, consistency, and foresight to develop capacities and circumstances that may become advantageous later. Their value can remain unrealized or never arrive. Cultivation includes support, renewal, and correction so that preparation retains a fitting relation to changing purposes and conditions.
See also: Cultivation; Readiness; Renewal; Opportunity Principle.

Cycle
A recurring pattern whose consequences help reproduce the conditions of its return. Repetition alone does not establish a cycle. The sustaining connection, delays, and direction of its effects determine how it may be strengthened, interrupted, or changed.
See also: Feedback; Generator; Coevolution.

D

Decelerating
Decreasing the pace of a specified process. Deceleration can permit perception, preparation, agreement, or recovery to develop. Its value depends upon what changes during the interval and what opportunity, protection, or support may be lost through delay.
See also: Accelerating; Delay; Judgment.

Decision
An act of settling upon a course. A decision can commit means, change expectations, close branches, and prepare later choices before an outward movement occurs. Its grounds remain open to revision as consequences disclose more of the field.
See also: Choice; Commitment; Branch.

Delay
An interval separating processes or postponing their meeting. Delay can permit preparation or close an opportunity; it can also conceal effects still forming. Further action taken before earlier consequences appear may accumulate beyond what was intended.
See also: Feedback; Decelerating; Consequence.

Denial
Withholding something through which an undertaking would acquire a target, access, reaction, justification, or effect. In the peace-oriented use, denial addresses what a harmful course actually requires while attending to displaced danger, continuing duties, and the peaceful possibilities left available.
See also: Denial Principle; Dependency; Refusal.

Denial Principle
Withholding the target, access, reaction, role, or apparent justification through which an attack would operate. Its effectiveness depends upon an actual dependence. Denial remains accountable to continuing obligations, displaced danger, and the peaceful possibilities left open to the person whose attack is constrained.
See also: Denial; Strategic silence; Possibility Principle.

Dependency
A relation through which an act, capacity, or result requires something supplied or preserved elsewhere. Dependence can extend agency while making it vulnerable to withdrawal or failure. Identifying a dependency includes finding its actual means, timing, and bearers.
See also: Connection; Condition; Interdependence.

Deterrence
Prevention of an act through an expectation of unacceptable cost or likely failure. Its effectiveness depends upon the prospective actor's reckoning and the credibility of the consequence. The conditions that prevent a particular attack can leave the wish to attack unchanged.
See also: Deterrence Principle; Credibility; Will-to-conflict.

Deterrence Principle
Preventing attack by making its likely failure or unacceptable cost credible to the prospective attacker. This advanced grand strategy depends upon actual capacity, credible consequences, and a purpose of peace. Its threshold can change, and preventing an attack need not remove the will-to-conflict.
See also: Deterrence; Credibility; Will-to-conflict; Principle of Restraint.

Development
Supplying conditions in which a benefit or capacity not yet established can form. Development requires means, opportunity, time, and correction suited to its stage. It differs from sustaining an established good, although the two tasks can assist one another.
See also: Cultivation; Capacity; Sustaining.

Dignity
The standing of a person as more than an error, hostile undertaking, weakness, or defeat. Dignity remains within the judgment when harmful conduct is restrained. It excludes intentionally making public humiliation a means or end of strategic action.
See also: Grounding Principle; Mirror Principle; Golden Rule.

Disarmament
Removal or disabling of the means through which an attack can proceed. In the developed account of Redirection, the harmful undertaking loses its means while the person's body, dignity, and peaceful future remain within care. The available method depends upon actual conditions and capacity.
See also: Principle of Redirection; Protection; Object.

Domain
An aspect of the situation through which an act receives means or produces consequences. The seven domains are body, attention, relationship, work, resources, purpose, and renewal. Distinguishing them helps reveal neglected support and costs displaced from one to another.
See also: Body; Relationship; Resources; Renewal.

E

Earth
The elemental name connecting Understanding as capacity with Grounding as mode. Understanding clarifies relations, dependencies, and limits; Grounding gives a response steadiness, presence, and dependable support within those conditions.
See also: Understanding; Grounding; Capacity.

Economy
The relation between useful accomplishment and the means, effort, time, and losses it requires. Strategic economy considers preservation, maintenance, and future consequences alongside immediate expenditure. A reduced visible cost may merely transfer work or harm to someone else.
See also: Cost; Advantage; Harmonic effect amplification.

Emptying
The mode of releasing a claim, attachment, preoccupation, or completed form so that another fitting response can arise. Space names this mode in the elemental register. Release preserves attention to continuing duties, protection, and the contributions that enter the opened room.
See also: Awareness; Completion; Holding.

Equanimity
The steadiness that keeps perception, judgment, and effective conduct available amid disturbance. Equanimity can include strong feeling, correction, refusal, or urgent movement. It depends upon inward cooperation and supporting conditions, including rest, bodily capacity, provision, and dependable help.
See also: Grounding; Judgment; Capacity.

Equanimity Principle
Maintaining clarity, awareness, coherence, and steadiness amid pressure so that judgment remains available. In the developed account, feeling can enter understanding without commanding every response. Inner cooperation, bodily support, and the ability to receive correction help sustain this presence.
See also: Equanimity; Awareness; Self-Adversary Principle.

Evidence
What can support or challenge a claim about an event, condition, or relation. Evidence must be distinguished from sincerity, repetition, agreement, and expectation. Its strategic usefulness depends upon access, interpretation, and a path into decisions it can change.
See also: Belief; Inference; Correction.

Expanding
Increasing the extent of a specified object, such as reach, scope, attention, participation, or available possibility. Expansion changes demands for provision and coordination as well as opportunities. Its direction is judged through what grows and whom that growth affects.
See also: Contracting; Boundary; Capacity.

Expectation
Anticipation of what is likely to occur, often shaped by repeated conduct, memory, and belief. Expectations influence preparation and perception before a decision is made. Some support dependable cooperation; others sustain harmful patterns or make an adversary's prediction effective.
See also: Belief; Habit; Commitment.

Experience
Contact with events, conditions, and consequences from which learning can arise. Fire names this cultivated capacity. The event, its remembered account, and the lesson drawn from it remain distinct; repetition becomes useful through attention, interpretation, and correction.
See also: Fire; Evidence; Learner.

F

Feedback
A returning effect that influences the process producing it. Feedback requires an actual path through information, resources, expectation, participation, or another connection. It may reinforce, oppose, stabilize, or redirect a process; its value depends upon what that process sustains.
See also: Cycle; Connection; Correction.

Field
The arrangement of relationships within which positions, choices, and effects acquire practical meaning. The field includes participants, resources, expectations, obligations, permissions, resistance, and support. The Observer stands within it, and an act changes the conditions of subsequent observation and action.
See also: Configuration; Complete situation; Awareness.

Fire
The elemental name connecting Experience as capacity with Observing as mode. Experience supplies contact with events and consequences from which learning can arise; Observing attends to the present encounter and what it reveals.
See also: Experience; Observing; Capacity.

Forgiveness
The inward loosening of grievance and retaliation’s command over one’s life. Truthful memory, unfinished grief, and necessary protection can remain. Forgiveness requires neither another’s confession nor renewed contact, and it does not establish trust or remove the need for repair.
See also: Reconciliation; Trust; Repair.

Form
An identifiable arrangement or organized manner of action. Form can make skill teachable and conduct dependable, while its suitability depends upon purpose and conditions. A form may be preserved, varied, or released without losing the understanding that once gave it value.
See also: Configuration; Capacity; Formlessness.

Formlessness
Freedom from a fixed or readily exploitable form of response. It can involve concealment, variation, or responsiveness beyond an adversary's current account. Its strategic use is contextual; dependable commitments and the disclosure needed for cooperation retain their own value.
See also: Form; Adaptation; Mystery Principle.

Freedom
Practicable room to judge, choose, act, refuse, or depart. Freedom requires support as well as permission and may grow through dependable commitments. Its value is not measured solely by the number of options; limiting effective coercion can restore another person's agency.
See also: Agency; Consent; Coercion.

Fundamental Principle of Strategy
Making a fitting act accomplish the most with the least. In the developed account, the measure includes worthy purposes, preserved life, future freedom, and costs borne by others. Economy is judged through the whole act and the other principles.
See also: Economy; Harmonic effect amplification; Grounding Principle.

Future ally
An adversary considered in relation to the shared life and possible cooperation that may follow conflict. This perspective preserves room for mutual respect without presuming future consent or trust. A workable alliance still requires changed conditions, dependable conduct, and willing participation.
See also: Alliance; Principle of Unity; Trust.

G

Generator
A condition or relation that helps produce further states of a process. Several generators may operate together or change influence over time. Altering a present effect does not necessarily alter what renews it; one intervention can sometimes reach both.
See also: Continuation; Feedback; Intervention.

Going
Beginning, resuming, or continuing a movement. Each has different conditions: a beginning needs usable means, resumption needs renewed judgment, and continuation needs a present reason. Going commits support and helps shape the choices that can follow.
See also: Continuation; Decision; Commitment.

Golden ratio
The proportion in which a whole stands to its larger part as that larger part stands to the smaller: for positive a > b, (a + b)/a = a/b = (1 + √5)/2. This mathematical equality supplies no automatic measure of fairness or strategic worth.
See also: Balance; Judgment.

Golden Rule
A guide to conduct that brings one's own vulnerability and experience of receiving an act into concern for another. It reaches the means as well as the intended end, while attentive care must still learn the other person's actual needs and differences.
See also: Dignity; Care; Love.

Grounding
The mode of giving a response presence, steadiness, and dependable support. Earth names this mode. Grounding can include movement and correction. Its practical work is distinct from the Grounding Principle's motivating recognition of actual shared belonging.
See also: Earth; Grounding Principle; Equanimity.

Grounding Principle
Recognition that self and adversary actually belong to the shared whole affected by their conduct. It gives the principles their motivating direction: preserve the person while stopping harm and making peace possible. Shared belonging retains differences in experience, power, responsibility, and perspective.
See also: Shared whole; Grounding; Golden Rule; Principle of Unity.

Guide
A person helping another find a passage or develop the means for judgment and action. Guidance can supply knowledge, support, and coordination while leaving purposes and decisions with those concerned. Its success can include the other's growing independence.
See also: Agency; Learner; Freedom.

H

Habit
A recurring disposition through which a response becomes easier to perceive, imagine, and perform. Habit can support dependable care or continue conduct beyond its fitting occasion. Changing it may require altering the conditions that reward, protect, or renew it.
See also: Character; Expectation; Generator.

Harmonic effect amplification
The strengthening of compatible effects through actual connections by which one assists another. Several desirable consequences do not establish amplification by their number alone. Each effect retains its conditions, costs, and relation to those who receive it.
See also: Consequence; Connection; Economy.

Harmony
A fitting relation in which differences can contribute, coexist, or remain truthfully distinguished. Harmony includes room for objection and correction. Shared appearance or smooth agreement does not settle the worth of the purpose or the burdens carried by those who yield.
See also: Complementation; Coexistence; Balance.

Hidden advantage
A capacity or difference whose usefulness becomes apparent under a particular purpose or configuration, although it appeared disadvantageous elsewhere. Perspective can reveal the opening; position, means, and timing determine whether the proposed advantage can become effective.
See also: Principle of Hidden Advantage; Configuration; Perspective.

Holding
Maintaining a relation, commitment, boundary, resource, or reference through time. Holding can require movement, repair, replenishment, and correction. Its fitting duration depends upon the continuing purpose and obligations of what is held.
See also: Commitment; Continuity; Boundary.

I

Identity
An account or image of the person that can organize conduct and expectations. Its useful continuity can coexist with revision through experience. Defending an image becomes a competing undertaking when it prevents admission, correction, or release of an exhausted role.
See also: Character; Authorship; Emptying.

Imagination
The capacity to bring what is absent or not yet actual into consideration. It opens possible courses and anticipated consequences. An imagined possibility requires support to become practicable; an imagined threat remains distinguishable from an event that has occurred.
See also: Alternative; Expectation; Judgment.

Improvisation
The formation of a response as the situation develops, drawing upon cultivated capacities and available support. Shared improvisation needs dependable references and intelligible contributions so that new initiatives can enter the undertaking without losing necessary coordination.
See also: Capacity; Adapting; Authorship.

Inertia
In the extended strategic usage, a process's tendency to continue under the relations presently sustaining it. What continues may be a state, direction, rate, or rule of change. The operative relation must be discovered; no universal physical law is assumed for human processes.
See also: Continuation; Generator; Momentum.

Inference
The passage from evidence to a claim about meaning, cause, relation, or likely continuation. Inference extends beyond direct observation and remains necessary for judgment. Its grounds should be clear enough for another person to examine and correct.
See also: Evidence; Belief; Knowledge.

Information
An account or signal through which something about a situation can become known. Its usefulness depends upon credibility, independence, access, interpretation, and arrival in time to affect a decision. Several channels can repeat one source without supplying independent confirmation.
See also: Evidence; Knowledge; Correction.

Initiative
The capacity to help determine the next consequential movement. Initiative can arise through preparation, changed relations, timing, or a deliberate interval. Speed alone does not establish it when another's successive demands continue to govern the response.
See also: Agency; Decision; Accelerating.

Inner partnership
Cooperation among the concerns and capacities that participate in choice. Desire, memory, feeling, protection, and commitment gain fitting objects, duties, and times while remaining able to correct one another. Challenge can continue without requiring a part of the person to hold permanent command.
See also: Self-Adversary Principle; Equanimity; Will.

Integrity
The dependability of conduct in relation to worthy commitments, truth, and responsibility. Integrity remains recognizable through changes of method and permits admission and correction; preserving an appearance of consistency can undermine it.
See also: Character; Alignment; Trust.

Intention
What the Actor means or seeks to bring about through an act. Intention directs preparation but does not contain all consequences or establish how the act is received. Its relation to conduct and result remains part of responsible judgment.
See also: Aim; Alignment; Consequence.

Interdependence
A relation in which contributions support, limit, enable, or correct one another. Among principles, it concerns their combined interpretation in an act. In undertakings, it concerns actual dependencies; sound coordination does not require every operation to be tightly coupled to every other.
See also: Dependency; Coordination; Complementation.

Intervention
An act entering a process already under way. It may interrupt an operation, redirect a course, change a rate, delay a meeting, or alter sustaining relations. Its consequences depend upon the aspect reached and the continuation already present.
See also: Generator; Continuation; Consequence.

J

Judgment
The bringing of purpose, perception, knowledge, means, limits, and consequences into a decision about the actual situation. Judgment considers what deserves to be done as well as what can succeed, while remaining able to receive correction from action and others.
See also: Choice; Decision; Complete situation.

K

Knowledge
What is sufficiently established about self, others, conditions, and relations to inform judgment. Its reach depends upon evidence, access, timing, and acknowledged limits. Knowledge becomes strategically useful where it can reach a decision and the means for action.
See also: Evidence; Information; Judgment.

Knowledge Principle
Knowing oneself, the adversary, and the relations through which action becomes effective. Knowledge can disclose advantages, disadvantages, and ways of altering their relation. Its strategic value depends upon reaching judgment and conduct while the relevant conditions remain present.
See also: Knowledge; Perspective; Advantage.

L

Learner
The person receiving experience, instruction, and correction in ways that can change perception, judgment, capacity, or conduct. Learner names an activity open throughout life, not a fixed rank. Its development includes the ability to question and carry understanding further.
See also: Experience; Guide; Correction.

Leverage
The advantage created when a reachable change acts through a configuration to open several consequential possibilities. Leverage depends upon the connections and supports carrying its effects; an influential position alone does not guarantee a fitting or durable result.
See also: Configuration Principle; Causal node; Advantage.

Line
A figure joining positions and making direction visible. Strategically, it can express an aim, connection, or intended passage toward a destination. The actual course must include changing ground, duration, support, exposure, and the purposes of others.
See also: Aim; Connection; Bending.

Listening
Receiving another's contribution while allowing them to correct the meaning assigned to it. Listening includes attention to what dependence or power makes costly to say. It becomes consequential when what is learned can alter the response, purpose, burden, or responsibility.
See also: Companion; Correction; Consent.

Love
An orientation that gives another's good a place in attention, judgment, and conduct. Love can remain active without affection, reciprocity, or possession. Its expression includes truthful care, protection, refusal where needed, and support for the other's freedom to develop.
See also: Care; Dignity; Freedom.

M

Master Strategist
A figure of mature strategic judgment that considers purposes, means, dependencies, consequences, and continuing life together. The term emphasizes developed discernment, including sufficient action, correction, and timely release; it does not confer a fixed rank or permanent identity.
See also: Strategist; Wise; Role.

Means
The capacities, resources, access, permission, authority, and support through which an intention can become effective. Their availability has a place, time, and cost. Another person's willing contribution remains theirs to give, even when the undertaking depends upon it.
See also: Provision; Resources; Permission.

Melody
A recognizable line developing through a succession of notes. In the image of strategic composition, melody draws attention to the continuity and changing direction of a course, considered alongside its rhythm and the other contributions with which it meets.
See also: Rhythm; Polyphonic action; Trajectory.

Memory
The carrying of experience, affection, interpretation, and obligation beyond their original occasion. Memory supports continuity and learning while remaining open to correction: later understanding can change an event's meaning without changing what occurred.
See also: Reflection; Return; Self.

Middle Path
Attention to the whole relation and to possibilities concealed by an apparent choice between extremes. Changing an object, timing, boundary, or arrangement may disclose a fitting course. Genuine incompatibilities still require judgment, protection, refusal, or a definite choice.
See also: Proportion; Reconfiguration; Refusal.

Mirror
The relation through which conduct makes an encounter available to inward recognition. Another can meet their own act and the person receiving it, while the Practitioner remains exposed to correction. A truthful mirror leaves recognition free and excludes intentional public humiliation.
See also: Mirror Principle; Recognition; Dignity.

Mirror Principle
Conduct through which another may recognize their own action and the person receiving it, while the Practitioner also becomes available to correction. Recognition is inward and freely arising, even in a public encounter. Intentional public humiliation is excluded; protection does not depend upon confession or insight.
See also: Mirror; Recognition; Dignity; Grounding Principle; Principle of Restraint.

Mode
A manner of meeting the field: Observing, Spiraling, Adapting, Grounding, or Emptying. Modes can combine and change in prominence. Each can draw upon every capacity and employ every movement as its object, purpose, and conditions require.
See also: Capacity; Movement; Observing; Grounding; Emptying.

Momentum
The strength and manner of a process's continuation through time, carried by its generators and supports. In human undertakings these may include habit, repeated choice, commitments, provision, and expectations. Their continuation obeys no assumed universal physical conservation law.
See also: Trajectory; Generator; Inertia.

Movement
A way an act, relation, or course changes or continues. The twelve movements form six pairs concerning course, reference, extent, continuance, pace, and proceeding. Their meanings depend upon objects, purposes, agency, support, time, and effects.
See also: Mode; Object; Quality.

Mystery Principle
Preserving uncertainty about preparation, capacity, or response where disclosure would assist an attack. Silence, improvisation, and changing forms can protect freedom of action. Their use remains answerable to duties, consequences, and the dependable understanding that cooperation requires.
See also: Strategic silence; Formlessness; Question Principle.

N

Non-defeat
The condition sought when a conflict gives way to workable shared success. A harmful undertaking can lose its means while persons retain dignity, agency, and a future. The judgment includes real losses and remaining conditions; a favorable name cannot settle it.
See also: The Principle of Non-defeat; Shared victory; Victory.

O

Object
What an act changes, sustains, or addresses. A person, a harmful undertaking, and its means are distinct objects, as are attention, a boundary, and a claim. Identifying the object prevents a movement's name from concealing what it actually affects.
See also: Movement; Purpose; Strategic grammar.

Obligation
A continuing responsibility arising through a promise, dependence, act, or consequence. A changed form, completed role, or withdrawal may leave obligations intact. Their fulfillment or transfer requires usable means and regard for those who depend upon them.
See also: Responsibility; Withdrawal; Provision.

Observer
The participant who attends to and interprets the field while occupying a position within it. Interest, access, questions, and reactions shape both the available view and subsequent evidence. The Observer's own effects and claims remain open to examination.
See also: Observing; Perspective; Position.

Observing
The mode of attending to what occurs, distinguishing observation from inference, and noticing the Actor's contribution to the occurrence. Associated with Fire, it serves judgment through information that can alter conduct while respecting the time and opportunity available.
See also: Observer; Mode; Understanding.

Obstacle
A condition that prevents or burdens a particular course. It may concern bodily limits, resources, power, arrangements, assumptions, or the response's fit with circumstances. Its location and sustaining conditions determine which changes are possible and relevant.
See also: Shadow; Means; Reconfiguration.

Opportunity
An opening through which a purpose can be pursued when recognition, access, capacity, sufficient duration, and support meet. Its value is judged through the purpose and through the obligations and consequences that continue after the opening is taken.
See also: Readiness; Timing; Position.

Opportunity Principle
Recognizing an opening and developing the capacity to use it while its conditions hold. An apparent opportunity becomes practicable through access, means, readiness, and timing. Its value includes what entering it commits, exposes, consumes, and makes possible afterward.
See also: Opportunity; Readiness; Timing Principle; Configuration Principle.

Order
An arrangement through which distinct contributions become dependable. Strategic order joins purpose, responsibility, provision, and correction while preserving discretion where circumstances change. Its apparent stability must be examined alongside the visible and hidden work that sustains it.
See also: Square; Responsibility; Provision.

Outcome
What an act or undertaking actually produces under the conditions it encounters. An outcome differs from intention and does not alone establish the quality of judgment; favorable help, unforeseen obstruction, and continuing consequences belong in its assessment.
See also: Purpose; Revision; Victory.

P

Participation
Taking part in an undertaking through contribution, decision, support, or response. Its substance depends on actual means and influence. People affected without participating in the decision still belong within the account of the undertaking's consequences.
See also: Shared authorship; Role; Responsibility.

Pattern
A recognizable relation or recurrence among conditions, choices, acts, or effects. A pattern can guide expectation and judgment while remaining answerable to evidence, exceptions, and changed circumstances. Its repetition may disclose both useful continuity and a source of renewed harm.
See also: Recurrence; Repetition; Surprise.

Pause
A suspension of activity that preserves a possible return. Its value depends upon what the interval permits and what continues to be supported during it. The conditions for resumption may require maintenance even while the visible movement has stopped.
See also: Stopping; Timing; Readiness.

Peace
The cessation of war and the continuing condition in which life, dignity, freedom, and a shared future can be sustained. Its lasting form seeks relinquishment of the will-to-conflict and dependable means for differing people to live, work, and choose together.
See also: Grounding Principle; Will-to-conflict; Shared victory; Protection.

Peacemaker
A person acting to end attack, preserve dignity and choice, and develop conditions in which peaceful purposes can continue. The figure seeks relinquishment of the will-to-conflict while maintaining necessary protection and leaving another person's inward recognition free.
See also: Peace; Recognition; Role.

Permission
Authorization or consent that makes a particular participation, access, or action allowable. Permission differs from physical capacity and must have the scope the act requires. Its possession leaves the purpose and consequences of its use open to judgment.
See also: Means; Participation; Responsibility.

Perspective
The view formed through a position, question, scope of attention, and available knowledge. A perspective reveals some relations and conceals others. Changed scale, another account, and contrary evidence can enlarge or correct it while preserving differences of responsibility and power.
See also: Observer; Position; Understanding.

Perspective Principle
Cultivating the standpoint and understanding through which a situation becomes available to judgment. Clarity, breadth, flexibility, pattern recognition, and self-correction develop together. Another person's view can disclose what one's own position conceals, while every account remains answerable to evidence and consequence.
See also: Perspective; Observer; Knowledge Principle.

Point
A position or concentrated reference in the geometric vocabulary. A point locates; a center organizes relationships among positions. Different centers of command, provision, vulnerability, or concern can coexist and should retain their distinct functions in judgment.
See also: Space; Void; Strategic geometry.

Polyphonic action
Shared action in which distinct contributions retain a meaningful voice. Differences can supply complementary capacities and perceptions. Their meeting requires intelligible commitments, workable dependencies, and correction without requiring one participant to prescribe every contribution.
See also: Shared authorship; Simultaneity; Participation.

Polyrhythmic timing
Coordination of processes with different beginnings, rates, intervals, and durations so their necessary meetings become possible. A shared occasion requires compatible readiness, even when the processes preparing it cannot proceed at one pace.
See also: Rhythm; Timing; Readiness.

Position
The situation from which a participant can act, including reach, exposure, access, support, dependence, and practicable alternatives. Position is relational: changes in agreement, permission, timing, or resources can alter it without any physical movement.
See also: Perspective; Power; Opportunity.

Possibility
A course, response, or outcome that the relevant conditions allow. Perceived possibilities can differ from those actually within reach. Changing knowledge, capacity, access, relationships, or timing can disclose an existing opening or make a new one practicable.
See also: Possibility Principle; Position; Means.

Possibility Principle
Expanding workable possibilities of defense while restricting the routes through which attack or coercion can succeed. The object of restriction matters: protection can remove harmful options while preserving the person's independent judgment, dignity, refusal, and peaceful future.
See also: Possibility; Agency; Denial Principle.

Power
The capacity to make something happen, authorize it, withhold it, or alter its conditions. Its roots lie in actual permissions, resources, choices, and dependencies. Formal responsibility and visible prominence may differ from the places where consequential control resides.
See also: Root access; Means; Responsibility.

Practitioner
A person cultivating and exercising capacities, forms, perception, and judgment in the situations they meet. The role includes recognizing limits, receiving correction, adapting a form, and stopping when its purpose no longer warrants continuation.
See also: Technique; Understanding; Role.

Preparation
The development and arrangement of conditions that an act may require: knowledge, capacity, provision, access, support, and alternatives. Preparation changes the field before visible action begins and must remain attentive to the changing occasion it is meant to serve.
See also: Readiness; Provision; Reserve.

Prevention
The cultivation task concerned with harm not yet present and the conditions through which it might arise. Prevention can preserve freedom and resources by keeping a harmful course from acquiring the means of continuation.
See also: Protection; Sustaining; Preparation.

Principle
A motivating perspective, constraint, suggested pattern, or relation of judgment that helps direct action. Principles work together: one can ground a purpose, another disclose conditions, another limit means, and another correct the course. Their application requires attention to the particular field.
See also: Purpose; Strategic grammar; Quality.

Principle of Hidden Advantage
Discovering conditions under which an apparent disadvantage can contribute advantage. The change may concern configuration, purpose, timing, or the field in which a difference matters. A changed perspective reveals a possibility; actual circumstances and means determine whether it becomes usable.
See also: Hidden advantage; Configuration Principle; Perspective Principle.

The Principle of Non-defeat
Seeking arrangements in which worthy purposes can succeed together without requiring anyone's continuing defeat. Harmful undertakings can lose their means while persons retain dignity, agency, and a future. Shared victory requires real conditions and each participant's judgment; renaming a loss does not remove it.
See also: Non-defeat; Shared victory; Grounding Principle; Principle of Unity.

Principle of Redirection
Changing the path of an attack so that its force undoes the means of injury while preserving the person. Its advanced realization requires suitable position, timing, balance, and capacity. The intended result is disarmament, with dignity and a peaceful course remaining available.
See also: Redirection; Denial Principle; Mirror Principle; Grounding Principle.

Principle of Restraint
Allowing care to govern a strength capable of protecting, including withholding an expected retaliation. Restraint can interrupt escalation and make another response credible. It preserves adequate protection and dignity without demanding recognition, gratitude, confession, or public humiliation as its return.
See also: Restraint; Mirror Principle; Deterrence Principle.

Principle of Unity
Keeping the possibility of future shared life within present conduct, including regarding an adversary as a potential ally. Mutual respect and a workable peace guide the encounter. Future trust still requires evidence, and alliance preserves each participant's independent contribution and judgment.
See also: Unity; Future ally; Grounding Principle; The Principle of Non-defeat.

Propagation
The passage of an effect through connected conditions or participants. Its reach depends upon actual paths and sustaining means. Effects can strengthen, weaken, divide, change direction, or stop as they pass; a small beginning does not determine a large result.
See also: Momentum; Trajectory; Support.

Proportion
The relation of contributions to one another and to the undertaking they sustain, including amount, placement, sequence, duration, and rate. Fitting proportion is judged through purposes, needs, capacities, and consequences; a mathematical ratio alone establishes no just allocation.
See also: Square; Provision; Quality.

Protection
Preservation of life, dignity, agency, and necessary conditions against harm. Protection can restrict an attack's means while preserving peaceful possibilities for the person attacking. Its continuance is judged through present danger and does not require the attacker's recognition or agreement.
See also: Peace; Recognition; Refusal.

Provision
The supply and availability of means required for an act or continuing condition. Possession alone may be insufficient: resources must be reachable, usable, and sustained when needed. Provision includes maintenance, replenishment, and the people carrying those tasks.
See also: Resources; Reserve; Support.

Purpose
What an undertaking seeks to make possible or preserve. Purpose directs action and helps establish its boundary and completion. Its worth remains answerable to the means, costs, and consequences of pursuit, and can itself require revision as understanding changes.
See also: Outcome; Revision; Strategy.

Q

Quality
The manner in which an act is performed and received: its attention, care, fit, reliability, and responsiveness. Outward composure and declared intention give only part of the account. Consequences and the experience of those affected help disclose its actual quality.
See also: Movement; Receiving; Responsibility.

Question Principle
Changing action, position, capacity, or framing so that an adversary's account repeatedly loses its usefulness. Its protective force comes from real changes and the interval needed to understand them. The work remains governed by purpose, actual means, necessary disclosure, and the other principles.
See also: Mystery Principle; Anti-Pattern Principle “(1, 2, 3, 5)”; Reframing.

R

Reach
The extent over which an act, capacity, or influence can become effective. Reach depends upon actual means and relationships. Widening it changes requirements and consequences; a wider field of concern can appropriately lead to more limited action.
See also: Position; Means; Universal concern.

Readiness
The availability of sufficient capacity and support for an occasion that calls upon them. Readiness joins preparation to the current field; knowledge or strength may remain unusable when access, timing, permission, or another necessary condition is absent.
See also: Preparation; Opportunity; Means.

Receiving
Allowing help, care, correction, or another contribution to enter the relation while retaining judgment of its fit, terms, and consequences. The recipient can accept, refuse, or shape what is offered. Gratitude need not confer ownership of later choices upon the giver.
See also: Refusal; Relationship; Revision.

Recognition
An inward seeing of a fact, relationship, responsibility, or one's contribution to a situation. Recognition differs from public acknowledgment, agreement, changed conduct, and restored trust. It may remain private, partial, or delayed while protection and accountability continue.
See also: Acknowledgment; Mirror Principle; Trust.

Reconciliation
The freely sustained restoration or formation of a workable relationship after conflict or injury. It requires consent, responsibility, appropriate change of conduct, and conditions both can inhabit. Forgiveness, acknowledgment, renewed contact, and trust have their own grounds and timing.
See also: Forgiveness; Trust; Consent; Repair.

Reconfiguration
A change in the relationships through which action and effects become possible, including access, dependence, roles, boundaries, expectations, or purpose. Its significance appears in what the altered arrangement subsequently produces and what necessary functions it can continue to sustain.
See also: Shih; Root access; Transformation.

Recurrence
The return of an event, state, or pattern. Recurrence may result from continuing supports, repeated choices, or feedback that reproduces its conditions. Its explanation requires identifying what renews it, including relations left intact by an interruption.
See also: Repetition; Vicious cycle; Virtuous cycle.

Redirection
A change of course produced through an altered relation. Its broader use includes the direction of attention, effort, or a continuing process. The Principle of Redirection names the advanced response in which an attack's force undoes its means while preserving the person.
See also: Principle of Redirection; Trajectory; Disarmament.

Reflection
Examination of experience, conduct, conditions, and consequences in order to understand their relations. Reflection can correct a remembered account or reveal a neglected cost. Its contribution to learning becomes consequential when what is learned can change later judgment or action.
See also: Memory; Revision; Return.

Reframing
Changing the question, scope, or account through which a situation is understood. Reframing can reveal neglected purposes, costs, relationships, or possibilities. Its practical worth depends upon what the changed view enables people to recognize and do within the actual field.
See also: Perspective; Question Principle; Reconfiguration.

Refusal
A decision to withhold participation, access, agreement, or an offered course. Refusal can protect a person, boundary, or purpose while preserving regard. Meaningful cooperation requires that refusal remain possible without making a person surrender safety or dignity.
See also: Permission; Protection; Receiving.

Relationship
The connection through which participants influence one another's possibilities by trust, dependence, communication, obligation, or power. Relationship is also one of the seven domains. Its conditions help determine what an outwardly similar act means and makes possible.
See also: Trust; Triangle; Power.

Releasing
The movement that loosens or ends a hold upon a claim, form, restriction, attachment, or expected result. Care and responsibility can continue through release. Remaining obligations, willing transfers, and the conditions of others' freedom distinguish its fitting use.
See also: Stopping; Obligation; Withdrawal.

Renewal
Restoration and sustaining of the capacities action consumes, including body, attention, provision, and relationships. One of the seven domains, renewal requires conditions suited to each need and attention to who carries the work while another recovers.
See also: Rest; Repair; Provision.

Repair
Work addressing injury, damaged conditions, broken trust, or unfinished consequences. Repair may restore function, change an arrangement, or make future contact possible. Its performance creates no entitlement to another person's forgiveness, gratitude, or renewed vulnerability.
See also: Responsibility; Reconciliation; Trust.

Repetition
The repeated performance or occurrence of a form. Repetition can establish fluency, expectation, and dependable conduct, or reinforce an error. Learning requires attention to what repetition produces and the opportunity to revise what no longer fits.
See also: Reflection; Revision; Pattern.

Reserve
Means kept available for later response, recovery, correction, refusal, or a changed course. Reserve needs maintenance and a worthwhile purpose. Its value includes preserving possibilities after the immediate act, together with the costs imposed by keeping it available.
See also: Provision; Readiness; Resources.

Resources
Means upon which an act or arrangement can draw, including effort, attention, time, materials, knowledge, and assistance. One of the seven domains, resources are judged through quantity, access, location, readiness, renewability, and competing duties.
See also: Means; Provision; Reserve.

Responsibility
Answerability for one's participation, commitments, and consequences reasonably within sight, together with learning from those disclosed later. A duty requires usable knowledge, resources, access, discretion, time, and sufficient authority. Responsibility can remain where control of the whole outcome is absent.
See also: Obligation; Means; Power.

Rest
Release from exertion or demand, valuable both for renewal and present living. Shared arrangements must reckon whose work makes another's rest possible. In composition, a rest is a meaningful interval in a contribution, shaping rhythm and making room for another voice or the end of a phrase.
See also: Renewal; Rhythm; Silence.

Restraint
The deliberate limiting or withholding of an available response in accordance with purpose, care, and consequence. Restraint can break an expected escalation and make another course credible. Adequate protection and the burdens carried by others remain part of its judgment.
See also: Principle of Restraint; Protection; Mirror Principle.

Return
Revisiting a question, relation, place, or undertaking under conditions that may have changed. Return can support reflection, revision, repair, or completion. Its value depends on what is carried forward and what the renewed encounter discloses.
See also: Spiral; Memory; Reflection.

Revision
A change of account, purpose, means, sequence, reach, or arrangement in response to learning. Revision becomes effective when it reaches the judgment or practice that would otherwise continue the former error, while preserving obligations that still require fulfillment.
See also: Reflection; Reconfiguration; Purpose.

Rhythm
A pattern of recurrence, interval, pace, and variation. Several rhythms can coexist in one field, including preparation, response, provision, recovery, and learning. Their coordination can support cooperation; changing a rhythm can interrupt an expectation or harmful continuation.
See also: Timing; Polyrhythmic timing; Surprise.

Role
A way of participating organized around a need, with associated duties, capacities, permissions, and expectations. Roles can change or end while understanding and remaining responsibilities continue. The capitalized figures name functions of conduct, not permanent kinds or ranks of person.
See also: Participation; Responsibility; Self.

Root access
The practical ability to reach and alter a condition that helps sustain an undertaking or renew a harmful pattern. Identifying the root does not supply access. Changes there must account for necessary functions carried by the same arrangement.
See also: Power; Recurrence; Reconfiguration.

Rotating
Changing position around a center while retaining that center as a reference. Rotating can disclose relations concealed from the former position or coordinate movement around a shared purpose. The usefulness of the circuit depends on what it changes or reveals.
See also: Spinning; Point; Perspective.

S

Scale
The extent and organization of participation: personal, relational, communal, systemic, or universal in concern. Similar patterns at different scales require different means, permissions, communication, and correction. A wider concern leaves actual knowledge and practical reach finite.
See also: Reach; Universal concern; Strategic grammar.

Self
The functional continuity through which a person remembers, judges, chooses, acts, and answers for conduct. These capacities remain valuable. A defended self-image can burden them when preserving an appearance obstructs correction, responsibility, or the release of a completed role.
See also: Identity; Memory; Inner partnership; Role.

Self-adversary
A relation in which a person's own conduct repeatedly obstructs their purposes or spends the capacities needed to fulfill them. Learning what each inward response protects can permit challenge to become useful and the formerly opposed contributions to enter cooperation.
See also: Self-Adversary Principle; Inner partnership; Self-division.

Self-Adversary Principle
Learning the inward patterns through which a person frustrates their own purposes, then developing challenge into cooperation among their capacities. The movement from self-fight through self-dance toward partnership preserves the useful concerns within resistance while ending their claim to permanent command.
See also: Self-adversary; Inner partnership; Equanimity Principle; Self-Divided Principle.

Self-Divided Principle
Recognition of purposes and methods that obstruct one another within a person or undertaking. Self-deception can conceal these dependencies and intensify vulnerability. The developed account seeks to make the contradiction intelligible while preserving the person's freedom to outgrow it and form a workable arrangement.
See also: Self-division; Contradiction; Self-Adversary Principle.

Self-division
An inward arrangement in which purposes, accounts, or methods prevent one another from becoming workable. The term can also describe an undertaking whose parts undermine its aim. Recognizing the contradiction permits examination of the relations sustaining it and the conditions needed for cooperation.
See also: Self-Divided Principle; Contradiction; Alignment.

Sequence
The order in which acts occur. Necessary sequence follows actual dependence, where one act needs a condition another supplies or preserves. Inherited order can be revised when its dependence no longer holds or simultaneous work becomes possible.
See also: Simultaneity; Transition; Strategic grammar.

Shadow
A distortion or concealed cost of a manner of responding, visible when it undermines what it was meant to serve. Shadows arise through the relation of conduct to circumstances, objects, and consequences; correction may require another measure, support, or mode.
See also: Mode; Obstacle; Quality.

Shared authorship
An undertaking shaped through several people's meaningful power to initiate, alter, and influence its course. Authorship requires usable knowledge and means, intelligible commitments, and correction. A named part or nominal invitation alone does not establish that influence.
See also: Participation; Polyphonic action; Responsibility.

Shared victory
An arrangement in which worthy purposes become practicable together without requiring another person's continuing defeat. Each participant retains judgment of the offered success. Changes in terms and means must be real, and costs displaced to others remain part of the reckoning.
See also: Victory; Peace; Reconfiguration.

Shared whole
The actual belonging of self and others within the whole affected by their conduct. This recognition grounds care for an adversary as well as a companion. Shared belonging preserves distinctions of experience, responsibility, power, and perspective while giving their relation a common significance.
See also: Grounding Principle; Interdependence; Dignity.

Shih
Strategic potential arising through a configuration of participants, capacities, resources, relationships, timing, and movement already under way. Its force changes as the arrangement changes. Potential becomes effective through actual means and support available where and when they can serve.
See also: Configuration; Momentum; Reconfiguration.

Silence
An interval without speech or an expected response. Silence can make room for thought, reception, or another contribution, and can also conceal uncertainty, pressure, or an unanswered duty. Its meaning depends upon the relationship and conditions in which it occurs.
See also: Strategic silence; Receiving; Rest.

Simultaneity
Several acts occurring together. Their combined demands must be compatible at the required moment, even when their total demands appear affordable. Simultaneity differs from one act producing several consequences, though both relations can occur within one strategy.
See also: Sequence; Polyphonic action; Strategic grammar.

Space
The elemental name associated with Awareness as capacity and Emptying as mode. In geometry, space is room for difference or movement to become possible. Strategic space requires actual time, attention, access, permission, or support within the surrounding field.
See also: Void; Point; Reach.

Spinning
Turning around an axis, in the repertoire's working distinction from changing position around a center. The axis organizes the turning. Repeated revolutions can disclose change or renew the same difficulty, requiring attention to what the axis preserves.
See also: Rotating; Recurrence; Movement.

Spiral
A geometric form combining turning with changing distance from a center. Strategically it expresses recurrence with altered reach, understanding, capacity, or relation. The direction and consequences must be judged: change across a circuit can deepen benefit or harm.
See also: Spiraling; Return; Recurrence.

Spiraling
The mode joining return with change, associated with Wind. It revisits a question or undertaking after experience or circumstances have altered what is possible, and can turn existing movement through a useful connection into another course.
See also: Spiral; Mode; Return.

Square
The geometric image of an enclosed, ordered field. Its strategic use considers dependable relations among purpose, responsibility, provision, and correction. The figure does not prescribe four participants or tasks; actual requirements determine the arrangement.
See also: Order; Responsibility; Proportion.

State
The condition of a process at a specified moment. A stable state may require continuing work, while a changing state may follow an unchanged generating relation. Its appearance alone does not reveal the full history or likely continuation.
See also: Momentum; Trajectory; Recurrence.

Stopping
Ending or suspending a specified movement. Stopping can interrupt harm, preserve capacity, or acknowledge completion. A pause, an ending, and withdrawal leave different obligations and possibilities; other necessary processes may need to continue through the cessation.
See also: Pause; Withdrawal; Releasing.

Strategic geometry
A vocabulary of figures that makes position, reference, room, direction, boundary, return, threshold, relationship, order, and proportion easier to distinguish. Its strategic usefulness depends on actual correspondences and consequences; mathematical properties retain their own exact meanings.
See also: Point; Spiral; Proportion.

Strategic grammar
The relationships through which acts acquire meaning and combine into strategy: Actor, object, purpose, means, mode, movement, reach, quality, sequence, dependence, alternatives, simultaneity, transitions, and revision. These distinctions keep choices intelligible as their circumstances and effects change.
See also: Strategy; Movement; Sequence.

Strategic silence
Deliberate withholding of a response where it protects preparation, denies fuel to conflict, or preserves room for another act. Its value depends upon purpose and consequences. Duties to provide information or an owed answer remain part of the judgment.
See also: Silence; Protection; Obligation.

Strategist
The person considering choices and their connected consequences across an undertaking. The figure weighs conditions, means, timing, purposes, and what remains possible afterward. Strategic capacity can serve a peaceful life and can be released from a contest whose work is complete.
See also: Strategy; Master Strategist; Role.

Strategy
A connected course of choices and acts through which an Actor pursues a purpose within a changing field. Each choice changes later possibilities. Strategy includes preparation, adaptation, relationships, and completion, while the worth of its purpose remains open to judgment.
See also: Choice; Purpose; Strategic grammar.

Support
A condition or contribution through which an act, capacity, or arrangement becomes practicable and can continue. Support includes people and dependencies beyond the visible act. Its availability, renewal, costs, and freedom to change belong within the undertaking's reckoning.
See also: Provision; Means; Responsibility.

Surprise
A departure from an established or anticipated pattern. Surprise can interrupt harmful repetition, defeat an adversary's successful prediction, or renew attention and enjoyment. Its value is contextual; dependable promises and the references needed for shared action remain important.
See also: Anti-Pattern Principle “(1, 2, 3, 5)”; Expectation; Trust.

Sustaining
The cultivation task of maintaining an established benefit through renewal and suitable support. Sustaining includes correction when changed circumstances make the original arrangement burdensome or inadequate. Preserving the good may require changing the form that first carried it.
See also: Renewal; Support; Revision.

T

Technique
A cultivated means of performing an act, associated with Wind in the capacity register. Technique becomes dependable through coordination and support, and useful through judgment of its occasion. It includes the ability to alter or stop the practiced form.
See also: Practitioner; Wind; Readiness.

Threshold
A condition or passage at which the terms of response change. Thresholds can gather small changes into a markedly different result. Crossing requires attention to the means and obligations beyond the passage, including what cannot afterward be readily reversed.
See also: Vertex; Transition; Timing.

Time
The dimension through which conditions develop, acts meet, consequences emerge, and capacities recover. Time is also a finite means upon which undertakings place claims. Duration, delay, sequence, and recurrence affect what a seemingly identical act can accomplish or cost.
See also: Timing; Rhythm; Trajectory.

Timing
The relation between an act and the conditions or processes it must meet or influence. Fitting timing joins readiness, opportunity, duration, and the responses of others. Waiting, beginning, changing pace, and ending each gain meaning through the interval they occupy.
See also: Opportunity; Readiness; Polyrhythmic timing.

Timing Principle
Judging an act through the moment and rhythms in which it occurs. The same action can acquire different consequences as readiness, expectation, or support changes. Several processes may require distinct paces whose necessary meetings are coordinated without erasing their differences.
See also: Timing; Rhythm; Polyrhythmic timing.

Trajectory
The course a process takes through successive states over time. Its direction can change while momentum grows or weakens. A projected trajectory remains conditional upon the generators, supports, information, and choices through which the process continues.
See also: Momentum; State; Continuation.

Transformation
A consequential change in the relations, capacities, purposes, or arrangements through which conduct becomes possible. Transformation is established by what can actually occur and continue afterward; its extent must be judged alongside remaining dependencies, obligations, and limits.
See also: Reconfiguration; Transition; Outcome.

Transition
A passage between modes, forms, roles, arrangements, or aims. The interval has requirements of its own: two workable conditions may be separated by an unsupported passage. Transition therefore includes the continuity needed while the former form changes or ends.
See also: Threshold; Support; Releasing.

Transmission
The passage of understanding in a form another can examine, use, question, and adapt. Transmission becomes durable through independent judgment and correction beyond the original practitioner's presence. The recipient retains responsibility for the different ground upon which the understanding is carried.
See also: Understanding; Technique; Responsibility.

Transmutation
Conversion of incoming attacking effort into a means of development while that effort operates. The conversion requires capacity and a configuration able to perform it. Its value is shown by actual development, distinct from the work of repairing injury or learning afterward.
See also: Transmutation Principle; Anti-Damage Principle; Redirection.

Transmutation Principle
Converting incoming attacking effort into a means of development while that effort operates. This advanced response requires capacities and relations able to perform the conversion. It differs from repairing damage or learning afterward, and its gain should support development that can continue when opposition ends.
See also: Transmutation; Principle of Redirection; Anti-Damage Principle.

Triangle
The geometric image of three positions and their connections. A third participant, need, purpose, supporting condition, or audience can change an apparently two-sided relation. Its significance depends upon the actual work, influence, and consequences carried through those connections.
See also: Relationship; Strategic geometry; Shared victory.

Trust
Entrusting a particular vulnerability, responsibility, promise, or part of a shared future to another. Its reach should answer to dependable conduct and actual means. Recognition, forgiveness, and agreement can assist restoration while remaining distinct from evidence that trust can safely grow.
See also: Integrity; Forgiveness; Reconciliation; Vulnerability.

Turning
Changing a heading so that another direction guides the next course. A turn may be gradual or swift and can overlap with bending. Its consequences include altered dependencies, commitments, support, and the means by which others can understand and meet it.
See also: Movement; Trajectory; Transition.

U

Understanding
The capacity to join particulars through causes, conditions, dependencies, and limits, associated with Earth. Understanding includes its grounds, uncertainties, and the Actor's participation in the field. It becomes practically useful when the relations discovered can inform judgment and conduct.
See also: Perspective; Observing; Transmission.

Unity
A relation through which distinct participants can sustain a common undertaking or future. The peace-oriented use preserves their independent judgment and possible differences. Grounding supplies shared belonging as its motivation; willing cooperation and workable conditions give unity a continuing practical form.
See also: Principle of Unity; Grounding Principle; Cooperation.

Universal concern
The widest scale of care, including affected life beyond immediate membership, visibility, distance, or generation. It keeps absent people and inherited consequences within the judgment while leaving knowledge, authority, and practical reach finite and requiring actual means of assistance.
See also: Scale; Reach; Responsibility.

V

Vertex
A meeting of directions in the geometric vocabulary. Strategically, several histories, purposes, commitments, or possible courses can converge upon one decision. A vertex can coincide with a threshold while emphasizing convergence rather than the change of conditions beyond it.
See also: Threshold; Turning; Strategic geometry.

Vicious cycle
A recurring pattern whose consequences help reproduce conditions of harm, confinement, exhaustion, or renewed conflict. Interrupting one occurrence can leave those conditions available. Reversal requires a change that reaches the relation sustaining the return.
See also: Recurrence; Root access; Virtuous cycle.

Victory
Prevailing in a contest or attaining the sought result of an undertaking. Its worth is judged through preserved life, dignity, agency, costs, and the future left possible. Another person's victory remains theirs to assess.
See also: Shared victory; Outcome; Peace.

Virtuous cycle
A recurring pattern whose consequences help sustain worthwhile capacity, trust, care, freedom, or shared possibility. Its benefit includes those supplying the means and receiving the effects. Changed circumstances may require revision of the form that keeps it going.
See also: Recurrence; Sustaining; Vicious cycle.

Void
Openness associated with Emptying in the elemental language. In the geometric vocabulary it holds point and space together: concentrated reference and room for another configuration to emerge. Release can make this openness available while care and responsibility continue.
See also: Space; Point; Releasing.

Vulnerability
Exposure to harm, loss, dependence, or interruption through a particular condition. Vulnerability must be understood with its object and supports. Another person's exposure enters the duty of care; freely offered vulnerability differs from exposure demanded as proof of trust or forgiveness.
See also: Protection; Trust; Position.

W

Water
The elemental name connecting Adaptation as capacity with Adapting as mode. It directs attention to fitting a response to changing conditions while preserving what deserves dependability. Its use depends upon the actual relations and purposes within the situation.
See also: Mode; Readiness; Reconfiguration.

The Water Principle
Continuous adaptation to the changing circumstances of the complete situation. The response takes form through what is present while retaining the purposes and limits that make it fitting. Adaptation can change a method, pace, relation, or aim when its continuation renews harm.
See also: Adaptation; Complete situation; Grounding Principle.

Way
A manner or path of acting and living, expressed through purposes, choices, conduct, and their consequences. The way can continue through changing forms and completed undertakings while remaining answerable to the life it sustains.
See also: Purpose; Strategy; Peace.

Will
The faculty of choice, working through attention, feeling, memory, imagination, understanding, and judgment. These influences can cooperate or conflict. Will remains answerable for its participation while recognizing that other people's decisions also shape what an intention can accomplish.
See also: Choice; Self; Inner partnership; Will-to-conflict.

Will-to-conflict
The wish to initiate or renew injury, coercion, or domination. Its relinquishment releases will toward other purposes while preserving independent judgment, disagreement, refusal, and protection. Enduring change concerns both inward willingness and the conditions that repeatedly cultivate hostility.
See also: Will; Peace; Peacemaker.

Wind
The elemental name connecting Technique as capacity with Spiraling as mode. Acquired forms become available for return, variation, and changes of course. Their fit remains dependent on judgment, supporting conditions, and the consequences of their use.
See also: Technique; Spiraling; Movement.

Wise
The figure of judgment concerned with what deserves to be done and the life that conduct leaves possible. Wisdom weighs destination, means, proportion, timing, and consequence, remains receptive to correction, and makes room for care, beauty, rest, and another's freedom.
See also: Master Strategist; Purpose; Role.

Withdrawal
The ending of a particular manner of participation. Obligations can remain after withdrawal and may require completion, repair, or transfer with usable means. Its consequences include what others must continue carrying and which conditions of return remain available.
See also: Stopping; Obligation; Releasing.

Work
The activity through which intention enters conduct and an undertaking is prepared, maintained, corrected, and completed. One of the seven domains, work includes less visible care and coordination, together with the people who bear its continuing burden.
See also: Purpose; Responsibility; Support.

Y

Yielding
Allowing position, prominence, initiative, or guidance to pass where another contribution can serve the undertaking more fittingly. Yielding can preserve active perception and correction. A consequential transfer also passes the knowledge, means, and responsibility required to carry the work.
See also: Role; Shared authorship; Responsibility.
