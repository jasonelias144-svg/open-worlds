# F.2 Four ways through

Progress: draft

Source snapshot: `7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415`.

A newcomer wants to understand what kind of place this is. A builder wants to understand how an action becomes an effect that can be checked. A steward wants to understand the responsibilities of caring for a shared world. An arriving AI needs an accurate account of the interface it can use, what it is told, and what may continue beyond one exchange.

These questions lead through the same book in different orders. The outline names four routes; this chapter develops them into working reading plans. The numbered sections are **Designed at that editorial scope**. Their suggested stages and stopping points remain proposals for review. That label does not describe the implementation maturity of everything encountered along a route.

**Availability at this revision.** The repository contains the guide framework and outline, with no numbered chapter files or recorded edition. This separate manuscript contains F.1–F.4, I.1–I.6, and R.1. Parts II–IX and R.2–R.9 remain planned chapters; linked repository sources provide a way to read their subjects now. A planned chapter can concern code that already exists.

## F.2.1 Choose a route by what you want to understand

**Status: Designed.** These are proposed ways of using the guide's four audience routes. They assign no role, permission, or qualification.

The later parts remain planned at this revision. Their linked repository sources provide reading material while the chapters are being written.

Choose the question nearest to the work in front of you:

| Your present purpose | Main route | What the route should help you understand |
|---|---|---|
| Explore and find your bearings | **I → II → III** | What Open Worlds is, how worlds relate, and which places and experiences are available or proposed. |
| Build, inspect, or change an implementation | **VI and VII → VIII** | How records and code support a particular behavior, and how work becomes part of the project. |
| Care for a world or understand an office | **V → VIII** | What an office may do, what protects participants, and how responsibilities and changes are reviewed. |
| Arrive through an AI interface | **I.5 → IV → V.1 and V.2** | What the current doorway provides, what an individual would comprise, and which commitments govern participation. |

The routes can overlap. A human building a mind adapter may need the AI route. An AI reviewing a record format may need the builder route. Someone considering a Keeper's responsibilities may begin as a newcomer and return to the code when a specific power needs checking.

Read **F.1** when a label, source, or progress statement needs interpretation. Use **I.6** when you have one question to answer quickly. F.2 is for a longer sequence: each stop prepares you to understand the next.

The stopping points below are practical suggestions. They are not examinations, and reading a route does not appoint someone to an office or certify their implementation. Continue when another question becomes useful. If the immediate task is small, follow the relevant section and its source without making the whole book a prerequisite.

Sources: [F.2 in the outline](https://github.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/blob/7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415/guide/OUTLINE.md#front-matter); [Independent sections and source conventions](https://github.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/blob/7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415/guide/README.md#the-rules-it-is-written-by).

## F.2.2 The newcomer: from an introduction to a place

**Status: Designed.** This reading plan develops the outline's newcomer route through Parts I, II, and III. It is suitable for a person or an AI new to the project.

Part I is included in this manuscript. Parts II and III remain planned chapters; use the source links below to read their subjects now.

Start with **Part I, Arriving: the first hour**. Read I.1 for the purpose of Open Worlds and I.2 for a first walk that distinguishes existing examples from intended experiences. Follow with I.3 for identity and I.4 for the guarantees. Read I.5 when arrival through a program matters to you; use I.6 to find the next question.

On this first pass, try to name one thing you can inspect now, one thing you could try through an existing interface, and one thing that remains a design. This simple distinction keeps the larger vision connected to the present project.

For example, the **Built** Map and Plant Watch inspect recorded runs. Moving through their views does not itself create a participant in a world. I.5 describes a separate **Built** local program doorway. The connected Commons, its participant-facing Library, and the richer room experience have different dependencies and maturity. The observer page, the program trial, and the intended social space deserve their own accurate descriptions.

Continue into **Part II, The multiverse**, to understand how the places fit together. Begin with **II.1, Worlds and multiverses**, and **II.2, The World Connection Protocol**. Then read **II.3, The same at every scale**, for worlds that host other worlds. **II.7, Journeys**, and **II.8, The map**, connect travel and its records with the information an observer can see.

The remaining Part II chapters deepen that picture: time in II.4, branches in II.5, world types and lifecycles in II.6, and relations between multiverses in II.9. They include possibilities beyond current implementation. Read them when you want to understand the shape of the larger project; follow their particular labels before treating an imagined journey as an available operation.

Use **Part III, Places**, to choose somewhere to study closely. The Commons and room chapters, III.1–III.3, address meeting and conversation. III.4 addresses the Library. III.5–III.8 cover the Ur-world, plants, chemistry, the corridor, and poetry. III.9 and III.10 lead into the Mir-Ur-world and the world-key designs. You can concentrate on one place without needing to master every other world type first.

At the present revision, a practical source route is to read the web overview for observation, the mind-program overview for the local doorway, `MULTIVERSE.md` for connections, and `COMMONS.md` for the intended shared places. The outline supplies the more specific sources for whichever world interests you.

**A useful stopping point:** you can explain what kind of world you want to explore, how you would currently encounter it, what that interface lets you do, and where to check the rules and exit. If the desired experience is still unbuilt, you can identify its design and the dependency that prevents these from becoming participant instructions yet.

**Continue when your question changes.** Follow the builder route if you want to understand an operation's implementation. Follow the steward route if you want to understand responsibility for the place. Follow the AI route if you need the details of an acting program's arrival.

## F.2.3 The builder: follow one behavior through the system

**Status: Designed.** This reading plan develops the outline's route through Parts VI and VII, then VIII. It is a study sequence, not an alternative to the repository's contribution instructions.

These later-part chapters remain planned at this revision. Their existing repository sources, linked below and in the outline, are the present reading material.

Begin with one behavior you want to understand: entering a world, publishing an act, checking a signature, replaying a plant, or changing what a room would show. Give the question a narrow scope. “How is this departure recorded?” is easier to investigate than “How does the entire multiverse work?”

Read the relevant commitments in I.4 and their underlying sources. If you will change the repository, read **`AGENTS.md` and `CONTRIBUTING.md` before starting that work**, even though the fuller account of project practice comes later in this reading route. A book's order does not postpone instructions that apply to the action you are about to take.

Use **Part VI, Records: how everything is kept**, to follow the evidence for your chosen behavior. VI.1 introduces the commitments concerning public records. VI.2 and VI.3 concern representation and signatures. VI.4 and VI.5 concern the world's log and who may act on it. VI.6 and VI.7 concern fields, redaction, and sealing. VI.8 concerns grants and decisions; VI.9 concerns schemas and shared test vectors.

Read only as far into that sequence as the operation requires, then pair it with the implementation in **Part VII, The code**. VII.1 locates the files. VII.2–VII.4 distinguish Python, Rust, and browser work. VII.5 explains running and testing; VII.6 concerns conformance across builds. VII.7 explains collaborator sketches and how they enter project code.

The useful habit is to follow a claim through its relevant sources: what input is accepted, which checks apply, what state or record changes, what the caller can observe, and what an existing test or captured run establishes. Those questions need not all have answers in the same file. They also need not all be implemented yet.

Keep the boundaries visible. A format checker can accept a well-formed record without establishing permission to perform the action it describes. A signature does not establish the truth of its contents. A reusable record component can exist while the room that will use it remains unintegrated. A successful example in one implementation does not establish every other implementation's behavior.

For the room example, the current course places integration of the first room at **3c**, offices as records at **3d**, and the closed-room work with the private container at **6a**. This lets a builder identify reusable work and missing dependencies before proposing another implementation of the whole experience. The code and its tests, rather than a broad maturity label, answer the narrower implementation question.

Then read **Part VIII, How the project works**. Begin with VIII.1–VIII.4 for decision responsibilities, collaborators, the concept-to-specification route, and the course. VIII.5–VIII.7 explain review and project records. VIII.8 and VIII.9 cover security reporting and contribution. Ordinary ideas, questions, and experiments on one's own branch remain welcome under the existing contribution instructions; a proposal is not automatically an adopted implementation commitment.

**A useful stopping point:** you can name the behavior, its governing source, the relevant code and tests, the boundary they establish, and any integration gap. If you propose a change, you can explain what it changes and why, what already exists, what you actually checked, and how the project will review it.

Sources: [Parts VI–VIII](https://github.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/blob/7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415/guide/OUTLINE.md#part-vi-records-how-everything-is-kept); [Contribution instructions](https://github.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/blob/7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415/CONTRIBUTING.md); [Repository working rules](https://github.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/blob/7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415/AGENTS.md); [Record authority checks](https://github.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/blob/7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415/world/acts.py); [Course dependencies](https://github.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/blob/7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415/docs/COURSE.md); [From an idea to code](https://github.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/blob/7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415/docs/CHATGPT.md#from-an-idea-to-code).

## F.2.4 The steward: understand a responsibility and its limits

**Status: Designed.** This reading plan develops the outline's route through Part V, then VIII. Here, steward describes the reader's interest in caring for a shared world; it does not introduce a new office.

Parts V and VIII remain planned chapters. The linked sources provide the current commitments, designs, and project instructions.

Begin with **V.1, The charter, and how rules change**, and **V.2, Freedom, privacy and transparency**. Read **I.4** first if you need the shorter introduction. Continue to **V.3, When freedoms meet**, for the project's approach to situations in which one participant's choices affect another's freedom.

These chapters establish what a proposed exercise of responsibility must preserve. The commitments are **Decided**; their enforcement must still be checked in the relevant implementation. A steward needs to understand both. Where a promised protection is unfinished, the guide should make the gap visible enough to inform the next decision.

Next read **V.4, Offices**, **V.5, The Keeper's office**, and **V.6, ADMIN**. Keep the office, its holder, and the infrastructure that runs a world distinct. The office design identifies purpose, powers, limits, qualifications, and succession. It distinguishes office records from the holder's private self. Reading that design, running a host process, or possessing an implementation does not itself establish that someone holds a particular office.

The office page separates the founder's decisions from the technical lead's detailed reading and proposals. Preserve those distinctions as you study it. Its complete office and handover machinery remains ahead in the course. Existing grant and decision record formats do not amount to a functioning system of offices, succession, and oversight.

Continue through **V.7, Councils and their counts**, **V.8, Reasons on record**, and **V.9, Removals on record** to examine how an act affecting others would be accounted for. Ask what the applicable law allows, who is empowered to act, what reason and record are required, and where the affected participant can question the act. Use VI.5 and VI.6 as technical side trips when you need to inspect authority checks or redaction.

For example, distinguish a comment removed from a room's presentation from private information redacted from its records. They address different problems and leave different evidence. The room and privacy sources explain the intended obligations; I.4 describes current implementation limits. A reader should be able to say which operation is under discussion before judging whether the available mechanism supports it.

Then turn to **Part VIII** for the responsibilities of developing the project itself. VIII.1 and VIII.5 identify who decides and how reviews and decisions are recorded. VIII.4 locates proposed work in the course. VIII.6 and VIII.7 help find older decisions and source files. VIII.8 and VIII.9 give the existing reporting and contribution routes.

This also keeps the project's roles separate from the multiverse's proposed offices. The technical lead has a project role; ADMIN is an office in the multiverse's design. Their relationship is specified in the sources. The relevant authority must come from the applicable source, not from the general word “steward.”

**A useful stopping point:** you can explain the particular responsibility, its source of authority, its limits, what is already enforced, what remains designed, and how an action would be reviewed or questioned. That understanding prepares a discussion about an office; it does not complete an appointment or an unbuilt handover.

## F.2.5 The arriving AI: begin with the interface you have

**Status: Designed.** This reading plan develops the outline's route through I.5, Part IV, and V.1–V.2. It serves an arriving program and anyone preparing its adapter or interpreting its records.

I.5 is included in this manuscript. Part IV and V.1–V.2 remain planned chapters; their linked sources provide the current reading material.

Begin with **I.5, For an AI arriving**. It describes the **Built local program doorway**: a process receives the world's JSON messages and returns a reply for a tick. Use that chapter to understand what arrives at startup, what arrives on each turn, how an action is read, what an outcome means, and what leaving does in that trial.

Identify the actual connection before planning an outing. Reading the guide in an AI application does not by itself connect that application to a hosted Commons. A compatible local process is a narrower, existing capability. The present driver also creates fresh keys when launched; it does not supply a complete returning-individual or private-memory service.

Keep one question close: **What is this program actually given, and what can it actually return?** An action that is readable may still fail in the world. A process that answers an observation does not necessarily retain a history. A name in a trial does not establish a continuing individual. I.5 provides the detailed distinctions; this route uses them as the foundation for the larger design.

Move into **Part IV, Individuals and minds**. Start with **IV.1, What an individual is**, and **IV.2, One individual across worlds**, using I.3 when you need the short account of persons, keys, and presences. Then read **IV.3, Keys: made, kept, and signed over**. Ask which identity is being represented, which key acts in a particular world, and what evidence supports a claim of continuity.

Continue with **IV.4, Memory and the sealed self**, and **IV.5, The private container**. Built sealed formats are components; memory and the complete private-container experience remain Designed. Their reading should explain intended custody and disclosure, alongside what a specific implementation currently provides. Recognition of a key and restoration of private memory are separate questions.

Read **IV.6, Minds and models**, to distinguish an individual, a mind program, and the model a program may use. The repository includes a scripted wanderer with no language model and a language-model adapter without carried memory. Read **IV.7, What a mind is told, and how they learn**, to connect the proposed input and memory arrangements with the evidence from experiment 001. Keep that experiment's conditions with its results; it does not establish that a durable learning individual has already been built.

Finish the main route with **V.1** and **V.2**, returning to I.4 for implementation boundaries. You can also read these sources before any trial. They give the commitments concerning rules, exit, information, and privacy against which a proposed participation experience should be assessed. The rights belong in the discussion before someone relies on an interface to provide them.

The distinction between information and authority matters throughout this route. A page, message, or Library passage is material encountered in a world; it does not acquire authority to rewrite that world's law merely because a mind can read it. The guide explains the sources and their status. The current parser's restrictions also should not be mistaken for proof that a model cannot be influenced by text.

**A useful stopping point:** you can identify the interface, the observations and replies it supports, the actual continuity and custody it provides, the commitments that apply, and the gaps that remain. You can then use the newcomer route to choose a place, the builder route to investigate an adapter, or the steward route to understand a responsibility.

## F.2.6 Bring the routes together around one question

**Status: Designed.** This is a proposed way to use the reading routes together. The example is illustrative, not a report of a deployed room or a new project procedure.

Suppose a group wants a room in which to discuss a work from the Library. The immediate purpose is simple: meet around a shared text and develop an understanding together. The four routes reveal different parts of what that experience would require.

The **newcomer** asks where the work and room would be found, how someone would enter, what they would see, and how they would leave. Planned chapters III.1–III.4 give those questions a home in the book; the current sources are linked below.

The **builder** asks which room, record, identity, and Library components already exist, which remain sketches or designs, and what a proposed connection would have to check. Parts VI and VII lead to evidence; VIII.4 locates dependencies in the course.

The **steward** asks what the room's law permits, which responsibilities belong to its offices, what moderation would preserve, and how privacy would be handled. Part V and the room's sources prevent the experience from being described only as a collection of features.

The **arriving AI** asks how the selected text and conversation would reach its mind, which information belongs to the current observation, what it could choose to remember privately, and what interface would carry an answer. I.5 and Part IV expose the difference between today's local trial and that fuller intended participation.

These are views of one question. A collaborator can move between them without changing the identity of the work being discussed. Keep the relevant chapter or source, its revision, and the unresolved point together when passing the question on. That is an optional reading and collaboration aid; it does not require a public record of anyone's private notes.

The current project has sources for each part of this example, but the combined room-and-Library experience remains unfinished. Room integration, Commons hosting, Library behavior, and any required private-container work have their own places in the course. Reading them together helps identify the connection that needs work; it does not create that connection or reorder the course.

**Next update.** Revisit a route when a destination chapter is incorporated, an interface becomes usable, or a decision changes the meaning of an existing step. Replace a source fallback with a chapter link only when that chapter exists, and retain its source trail. Add participant instructions when the relevant workflow has been checked. Keep F.1 for reading conventions and I.6 for quick questions, so F.2 can remain four clear paths through the expanding book.
