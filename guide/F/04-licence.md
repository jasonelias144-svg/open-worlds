# F.4 Licence

Progress: draft

Source snapshot: `7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415`.

This chapter explains how the Open Worlds dedication applies when someone reads, copies, shares, adapts, translates, or prints the project's work. It also explains how to keep the terms of separately sourced material visible. The legal instrument is the repository's `LICENSE`; this chapter explains its use within the guide.

## F.4.1 The project's dedication

**Status: Decided.** The repository has chosen CC0 1.0 Universal, and the guide's framework applies that dedication to the guide while preserving quoted works' own terms.

Open Worlds makes its own material available for others to use and build upon. The root README permits use and change for any purpose without asking. The guide follows that choice. Its wording also makes the scope clear: a work quoted in the guide keeps its own licence.

CC0 dedicates the rights held by the person applying it to the public domain to the extent the law permits. If the waiver is ineffective, the instrument provides a public-licence fallback. This is why the precise statement includes the rights the project can dedicate and the limits allowed by law. It does not depend on a claim that every possible right disappears in every jurisdiction.

For a reader, begin with the material being used. The project's original explanation, an externally sourced quotation inside it, and a linked book can have different rights records. A single page can contain all three. The surrounding page's CC0 declaration does not make those distinctions disappear.

The dedication is already recorded. This chapter gives readers a way to understand it; it does not create a new licence or change an outside author's terms.

## F.4.2 Copy, share, adapt, translate, and print

**Status: Decided.** These uses follow from the existing CC0 terms for material covered by the dedication. They do not await a new publishing feature.

For the project's CC0 material, readers can make their own copies, distribute them, change the work, translate it, and incorporate it into something else. This includes commercial uses. The following examples describe uses of that covered material; separately sourced items retain their own terms under F.4.3.

| What a reader wants to do | Application to the project's CC0 material |
|---|---|
| Keep a copy to read offline | Save a copy and make a convenient reading version. |
| Share it with a friend or reading group | Distribute the material in full or as selected passages. |
| Build a lesson, introduction, or field guide | Select, rearrange, explain, and adapt the material. |
| Translate it | Make and share a translation. |
| Print a book | Produce copies for personal use, free distribution, or sale. |
| Build on the software | Copy and adapt covered project code, while considering the terms of any other components included. |

CC0 does not impose an attribution condition, require an adaptation to be sent back to the project, or require new contributions to use the same terms. An adapter's new material does not automatically acquire the project's dedication. Clear source and change notes remain useful; F.4.5 proposes a way to provide them voluntarily.

The project's own commitments about its purpose remain important. Open Worlds is intended as a freely usable public space, with explicit limits on its own commercial direction and the sale of participant data. Those governance commitments and the copyright permission serve different purposes. They do not add a noncommercial restriction to CC0. A paid printed copy of covered material can therefore be permitted without becoming an adopted project edition or changing how Open Worlds itself is governed.

## F.4.3 Keep separately sourced material identifiable

**Status: Decided.** The guide preserves quoted works' own terms, and the repository already identifies material outside its CC0 dedication. The examples here illustrate that boundary; they are not a complete inventory of outside rights.

A quotation, test example, image, dataset, or software component can have its own source and terms. Identify the particular material and the permission or other basis that applies to the intended use. A citation helps someone find a source; by itself, it does not grant rights in that source. Depending on the work and use, an applicable licence, permission, public-domain status, or legal exception may supply the basis. The guide does not determine that basis for every work it mentions.

There are concrete examples in the present repository:

| Material | What the repository records | Meaning for reuse |
|---|---|---|
| W3C example in `vectors/formats/eddsa-jcs-2022.json` | The `w3c` object identifies copied specification material and its W3C notice. | That material retains the cited W3C terms. A CC0 label for surrounding project work does not replace them. |
| Poetry quotations identified in `sketches/README.md` | The rights section expressly excludes those quotations from what the project can dedicate. | Their inclusion in a conversation or demonstration does not make them CC0. |
| *Art of Peace and the Strategist* in `library/` | The library page records the founder's CC0 dedication of that identified book. | That specific record can be followed without extending it to every historical record or future library item. |

The W3C example also shows why a source link and a licence obligation should be kept distinct. The linked W3C Software and Document License has requirements for notices and changes. Anyone redistributing that material should follow the full applicable terms, including the required notice, retained terms, and modification notice; merely labelling it CC0 would misdescribe the permission. This chapter does not certify the repository's implementation of every such requirement.

Dependencies need the same attention to scope. Open Worlds' declaration is not a substitute for the licence of an independently supplied package. An export that bundles other software, images, fonts, or source texts should identify the material it actually includes and follow the applicable terms. This is a principle for making a particular copy, not a claim that all those materials are present in the current guide.

For quotations in future chapters, the existing guide convention already calls for the quoted work's terms to be stated. A clearly associated source note helps preserve that information when a passage moves into a handout, translation, or print volume. The mere length of a quotation does not establish its legal status.

## F.4.4 Permission, access, and project authority

**Status: Decided.** This section explains existing boundaries in CC0, the project's history-access record, and its contribution and guide-review conventions.

CC0 concerns the rights the dedicator can give. It leaves patent and trademark rights unaffected, and it cannot waive another person's rights, including applicable privacy or publicity rights. It offers the work without warranties, to the extent permitted by law. A CC0 declaration therefore does not establish that all content is accurate or that every included item has been cleared for every use. Reuse also does not establish endorsement by Open Worlds or by an author.

The project's history provides a useful access example. `docs/HISTORY.md` publicly identifies earlier records, versions, and hashes. It also explains that the underlying stage files are held privately because they contain outside texts and translations whose terms require attention, together with personal material. Access is provided through the founder's authorization or supplied copies. Public metadata identifies those files without publishing their contents or granting a new permission to republish everything inside them.

Keep the particular object in view. A separately dedicated public book can be reused according to its own record. That does not make the whole conversation collection containing its development public. Equally, the fact that a historical collection is private does not undo a dedication already made for a separately identified work.

Project participation has its own scope. The contribution process specifies attribution of changes, truthful accounts of checks, and technical review before a merge. The guide also requires the founder's review of chapters. Those rules govern what becomes part of this project. An independent reader's use of CC0 material does not have to pass through that process, and an independent adaptation does not acquire a project's review simply by citing it.

F.3, Checking this guide, explains how to identify the source and compare a copy. File identity, review, and permission answer different questions. Matching a checksum supports a claim about bytes; a rights notice supports a claim about the stated terms. Neither alone establishes the other.

## F.4.5 Keep the source and changes clear

**Status: Designed.** This section proposes an optional editorial practice for copies and adaptations. It adds no attribution, share-alike, submission, or approval condition to CC0. Separate material's actual notice requirements still apply.

A useful copy can tell its next reader where it came from, which version it uses, what changed, and which material has separate terms. Those details make continued collaboration easier: another person or AI can find the starting point, inspect a correction, or compare a translation with the passage it translates.

One compact notice could contain:

> Independent adaptation of the Open Worlds Guide, chapter [identifier], from [identified edition or draft].
>
> Changes in this version: [brief description].
>
> Project material: CC0 1.0, within the scope of the project's dedication.
>
> Separately sourced material included here: [identify the items and their applicable terms, with required notices where relevant].

This is an illustrative layout. Replace its placeholders and adapt it to the actual copy. It does not determine the rights in an item, satisfy every possible outside licence by itself, or declare an adaptation an official edition. Where useful, state the chosen terms for newly added material separately.

Use the correct source identity. For an edition already adopted by the project, name that edition. For an external chapter draft, identify the draft or delivery package, and separately name the repository revision used to check its claims. A commit containing the underlying software does not necessarily contain the draft describing it.

Stable section identifiers help readers move between versions. A translator might keep `I.2.3` beside the translated section; an abridger might identify which sections were selected. A change note can say that wording was translated, an example replaced, or a technical claim updated. These suggestions support readable provenance while keeping voluntary editorial practice distinct from applicable licence requirements.

Sources: [Guide source, identifier, and independent-reading conventions](https://github.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/blob/7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415/guide/README.md#L229-L245); [Edition convention](https://github.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/blob/7ade210ace929b80ab82458536415ce83faab415/guide/README.md#L266-L278); [Creative Commons' CC0 FAQ on voluntary attribution](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/CC0_FAQ). ## F.4.6 Worked examples

**Status: Designed.** These are proposed reader practices applying the existing terms explained in F.4.1–F.4.4. They are illustrative, not additional conditions on reuse or claims that a publishing service exists.

### A copy for a reading group

A reader wants six paper copies of the arrival chapters. They select an identified draft or edition and print the relevant text, keeping any separately applicable notices with material that needs them. For the project's CC0 material, permission to copy is already given. They can distribute the copies freely or charge for them within that permission's scope.

An optional source note helps the group discuss the same passages. F.3's comparison procedure can help check that the print layout retained the intended text and status labels. Printing a chapter does not add a new technical review to it.

### A translation for new arrivals

A collaborator translates I.2, A first walk through a multiverse. The dedication allows translation of the covered material. They identify the source draft or edition, keep section references useful, and explain their translation choices where a term has no close equivalent.

They also preserve the distinctions the chapter communicates: an imagined scene still needs to read as imagined, and a planned feature still needs to read as planned. That is an editorial goal of a faithful translation. If the collaborator later offers the translation to Open Worlds, the existing contribution and chapter-review process applies to that incorporation.

### A teaching booklet combining several sources

A teacher combines a project explanation, an original exercise, and a passage from an outside author. The booklet identifies those components separately. The Open Worlds explanation follows its CC0 dedication; the new exercise has the terms its author chooses; the outside passage needs its own applicable basis for inclusion.

If that basis is unresolved, one practical option is to publish the original exercise and project explanation with a source reference, leaving out the outside passage until the question is resolved. Adding a CC0 heading to the booklet would not answer the question for that passage.

### A public book beside a history record

A reader finds the specifically dedicated *Art of Peace and the Strategist* in the repository library and also finds a history entry describing a larger conversation collection. They follow the book's own rights record for the public work and the history page's access account for the collection. The two records concern different objects, even though the project's development connects them.

The same care helps a growing library remain useful. A shared work can carry a clear account of its own origin and terms without making a promise about every work beside it.
