# The Open Worlds Guide

A comprehensive reference manual for Open Worlds: the project, and
every part of a multiverse. It is a companion to the project, written
so that a newcomer, whether a person or an AI, can find their way in a
multiverse and understand what Open Worlds is, how it works, and why.

This page is the guide's home. It holds the guide's contents and how
far each chapter has come, what is next, and how the guide is
written. It changes with every change to the guide.

- **Read it here:** each chapter, as it is written, from the contents
  below.
- **The plan:** [OUTLINE.md](OUTLINE.md): every chapter, what it
  covers, how far its subject has come, and the files it is written
  from.
- **Its full link,** for a conversation:
  `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jasonelias144-svg/open-worlds/main/guide/README.md`

  outline, the rules and this page are a first framework, and change
  as the guide finds its shape.
- **Edition:** none yet. Edition 1 is the front matter, Part I and

- **Drafted:** all eleven chapters of edition 1, by ChatGPT, from the
  repository at commit `7ade210`
  ([delivery 012](deliveries/012/README.md)). They are here word for
  word, marked `Progress: draft`.
- **Drafting first.** The founder, 2026-11-21: "This is not the time
  for editing, but for completing a first rough draft." The drafting
  phase is "large, wide, expansive, comprehensive, more is better";
  the editing phase then "cuts out everything not needed". Nothing is
  trimmed until then.
- **Its readers** (2026-11-22): the founder named them, below, in
  "Who it is for": a person or an AI, come to take part, to build, or
  to understand, now or later. F.2 is to be drafted again with the
  third purpose, and three rules are added for a reader who comes
  later.
- **Next:** the drafting goes on. The technical lead checks each
  chapter's claims about the code against the code, for facts only (a
  claim or a label the code does not bear out), and marks it `checked
  at <commit>`; the places where the project's own pages disagree,
  which ChatGPT's review found, are put right in those pages. The
  editing phase, and the first edition after it, come when the
  founder says the draft is complete.

## What it is for

The founder, 2026-11-21, word for word in `STATUS.md` ("Decided"):
"Ok, so I have figured out what I want to work on with ChatGPT: A
comprehensive guide to Open Worlds. But this would be intended as a
book companion to the Open Worlds as a project and all the elements of
a multiverse. It needs to be living documentation that can help a new
user interface with a multiverse and understand the Open Worlds
project. In order to ensure it touches all areas of code, I would like
you to provide an outline of such a comprehensive guide, which may end
up being larger than the theory of opposites. It needs to be
comprehensive and expandable. This is where we put everything, and in
a form that could be downloaded as a book, or printed as a reference
volume."

And to ChatGPT, after the first chapter: "This is a great start and it should be the main reference manual. But also, we should be able to fill [i]n the blanks with what we plan to develop, and that way it also becomes a living roadmap and a way to help preserve consistency."
So a chapter can describe what is planned as well as what is built,
under the labels that say which is which; ChatGPT proposes a short
"Next update" note for planned work (F.1.7).

And of this page: "create a page in the repo that we can update as
we continue to expand. It will be the home of this comprehensive
reference manual."

## Who it is for

The founder, 2026-11-22, word for word in `STATUS.md` ("Decided"):
"Ok, so I was thinking about the multiverse manual and I realized
that we should be aware of a variety of possible readers, with two
main types: human and AI. But we should accommodate a variety of
potential human readers: 1. User manual for multiverse
participation, 2. SDK for developing a multiverse from the code in
the repo, 3. Human reader with curiosity about how Open Worlds
functions, for scientific, philosophical, mathematical,
computational, creative or other reasons, but without any
participation. And these could be contemporary readers or those in
the future. Please make a note of this variety in the records to
guide development."

The technical lead's reading, to guide the writing. A reader is three
things at once, not one of a list:

- **Who reads:** a person, or an AI.
- **What for.** Three purposes:
  1. **To take part:** the user manual for life in a multiverse:
     arriving, finding a place, speaking, leaving, and what one is
     guaranteed. A steward of a world or an office is a participant
     who holds a responsibility.
  2. **To build:** the developer's guide, an SDK in the founder's
     word, for making a multiverse from the code in this repository:
     its formats, its code, its tests and its commands.
  3. **To understand,** without taking part: for a reader who comes
     for science, philosophy, mathematics, computation, art, or
     another reason, and wants to know what the worlds are, how they
     work, and why they are as they are.
- **When:** now, or later. A reader in fifty years may hold a printed
  edition and no repository, may never have heard of the services
  and the words of 2026, and may meet code that has long moved on.

Each purpose is open to a person or an AI alike. An AI may take part
(I.5); may build, as DeepSeek and Gemini build now from this
repository's code; or may study, as ChatGPT reads the repository to
write this guide. And since the guide is in the public
domain, an AI may learn from it without reading it as anyone's guide.

What follows, for the writing:

- **Ways through (F.2)** are by purpose, each open to a person or an
  AI. The four routes drafted on 2026-11-21 (a newcomer, a builder, a
  steward, an AI arriving) cover the first two purposes. The third is
  new: a route for the reader who will not take part, through Parts
  I, II, VI and IX, and then by interest: the plant and chemistry
  worlds for science (III.6, III.7), the poetry world for art
  (III.8), the book for mathematics and philosophy (IX.2), and a
  world's log and its kernel for computation (VI.4). The outline's
  F.2 says so; the chapter is to be drafted again.
- **The user manual** can only be as far as the worlds are. Nothing is
  live yet (`STATUS.md`, "Live"), so the route for taking part says
  what is built and what is planned, under the labels, as Part I does.
- **The developer's guide** describes the repository as it is: the
  Python core, the Rust workspace, the schemas and test vectors, and
  the commands. There is no packaged SDK: no library to install, and
  no promise that an interface stays the same between versions.
  Whether and when the project offers one is Open, and the founder's
  to decide.
- **A reader later** is served by rules 1 to 8 already: the labels,
  each chapter's sources at a named commit, numbers that never
  change, and editions with the hash of each file. Rules 9 to 11
  below are added for them.

## The rules it is written by

These hold for every chapter, from the first draft.

1. **The guide explains; it never rules.** What binds is the charter,
   a world's law, the decisions in `STATUS.md`, and the code. Where
   the guide and they differ, the guide is wrong, and is corrected.
2. **Every section says how far its subject has come,** with one of
   six labels:
   - **Built:** in the project's code, with its tests. The section
     names the files.
   - **Sketched:** code in `sketches/`, from a collaborator's
     records, not yet in the project's code.
   - **Decided:** a rule or a choice on record (the charter, or
     `STATUS.md`), whether or not code holds anyone to it yet.
   - **Designed:** a design page says how it will work; nothing is
     built.
   - **Idea:** on record, set aside, not designed.
   - **Open:** a question not yet decided, and whose it is to decide.
3. **Every chapter ends with its sources:** the files it explains,
   and the commit they were read at. When those files change, the
   chapter is read again.
4. **Numbers never change.** Parts are Roman numerals, chapters are
   numbered within their part, sections within their chapter:
   II.3.4 is part II, chapter 3, section 4. A new chapter takes the
   next number in its part; a section that is no longer true is
   marked so and keeps its number. So a reference to the guide, from
   anywhere, stays good.
5. **Each section can be read alone.** A reader may be shown one
   section and nothing else, as an AI often is. A section names what
   it builds on, with its number, rather than saying "as above".
6. **One word for one thing,** the glossary's word
   ([`docs/GLOSSARY.md`](../docs/GLOSSARY.md)). A new word is marked
   new, and goes into the glossary in the same change.
7. **Every figure has a text twin:** what it shows, in words, beside
   it, so a reader who cannot see it loses nothing.
8. **What can be made from the code is made from it.** The reference
   parts (record formats, schemas, test vectors, the files, the
   decisions) are written by script from the repository, not by hand,
   so they cannot drift from it.

Added 2026-11-22, for a reader who comes later ("Who it is for"):

9. **Every "now" has its date.** A chapter says the commit it was
   checked at; a sentence that says "today", "currently" or "not
   yet" says, or is in a section that says, when that was true.
10. **The guide does not depend on a link, or on its time.** A source
    is named by its path in the repository and the commit, so any
    copy of the repository answers it when a link has gone. A word of
    its time (a service, a tool, a kind of model) is explained where
    it is first used, and goes in the glossary with what it was.
11. **The reasons are kept with the rules.** Why something was
    decided, and by whom, is written beside what was decided, so a
    reader later can judge whether it still holds.

## How it is built

- **A chapter's file** is `guide/<part>/<nn>-<name>.md`: for example
  `guide/I/01-what-open-worlds-is.md` for I.1, and `guide/F/` and
  `guide/R/` for the front matter and the reference section. Its first
  lines are its number and title, and a line saying how far it has
  come: `Progress: draft`, or `Progress: checked at <commit>`.
- **The contents above** are written by `python3 guide/contents.py`,
  from the outline and the chapters' files; a test fails when they are
  not current. When a chapter is added to the outline, or a chapter's
  file is added or its progress changes, run it.
- **Every area of the repository is in the outline:**
  `tests/test_guide_outline.py` fails when a top-level folder or file,
  a page or tool in `docs/`, or a Rust crate is named in no chapter's
  sources.
- **Editions:** an edition is a commit, named with its date and the
  hash of each file. The book (EPUB, and PDF for print) is made from
  an edition, with the tools that built *A Mathematical Theory of
  Opposites*, so the printed volume and the living one can always be
  compared.
- **Core first** (the founder, 2026-11-21: "Let's start with the core
  functionality. Build out from there as needed."): the first
  edition is the front matter, Part I and the glossary. Each further
  part is written when its subject is built, or when a reader needs it.
